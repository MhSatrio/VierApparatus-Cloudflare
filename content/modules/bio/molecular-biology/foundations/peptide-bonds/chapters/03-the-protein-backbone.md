Every protein has a backbone, the continuous chain of atoms that runs from one end of the molecule to the other. The backbone is made of a repeating unit: a nitrogen atom, a central carbon atom called the alpha carbon, and a carbonyl carbon that carries a double-bonded oxygen. The same three-atom sequence appears again and again, one unit for each amino acid. The side chains are attached to the alpha carbons and stick out from the backbone.

The backbone has a direction. At one end is a free amino group, called the N terminus, and at the other end is a free carboxyl group, called the C terminus. Protein sequences are always written from the N terminus to the C terminus, following the order in which the chain was made. The direction matters, because the two ends of a protein are chemically different and are often treated differently by the cell.

Pauling, Corey and Branson (1951) examined the repeating backbone and found that it could adopt regular shapes stabilized by hydrogen bonds between its own atoms. Their analysis showed that the backbone's pattern is common to all proteins, which means that the same structural principles apply across the diversity of proteins.

Chothia (1992) emphasized that proteins from very different families share the same backbone geometry in their folded regions. The side chains differ, but the backbone pattern is similar, which is why folds can be recognized even when sequences are very different. The backbone is therefore a common framework across life.

The backbone's regularity has a cost and a benefit. Because the atoms are arranged in a fixed pattern, the chain can form regular structures such as helices and sheets. But the same regularity means that the backbone has limited freedom, which must be taken into account in any model of folding. Dill and MacCallum (2012) described how the problem of folding is shaped by the constraints of the backbone, as well as by the properties of the side chains.

The side chains are attached to the alpha carbons, one per unit. Each alpha carbon has four bonds: one to the nitrogen, one to the carbonyl carbon, one to a hydrogen and one to the side chain. This arrangement places the side chains at regular intervals along the backbone, and their directions follow from the geometry of the chain.

Students sometimes wonder whether the backbone is more important than the side chains. The backbone provides the framework, while the side chains provide the chemistry. Both are needed, and the protein's function depends on the combination of the two. A backbone without side chains would be a featureless chain, and side chains without a backbone would not be able to form a folded protein.

A useful picture is a string of beads in which each bead has a handle. The string is the backbone, and the beads are the repeating units. The handles are the side chains, which point in different directions and carry different properties. The string has a start and an end, which are the N and C termini.

The backbone also carries the hydrogen bonds that hold helices and sheets together. The carbonyl oxygen of one unit can accept a hydrogen bond from the nitrogen of another unit further along the chain. Because the backbone is the same in every protein, these bonds can form whatever the side chains are, which is why the regular structures appear across so many different proteins.

Students sometimes picture the backbone as a simple thread, but it is better seen as a structured scaffold with built-in attachments for hydrogen bonds. The scaffold holds the protein together, and the side chains decorate it according to the protein's needs.

To summarize, the protein backbone is a repeating chain of nitrogen, alpha carbon and carbonyl atoms, with one side chain on each alpha carbon. The backbone has a direction from the N terminus to the C terminus, and its regular geometry constrains how the protein can fold.
