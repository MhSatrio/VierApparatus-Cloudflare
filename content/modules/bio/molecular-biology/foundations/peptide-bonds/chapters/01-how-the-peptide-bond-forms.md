A peptide bond is formed when two amino acids join. One amino acid contributes its carboxyl group, which carries a carbon atom bonded to two oxygens. The other contributes its amino group, which carries a nitrogen atom with hydrogens attached. The two groups react, the nitrogen forms a new bond with the carbon, and a molecule of water is released. The bond that remains between the two amino acids is the peptide bond, and the product is a dipeptide.

The reaction removes the hydrogen from the nitrogen and the hydroxyl from the carboxyl group. Those two pieces combine to make water, which leaves the chain. Because every amino acid has both a carboxyl group and an amino group, the process can repeat at both ends of the growing chain. Each addition releases one water molecule, so a chain of a hundred amino acids is formed by joining ninety-nine bonds and releasing ninety-nine waters.

Pauling, Corey and Branson (1951) described the polypeptide chain as built from these repeating units. Their analysis showed that the bonds between the amino acids form a regular backbone, with the same arrangement repeated along the chain. The regularity of this backbone is what makes protein structures predictable.

Steitz (2008) described the ribosome, the machine that makes peptide bonds in the cell. The ribosome does not add water to the reaction; instead, it holds two amino acids in the correct position so that the bond can form, and the reaction proceeds in a controlled way.

Peptide bond formation is not spontaneous in the cell. The carboxyl group is not reactive enough to join the amino group on its own under normal conditions. The amino acids must be activated, and the cell pays an energy cost to make the reaction happen. The energy is supplied by molecules that carry the amino acids, which are charged with energy before they reach the ribosome.

Nirenberg and Matthaei (1961) showed how the sequence of bases in a messenger RNA specifies the order of amino acids that the cell joins in peptide bonds. Their experiments connected the codons of the message to the amino acids added to the chain, which made clear how the bonds are placed in the correct order.

The bond itself has a fixed structure. It links the carbon of one amino acid to the nitrogen of the next, and the arrangement of atoms around it does not change when the chain is folded. Understanding this structure is the first step to seeing why proteins have the shapes they do.

Students should note that the reaction is a condensation, meaning that two molecules join and release a smaller one. The loss of water is a standard feature of condensation reactions, and the same kind of reaction builds many other large molecules, including the polysaccharides and nucleic acids.

A useful picture is two beads on a string, joined by a clip that closes only when a small piece is removed. The clip is the peptide bond, and the small piece that is removed is the water. Each new bead joins the chain in the same way, and the chain grows one clip at a time.

The reaction is easier to follow when you draw it step by step. Start with two amino acids side by side, one with its carboxyl group pointing toward the other and one with its amino group pointing back. Remove the hydroxyl from the carboxyl group and one hydrogen from the amino group, and the two pieces combine to form water. What remains is a carbon-nitrogen link that joins the two amino acids. Students who practice drawing this sequence find that the bond, the water and the leftover groups become easy to track.

Chemists describe the bond as an amide, because it has the same kind of link found in many other molecules. Knowing this helps when reading about drugs and other compounds that contain amide bonds, since the same reaction and the same rigidity apply.

To summarize, a peptide bond forms when the carboxyl group of one amino acid reacts with the amino group of the next, releasing water. The ribosome holds the two amino acids in place, and the energy for the reaction comes from charged transfer molecules. The next chapter explains why the peptide bond is rigid and flat.
