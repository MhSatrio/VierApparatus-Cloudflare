Making a protein is one of the most expensive tasks a cell performs. Each peptide bond uses energy, and a cell that makes many proteins at once must have a steady supply of amino acids, transfer RNAs and ribosomes. The rate at which peptide bonds are formed is therefore carefully controlled, so that the cell makes the amount of protein it needs and no more. This final chapter of the module describes how that control works.

The first limit is the supply of amino acids. Each peptide bond requires an amino acid, and if one kind is in short supply, the ribosome must wait for it to arrive. Cells sense shortages and adjust protein synthesis accordingly, slowing the production of proteins that need the scarce amino acid. This keeps the chain-building machinery from stalling in the middle of a protein.

The second limit is energy. Charging a transfer RNA with its amino acid uses energy, and forming each peptide bond uses more. A cell that is low on energy reduces its protein synthesis, which frees resources for other essential processes. The link between energy and protein production is one of the reasons that growth and metabolism are closely coordinated.

Steitz (2008) described the ribosome as a machine whose speed can be adjusted. The rate of each cycle depends on the availability of the components and on the signals the ribosome receives. A ribosome that is short of a component waits, and a cell that is short of many components slows all its ribosomes.

Nirenberg and Matthaei (1961) showed that protein synthesis could be studied in cell-free systems, which allowed researchers to test how different components affect the rate. Their work made it possible to see that the amino acids, transfer RNAs and energy sources are all required for the process, and that their levels can change the rate of synthesis.

The control also operates on the level of the ribosome itself. Cells make ribosomes in large numbers when they are growing rapidly, and they reduce their number when growth slows. The balance between ribosome production and protein demand keeps the synthesis rate in line with the cell's needs.

Dill and MacCallum (2012) observed that the folding of newly made proteins also takes time, and that the cell must coordinate synthesis with folding. If proteins are made faster than they can fold, misfolding and aggregation can occur. Controlling the rate of synthesis therefore protects the cell from the problems of misfolded proteins.

Cells also respond to stress by changing their protein synthesis. When conditions are harsh, a cell may reduce the synthesis of most proteins while increasing the production of a few that help it survive. This shift lets the cell focus its limited resources on the most important tasks.

Students should appreciate that control of synthesis is a balance between speed and accuracy. A faster rate may produce more protein, but it may also increase errors and misfolding. A slower rate reduces errors but may leave the cell short of proteins it needs. The cell adjusts the balance according to its circumstances.

A helpful picture is a kitchen where the cooks must wait for ingredients, fuel and equipment. When the ingredients run low, the cooks work more slowly, and the kitchen manager decides which dishes to prepare. The cell's protein production works in a similar way, with the supply chain setting the pace.

To summarize, cells control the rate of peptide bond formation through the supply of amino acids, the availability of energy and the number of ribosomes. Synthesis can be slowed when resources are short or when the cell is under stress.
