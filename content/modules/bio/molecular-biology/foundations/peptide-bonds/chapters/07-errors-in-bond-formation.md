The ribosome forms peptide bonds with great accuracy, but the process is not perfect. A wrong amino acid can sometimes be added, or a codon can be misread. Cells have several checks that limit these errors, and they have ways to remove faulty chains when the checks fail. Understanding these safeguards shows how the cell protects the quality of its proteins.

The first safeguard is the match between codon and anticodon. The ribosome checks that the transfer RNA it brings to the A site has an anticodon that pairs with the codon of the message. A mismatch is usually rejected before the bond forms. This check depends on the same pairing rules that govern DNA and RNA, and it is one of the reasons the code is read so accurately.

The second safeguard is the choice of amino acid itself. Each transfer RNA must be loaded with the correct amino acid by an enzyme called an aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase. If the wrong amino acid is attached, the ribosome cannot tell the difference, because it checks only the transfer RNA's anticodon. Accuracy therefore depends on the loading step as much as on the ribosome.

Nirenberg and Matthaei (1961) showed how the codon determines which amino acid is added, which is the foundation of the code. Their work implies that any error in the loading or matching step would lead to a wrong amino acid in the chain, and cells must prevent this.

Steitz (2008) described the proofreading steps in which the ribosome tests the fit of the transfer RNA before the peptide bond forms. A correct fit triggers the reaction, while an incorrect one is rejected. The steps add time to each cycle, but they greatly reduce the rate of error.

When a chain is made with an error, it may still fold, but the wrong residue can disturb the shape. Dill and MacCallum (2012) discussed how the folding of a protein depends on its sequence, so a wrong amino acid can cause misfolding. Cells have chaperones and quality control systems to recognize such misfolded proteins and to refold or destroy them.

The quality control systems act at several points. Some detect misfolded chains as they emerge from the ribosome, and others detect proteins that have failed to fold after release. A protein that cannot be rescued is tagged and broken down, which prevents the damaged chain from causing harm.

Students should note that errors are rare under normal conditions. The combination of codon matching, careful loading and proofreading gives an overall error rate that is very low. Stress, such as a shortage of amino acids or damage to the ribosome, can increase the error rate, and cells respond by adjusting their protein production.

A helpful picture is a factory with several inspection stations. Parts are checked when they arrive, when they are assembled and when the product is finished. A defective item may pass one check, but it is unlikely to pass all of them, and faulty products are sent for recycling.

Some antibiotics exploit the ribosome's errors and checks. Certain drugs bind the ribosome and cause it to misread codons or to stop altogether, so that bacterial proteins are made incorrectly. Because bacterial ribosomes differ from human ones in important details, some antibiotics can target the bacteria while leaving human cells mostly unharmed.

This use of the ribosome shows that its error checks are a real target. A drug that disturbs proofreading or the fit of transfer RNAs can greatly increase the error rate, and the damage to the bacterium's proteins helps to kill it.

To summarize, the ribosome checks codon and anticodon matching, the loading enzymes choose the correct amino acid and proofreading steps reject poor matches. Misfolded or faulty chains are recognized and removed by quality control systems. The next chapter describes how the cell controls the rate of peptide bond formation.
