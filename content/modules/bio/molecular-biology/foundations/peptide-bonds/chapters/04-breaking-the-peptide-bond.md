Just as peptide bonds are formed by releasing water, they can be broken by adding water. This reverse reaction is called hydrolysis. Water attacks the carbon of the peptide bond, and the bond splits into two pieces, one with a free carboxyl group and the other with a free amino group. Without a catalyst, the reaction is extremely slow under normal conditions, because the peptide bond is stable. The cell therefore uses enzymes to speed up the breakdown when it is needed.

The enzymes that break peptide bonds are called proteases, or peptidases. Some cut a chain at random sites, while others cut only at particular amino acids or at the ends of a chain. The specificity of each protease is set by the shape of its active site, which holds the target bond in the right position for water to attack. Different proteases are therefore suited to different jobs.

Pauling, Corey and Branson (1951) described the backbone that proteases must cut through. The regular peptide units make the backbone a repeating target, and proteases can recognize particular sequences of side chains as they pass along it. The backbone's structure therefore influences where the cut is made.

Steitz (2008) pointed out that the ribosome also has a role in the life of a protein. When a chain is finished, it is released from the ribosome, and its later fate depends on whether it is folded, modified or broken down. The breakdown of misfolded or unneeded proteins is a normal part of cell life.

Breakdown is controlled because unregulated cutting would destroy the cell's proteins. Cells mark proteins for destruction with small tags, and specialized machines recognize the tags and carry the marked proteins to protease complexes that break them down. The control prevents damage to proteins that the cell still needs.

Chothia (1992) described how proteins in different families vary in their susceptibility to cutting, and that some regions are more exposed than others. Flexible loops and exposed stretches are more vulnerable to proteases, while the compact core is protected by its fold. The shape of the protein therefore determines how easily it is broken down.

Students should note that the cutting of a peptide bond is not always destructive. Some proteins are made as inactive precursors, and a protease cuts them to release an active form. Hormones, digestive enzymes and blood-clotting factors are often produced this way. Controlled cutting can therefore switch a protein on, as well as remove it.

A useful picture is a long string of beads that is cut at selected points. Cutting destroys the string where it is cut, and the cut position determines which pieces remain. Cells can cut strings to recycle their parts or to release a useful piece, and they decide where to cut using the signals and shapes described in this module.

Laboratories also use controlled cutting. Scientists add specific proteases to a protein sample to cut it at known sites, which helps them map the structure or prepare a smaller piece for study. The cutting is carefully timed and monitored, because too much enzyme would break the protein into fragments that are hard to interpret.

Digestive proteases work outside the cell, in the stomach and intestine, where they break food proteins into amino acids and small peptides. Their action shows the same chemistry in a different setting, and it is a familiar example of peptide bond hydrolysis in everyday life.

To summarize, peptide bonds are broken by hydrolysis, which adds water across the bond. Proteases speed up the reaction and choose their targets by the shape of their active sites. Cells control breakdown carefully, using tags and controlled cutting. The next chapter explains how the ribosome builds these bonds during translation.
