The ribosome is the machine that builds proteins by making peptide bonds in the order set by a messenger RNA. Its central task is to bring two amino acids into position and to make the bond between them. It does this with a set of sites, carrying transfer RNAs that hold the growing chain and the next amino acid. The reaction takes place in a cavity of the large subunit, and the ribosome repeats the cycle many times to add each amino acid in turn.

The ribosome has three sites for transfer RNA, usually called A, P and E. The P site holds the transfer RNA carrying the growing chain. The A site receives the next transfer RNA, carrying the next amino acid. The E site holds a transfer RNA that has finished its work and is about to leave. The arrangement lets the chain grow in an orderly way, with each new amino acid placed next to the one before.

Steitz (2008) described how the ribosome's architecture positions these sites. The large subunit forms a cavity where the reacting groups meet, and the small subunit reads the messenger RNA to place the transfer RNA in the correct site. The two subunits work together, and the ribosome changes shape as it moves along the message.

The bond itself is made in the peptidyl transferase center. In this site, the amino group of the new amino acid attacks the carboxyl group of the growing chain, which is attached to the transfer RNA in the P site. The ribosome holds the two groups in position so that the reaction can proceed without water being added. The chain is transferred from the P site transfer RNA to the A site amino acid.

Nirenberg and Matthaei (1961) provided the early evidence that linked the sequence of a messenger RNA to the amino acids added to a protein. Their experiments showed how each codon directs the placement of a particular amino acid, which is the information the ribosome uses when positioning the transfer RNAs.

Pauling, Corey and Branson (1951) described the peptide backbone that the ribosome builds. The backbone's regular geometry helps the ribosome place each amino acid in the right orientation for the next bond, and the same geometry allows the finished chain to fold later.

The ribosome then moves forward along the message by one codon. The transfer RNA that carried the chain moves from the P site to the E site, the empty transfer RNA leaves, and the new transfer RNA moves into the P site. The cycle repeats, adding one amino acid and one peptide bond at each turn.

Students should note that the ribosome's reaction is an example of catalysis by RNA. The key chemistry is performed by the ribosomal RNA, and the proteins of the ribosome mostly hold the RNA in place. This surprising feature was discovered after the basic process was understood, and it shows how the RNA and protein parts of the ribosome share the work.

A helpful picture is a conveyor belt with three stations. Each station holds an item that the next station needs. At the central station, the item is added to a growing line, and then the line moves forward one place. The belt keeps moving, and each cycle adds one link to the chain.

To summarize, the ribosome holds transfer RNAs in its A, P and E sites, places the next amino acid beside the growing chain and forms the peptide bond in its catalytic center. The chain moves forward one codon at a time, and the reaction is catalyzed by RNA. The next chapter describes how the genetic code tells the ribosome which amino acid to add.
