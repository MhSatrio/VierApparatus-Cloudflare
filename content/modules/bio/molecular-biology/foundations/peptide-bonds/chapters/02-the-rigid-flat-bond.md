The peptide bond is not a simple single link that spins freely. Its carbon-nitrogen bond has some double-bond character, which means that electrons are shared across the bond in a way that stops it from rotating easily. As a result, the six atoms around the peptide bond, two carbons, the nitrogen, the oxygen, the hydrogen and the carbon of the side chain, lie in one flat plane. This flat unit is one of the most important facts about protein structure.

The flatness has a practical consequence. Because the peptide bond cannot twist, the backbone of a protein can only change its shape at the atoms on either side of each flat unit. These two points of freedom are called phi and psi angles, and they determine the path of the backbone. The chain can bend and turn, but only in these controlled ways.

Pauling, Corey and Branson (1951) recognized that the flat peptide unit was essential to the regular helical structures they proposed. Their models of the helices used the flatness of the bond to fix the geometry, and they showed that a chain of such units could only fold into a limited set of arrangements.

Dill and MacCallum (2012) discussed the protein-folding problem and noted that the limited freedom of the backbone is one of the reasons the problem is tractable. The number of possible shapes a chain can take is enormous, but the flat peptide units reduce it dramatically. The folding process is therefore constrained by the chemistry of the backbone before side chains are considered.

The rigidity has consequences for the rest of the chain. Each flat unit places the side chains of its amino acids at fixed angles relative to the backbone. The spacing of the side chains along the chain therefore follows from the geometry of the peptide units, and the pattern of side chains on a helix or a sheet is set by the way the units are arranged.

Steitz (2008) noted that the ribosome must work with this rigidity during protein synthesis. Each new peptide bond must form in a precise position, and the flat geometry of the existing units guides where the next amino acid can be placed. The rigid bonds help the ribosome build the chain in the correct order.

The double-bond character is not absolute. The electrons are shared to a large extent, but not completely, and the bond can be distorted slightly by strong forces. Most of the time, however, the bond behaves as if it were rigid, and the approximation is useful for predicting protein shapes.

Students should understand that the flatness is a property of the peptide bond, not of the whole protein. The protein as a whole has many degrees of freedom because each of its residues contributes two rotatable points. The rigid bonds simply limit the choices at each step, making the overall shape more predictable.

A helpful picture is a chain of rigid plates joined by hinges. Each plate is one peptide unit, and each hinge is one of the two rotatable points at the alpha carbon. The chain can fold, but only through the hinges, and the plates keep their flat shape throughout.

The rigidity of the peptide bond has a useful experimental consequence. Because the bond cannot twist, the shape of a chain can be described with just two angles per residue, rather than with many independent rotations. Structural biologists exploit this when they build models of proteins from measurements. A model that respects the flat units is much easier to fit to the data than one that allows the bond to rotate freely.

Rigidity also means that some combinations of angles are forbidden. Atoms in the backbone and side chains would collide if the angles were chosen badly, and such positions are excluded from the possible shapes. The allowed combinations form a map that predicts which regions of a protein are likely to be helices, sheets or loops.

To summarize, the peptide bond has partial double-bond character, which makes the six atoms around it lie flat and prevents free rotation. The backbone changes shape only at the alpha carbons, and this limits the possible protein shapes. The next chapter describes the backbone that these bonds create.
