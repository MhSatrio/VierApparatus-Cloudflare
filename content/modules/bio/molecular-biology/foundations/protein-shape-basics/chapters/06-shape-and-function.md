A protein is useful because of its shape. An enzyme catalyzes a reaction because its active site has the right shape and chemistry to hold its substrate. An antibody binds its target because its surface fits the target like a key. A structural protein holds a cell together because its fold resists stretching. In each case, the function follows from the three-dimensional arrangement of the chain.

Active sites and binding surfaces are formed from residues that come together in the folded protein. Residues far apart in the sequence can meet in space, forming a pocket or a groove that no single part of the chain could make alone. The shape of the pocket determines which molecules can enter and which chemistry can take place inside it.

Koshland (1958) proposed that enzymes change their shape when a substrate binds, so that the active site fits the substrate more closely. This idea of induced fit explained why enzymes are so specific, and it showed that shape is dynamic as well as static. The enzyme's shape is part of its catalytic mechanism.

Benkovic and Hammes-Schiffer (2003) discussed how enzymes use their structures to lower the energy of reactions. The active site holds substrates in favorable positions, stabilizes the transition state and provides the chemical groups that participate in the reaction. The arrangement of residues is therefore central to catalysis.

Chothia (1992) noted that the surfaces of proteins often carry the functional sites, and that these surfaces are more variable than the core. A family may keep its fold while changing its binding surface, which allows related proteins to recognize different partners. Function can therefore vary while the basic architecture remains the same.

A change in shape can change function. A single substitution that disturbs the active site can abolish activity, while a change that alters the surface can change which partners a protein binds. Some diseases arise because a mutation causes misfolding, and the protein loses its function because its shape has changed.

Some proteins change shape deliberately to switch between states. A signaling protein may fold into an active form when it binds a small molecule and into an inactive form when the molecule is removed. The switch is a controlled change in shape, and it allows the cell to turn processes on and off.

Students should note that the link between shape and function is not always simple. A protein may have a large, flexible region that does not fold into a fixed structure, yet still plays a role. Such intrinsically disordered regions can bind partners by taking shape only when they meet their targets.

A useful picture is a lock and key, with the important refinement that the lock can slightly change its shape to hold the key more tightly. The key is the substrate or partner, and the lock is the active site or binding surface. Its fit determines whether the lock opens.

Shape also determines how a protein is recognized by other cell machinery. Enzymes that tag proteins for destruction often recognize exposed patches of a misfolded or damaged chain, and a protein whose surface has changed may be marked for removal. The cell therefore uses shape as a signal, not only as a function.

Understanding the link between shape and function helps researchers design drugs. A drug that binds an active site can block an enzyme, and the design depends on knowing the shape of the pocket. The same reasoning underlies the development of many medicines.

To summarize, protein function depends on the shape of active sites and binding surfaces, which are built from residues brought together by the fold. Shapes can change in response to binding, and changes in shape can alter or switch off function. The next chapter describes how shape is determined from sequence and environment.
