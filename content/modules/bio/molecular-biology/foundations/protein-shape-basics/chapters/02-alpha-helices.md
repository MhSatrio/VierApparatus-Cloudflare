The alpha helix is the most common regular fold in proteins. It is a coiled structure in which the backbone winds around an imaginary axis, making a turn about every 3.6 amino acids. The helix is stabilized by hydrogen bonds that run along the chain, linking the carbonyl oxygen of one residue to the nitrogen of a residue four places further along. Because these bonds repeat in a regular way, the helix is stable and easy to recognize.

Each turn of the helix is compact, and the rise along the axis is about 1.5 angstroms per residue. The side chains point outward from the helix, and they are arranged in a helical pattern, so that residues separated by three or four positions tend to lie on the same face. This arrangement matters, because it allows a helix to present a hydrophobic face to a core and a polar face to the solvent.

Pauling, Corey and Branson (1951) proposed the alpha helix as a possible structure for the polypeptide chain, based on the geometry of the peptide bond and the hydrogen bonds they expected. Their model was later confirmed by X-ray studies, and it became one of the foundations of protein structure.

Chothia (1992) noted that helices are among the most common building blocks of protein families. They appear in many different proteins, often packed together to form bundles or to span membranes. Their recurrence across families shows how a single regular fold can be reused in many contexts.

Dill and MacCallum (2012) discussed how helices form during folding. A stretch of the sequence with the right pattern of residues tends to adopt the helical shape quickly, and this local formation helps guide the chain toward its final fold. Helices can therefore be early events in folding, as well as final features of the structure.

Some residues favor helices and others disfavor them. Alanine, glutamate and leucine are often found in helices, while proline and glycine tend to break them, because proline's ring fixes the backbone and glycine's flexibility lets the chain bend. A helix often begins or ends near such residues, which act as signals for the start and stop of the fold.

Students should understand that the alpha helix is a pattern in space rather than a rigid object. The helix has a clear handedness, and it is usually right-handed in proteins. The regular repetition gives the helix its stability, but the exact length and position of each helix depend on the sequence around it.

A helpful picture is a coiled telephone cord. The cord winds around an axis, each loop is the same size, and the handle that turns it is the hydrogen bonds. The side chains stick out like small hooks, and they point in a regular pattern along the coil.

Helices can be amphipathic, meaning that they have one hydrophobic face and one polar face. Such helices are common at the surface of proteins and in membrane-associated regions. When two amphipathic helices pack together, their hydrophobic faces meet and bury themselves, while their polar faces remain exposed. The arrangement is a simple example of how a regular fold can encode a pattern of exposure.

Helices also act as spacers. A long helix can hold two parts of a protein apart at a fixed distance, or it can act as a rigid rod that links two domains. Because the helix is regular, its length can be predicted from the number of residues, which helps designers and researchers reason about the shape of a protein.

To summarize, the alpha helix is a coiled fold in which the backbone makes a turn about every 3.6 residues, held together by hydrogen bonds between residues four places apart. Its side chains point outward in a regular pattern, and certain residues favor or break it. The next chapter describes the beta sheet, the other main regular fold.
