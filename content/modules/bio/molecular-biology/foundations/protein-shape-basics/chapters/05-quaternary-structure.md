Many proteins are made of more than one chain. Each chain folds into its own tertiary structure, and then the chains assemble into a larger complex. This arrangement is called quaternary structure. The individual chains are called subunits, and the complex may contain two, four, six or many more. Quaternary structure gives proteins additional ways to work, because the subunits can cooperate, regulate one another or form a shared binding site.

Subunits are held together by the same kinds of forces that hold a single chain together. Hydrophobic patches on neighboring subunits pack together, hydrogen bonds link them at their surfaces and salt bridges hold charged residues in contact. Some assemblies also use covalent disulfide bridges. The result is a stable complex that does not fall apart in the cell.

Pearl and Prodromou (2006) described the chaperone Hsp90, which functions as a multi-subunit machine. Its subunits assemble and change shape together, and their cooperation is necessary for the chaperone to bind and release client proteins. The example shows how quaternary structure allows a complex to carry out a coordinated task.

Chothia (1992) noted that many protein families include both single-chain and multi-chain members. The subunit structure is often conserved within a family, and the contacts between subunits form a family-specific interface. This shows that quaternary structure can be as conserved as the fold of a single chain.

Assembly can also regulate a protein. Some enzymes are active only when their subunits come together, and they are switched off when the complex is separated. This allows the cell to control the activity of the protein by changing the balance between assembled and free subunits, without changing the sequence.

Dill and MacCallum (2012) pointed out that the folding of a complex can be more complicated than the folding of a single chain, because the subunits must find each other as well as fold correctly. Cells often help this process with chaperones, which guide the chains into the correct assembly and prevent them from clumping.

Students should note that quaternary structure is not always permanent. Some complexes assemble and disassemble in response to signals, and the change in quaternary structure can switch the protein between its active and inactive states. The flexibility of these interfaces is an important feature of many regulatory proteins.

A helpful picture is a set of interlocking building blocks. Each block is a chain with its own shape, and the blocks join at their edges to make a larger structure. The structure is stronger than the blocks alone, and it can be taken apart and rebuilt when needed.

Symmetry is common in quaternary structures. When identical subunits assemble, they often arrange themselves around a central axis, with each subunit related to the next by a simple rotation. Such symmetric assemblies are efficient, because only one kind of subunit must be made, and they form regular shapes such as rings, tubes and shells.

Some complexes contain different subunits that have distinct roles. A shared core may hold together several kinds of partner, and each partner contributes its own function. The division of labor allows a complex to perform steps that a single chain could not perform alone.

Assemblies also protect their subunits. A subunit that is exposed on its own may clump or be destroyed, but when it is held in a complex its hydrophobic surfaces stay hidden. The complex therefore serves as a shelter, and cells often make subunits in balanced amounts so that none is left over.

To summarize, quaternary structure is the arrangement of several protein chains into a complex. The subunits are held by the same forces as a single chain, and assembly can cooperate in function and regulate activity. The next chapter explains how protein shape relates to function.
