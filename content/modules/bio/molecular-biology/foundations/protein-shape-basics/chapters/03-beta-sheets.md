The beta sheet is the second common regular fold in proteins. Unlike the helix, which is formed by one stretch of chain winding on itself, the beta sheet is formed by several stretches lying side by side. Each stretch is called a beta strand, and it is extended rather than coiled, so the backbone runs in a zigzag pattern. Neighboring strands are held together by hydrogen bonds between their backbones, forming a broad, pleated sheet.

The zigzag gives each strand a characteristic pleat. The side chains of a strand point alternately above and below the plane of the sheet, so that a sheet has one face with side chains on one side and another face with the opposite arrangement. In a sheet with a hydrophobic core, one face is buried against other structures, while the other may be exposed to the solvent or to a membrane.

Pauling, Corey and Branson (1951) worked on the geometry of the pleated sheet as well as the helix, and their models showed how neighboring chains could form hydrogen bonds in a regular pattern. Their work established the sheet as a second basic fold, alongside the helix.

Two arrangements are common. In a parallel sheet, neighboring strands run in the same direction, from amino end to carboxyl end. In an antiparallel sheet, neighboring strands run in opposite directions. The hydrogen bonds differ between the two arrangements, and the difference affects the stability and the shape of the sheet.

Chothia (1992) noted that beta sheets are a recurring element of protein families, and that their pattern of strands and side chains is often conserved. A sheet can serve as a stable platform for other structures, and its face can be used to bind partners. The recurrence of sheets across families shows how this fold has been adapted to many roles.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the folding of a chain depends on the sequence, and sheets form when the sequence gives the right pattern of residues. Strands prefer residues that are bulky and that can pack side by side, and the sequence determines which stretches will form strands and which will form turns between them.

Students should note that sheets and helices often appear together in the same protein. A common pattern is a sheet that is flanked by helices, with the helices packing against the faces of the sheet. Such combinations form the cores of many proteins, and they show how simple regular folds can build complex structures.

A useful picture is a row of people holding hands, each standing side by side with neighbors and facing the same way or alternating. The hands are the hydrogen bonds, and the pleat of the row is the zigzag of the strands. The arrangement is stable because each person is linked to the next.

Beta sheets can be twisted. Because the strands are not perfectly flat, neighboring strands often rotate slightly relative to one another, so that the whole sheet forms a gentle spiral. The twist is a regular feature of many sheets, and it helps the sheet pack against other structures without creating gaps.

Sheets can also form barrels. When a sheet curls around to close on itself, it makes a cylinder with the side chains facing inward or outward. Barrels are common in proteins that hold small molecules in their centers or that span membranes, and they show how a flat fold can be shaped into a three-dimensional container.

To summarize, the beta sheet is formed by beta strands lying side by side and linked by hydrogen bonds between their backbones. Strands can run parallel or antiparallel, and the sheet has faces with alternating side chains. The next chapter describes the tertiary structure, the complete fold of a single chain.
