The primary structure of a protein is its sequence: the order of amino acids along the chain, from the amino end to the carboxyl end. The primary structure is the simplest level of protein shape, because it is just a list. Yet it contains everything needed for the higher levels, because the sequence determines how the chain will fold. Every other level of structure follows from it.

The sequence is written with one-letter or three-letter codes for the amino acids. A protein might begin with methionine, glycine, serine and so on, and its full sequence can run to hundreds or thousands of residues. Scientists often compare sequences to see how proteins are related. Two proteins with similar sequences are likely to share a fold and often a function.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the primary structure contains the information needed for folding. In his classic experiments, a protein was unfolded and then allowed to refold on its own, and it recovered its active shape. This showed that the sequence alone was enough to specify the native structure under the right conditions.

Pauling, Corey and Branson (1951) explained how the sequence gives rise to regular local structures. Some stretches of the chain can form helices, while others form sheets, and the pattern of residues decides which structure forms. The primary structure thus sets up the local folds that build the larger shape.

Chothia (1992) emphasized that the sequence is the main evidence used to classify proteins into families. Proteins that share a common ancestor often keep similar sequences, especially in the parts that matter for the fold and the function. Comparing sequences therefore reveals history as well as chemistry.

It is worth remembering that the sequence is not a fixed blueprint that fully describes the protein's behavior. The folded shape, the environment and the presence of other molecules all add to the outcome. The sequence is the starting point, and it is the easiest level to read, but it is not the whole story.

A useful way to think about primary structure is as the spelling of a word. The spelling does not describe the object, but it determines which object it names. Changing one letter can change the meaning, and the same is true of changing one amino acid in a protein.

The sequence also tells us where a protein is likely to be used. Signal sequences near the start of a chain can direct the protein to a membrane or to a compartment of the cell, and these short stretches are read from the primary structure alone. Some sequences mark a protein for destruction, while others mark it for modification. In each case, the information is written in the linear order of amino acids, long before the chain has folded.

Researchers use sequences in many practical ways. A new protein discovered in an organism can be compared with known sequences to suggest its likely job. Mutations in a sequence can be tested for their effect, and the results connect a single change in the chain to a change in health. The primary structure is therefore the entry point for much of protein science.

Mutations in the sequence can also be read as clues about structure. A substitution that is harmless at one position but harmful at another reveals which residues are essential. Comparing many such changes across a family helps scientists map the parts of a protein that must be kept intact.

To summarize, primary structure is the linear sequence of amino acids in a protein. It contains the information for folding, it is used to compare proteins and it is the foundation for all higher levels of shape.
