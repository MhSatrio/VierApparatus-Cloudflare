What makes a chain fold into one shape rather than another? The answer lies in the sequence and in the conditions around the chain. The sequence sets the pattern of hydrophobic, polar and charged residues, and the environment sets the strength of the forces that act on them. Together they decide the final shape, and understanding both is essential for predicting how a protein will behave.

The main forces are the hydrophobic effect, hydrogen bonds, salt bridges and disulfide bridges. The hydrophobic effect drives most of the folding, by burying nonpolar residues away from water. Hydrogen bonds stabilize the helices and sheets, and salt bridges and disulfides add specific contacts that fix the fold in place. The balance among these forces is delicate, and small changes can shift a protein to another form.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the sequence contains the information for the folded shape under the right conditions. His experiments demonstrated that a protein could refold correctly once the denaturing agents were removed, which means that the environment must be suitable for the sequence to find its native form.

Environmental conditions matter a great deal. Temperature affects the balance of the forces, and high temperatures can unfold a protein. The pH affects the charges of residues such as histidine, aspartate and glutamate, which changes the salt bridges and the overall stability. The concentration of salt and the presence of other molecules also influence folding, because they change the strength of electrostatic interactions.

Dill and MacCallum (2012) discussed the problem of predicting folds from sequence. The number of possible shapes is very large, and the chain must find the correct one quickly. The folding landscape is shaped by the forces described above, and the protein follows a path that leads toward low-energy states, although the path is complex.

Cells help proteins fold. Chaperones are proteins that bind unfolded or partly folded chains and guide them toward the correct shape, preventing them from clumping with one another. Pearl and Prodromou (2006) described how the chaperone Hsp90 helps client proteins reach a functional form. Such helpers are particularly important for large or complex proteins, which can misfold if left alone.

Misfolding can cause disease. A protein that folds incorrectly may expose hydrophobic surfaces, causing it to clump with others, or it may fail to reach its functional shape. Cells have quality control systems to detect misfolded proteins and to refold or destroy them, which protects the cell from their harm.

Students should understand that folding is a process as well as a state. The chain moves through intermediate shapes on its way to the final fold, and the intermediates can be stable or unstable. The route the chain takes depends on the sequence and on the conditions, and the final shape is reached when the chain settles into its lowest-energy form.

A helpful picture is a set of magnets and springs that settle into a stable arrangement. The magnets are the attractive forces, the springs are the bonds and the arrangement is the fold. Changing the strength of the magnets or the temperature of the room changes the arrangement the system reaches.

Some proteins are only partly folded in their normal state. Such proteins may fold fully only when they bind a partner, and their flexible regions take shape at the moment of contact. This flexibility is useful for signaling, because the protein can respond to many partners with different shapes.

The folding environment inside a cell is crowded. Many molecules fill the space, which can favor folding by reducing the room available for unfolded chains. At the same time, crowding can encourage clumping, which is one reason that chaperones are important in the busy interior of a cell.

To summarize, protein shape is determined by the sequence and by forces such as the hydrophobic effect, hydrogen bonds and salt bridges, all acting under specific conditions. Temperature, pH and salt influence folding, and chaperones help chains reach their correct shape. The next chapter describes how protein shapes are studied in the laboratory.
