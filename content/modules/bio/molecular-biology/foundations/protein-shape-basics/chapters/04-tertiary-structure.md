The tertiary structure of a protein is its complete three-dimensional fold. Helices and sheets are the secondary structures, the regular local folds. Tertiary structure describes how all of these pieces are arranged in space, how the chain turns and packs and how distant parts of the sequence come together. It is the shape that a single chain takes in its final, functional form.

The main force behind tertiary structure is the hydrophobic effect. Hydrophobic side chains are buried in the core, away from water, and the chain folds so that these side chains pack together. Polar and charged residues are placed on the surface, where they can interact with the solvent. The balance of these forces forms a compact globule, with a core of hydrophobic residues surrounded by a polar shell.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that tertiary structure is determined by the sequence, because a chain refolds to its original fold after unfolding. This result established that the fold is not imposed by an external template but is the stable outcome of the chain's own interactions.

Dill and MacCallum (2012) discussed the difficulty of predicting tertiary structure from sequence. The number of possible folds is enormous, and the chain must find the right one among them. The problem is hard because many interactions are weak and compete with one another, but the hydrophobic effect provides a strong guiding force.

Disulfide bridges can also stabilize tertiary structure. When two cysteine residues come together in the folded chain, they can form a covalent bond that locks the fold in place. Such bridges are common in proteins that are secreted from cells, where the surrounding fluid does not reduce them.

Charged residues contribute as well. Salt bridges between residues that are far apart in the sequence can hold parts of the chain together, and they add to the stability of the fold. Their effect depends on the surrounding conditions, such as salt concentration and pH.

Chothia (1992) observed that families of proteins share tertiary structures even when their sequences differ widely. The same fold may be built from different sequences, as long as the pattern of hydrophobic and polar residues produces the same packing. This is why a fold can be recognized in proteins that are otherwise unrelated.

Students should appreciate that tertiary structure is usually compact. Proteins pack their chains tightly, so that most of the interior is filled with side chains and there are few holes. The compactness is part of what makes proteins stable and able to form specific binding sites on their surfaces.

A helpful picture is a crumpled piece of paper that has been shaped into a ball. The creases are the secondary structures, and the ball is the tertiary fold. The inner layers are packed tightly, and the outer surface faces the environment.

Tertiary structures often contain distinct regions called domains. A domain is a compact part of the chain that folds independently and often performs its own task. A large protein may contain several domains joined by short linkers, and each can bind a different partner. The domain structure makes proteins modular, so that evolution can combine familiar parts into new arrangements.

Scientists classify tertiary structures into groups that share a general plan, such as the all-helix or the mixed alpha and beta classes. These classes make it easier to compare proteins and to see which folds are common. The grouping is a useful tool for organizing the large number of known structures.

To summarize, tertiary structure is the complete three-dimensional fold of a single chain, driven mainly by hydrophobic packing and stabilized by disulfide bridges and charges. It is determined by the sequence, and families of proteins often share a fold despite different sequences.
