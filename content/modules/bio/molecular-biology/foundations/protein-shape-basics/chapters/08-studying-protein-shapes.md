How do we know the shape of a protein? The answer has changed a great deal since the first models. Pauling and his colleagues proposed the helix and the sheet in 1951 using geometric reasoning and the known bond lengths, and their models were later checked by experiment. Modern methods can determine the positions of thousands of atoms, and computers can predict shapes from sequence. This chapter describes the main ways that protein structure is studied.

The first structural models were built from reasoning. Pauling, Corey and Branson (1951) used the geometry of the peptide bond and the hydrogen bonds they expected to find, and they tested their models against the spacings measured by X-ray diffraction. The approach showed how theory and experiment could be combined.

X-ray crystallography is the most powerful method for determining structure in detail. A protein is crystallized, a beam of X-rays is passed through the crystal and the scattered pattern is recorded. Mathematical analysis of the pattern produces a map of electron density, into which the atoms of the protein are fitted. The method has revealed the structures of very many proteins.

Other methods complement crystallography. Nuclear magnetic resonance uses magnetic fields to measure distances between atoms in solution, which is useful for proteins that do not crystallize easily. Cryo-electron microscopy freezes samples and images them with electrons, and it can reveal large complexes that are difficult to crystallize.

Chothia (1992) pointed out that the growing number of known structures has revealed the structural families of proteins. By comparing many structures, scientists have identified recurring folds and their relationships, which helps to organize the vast diversity of proteins.

Predicting structure from sequence is another goal. Dill and MacCallum (2012) described the protein-folding problem and the progress made in predicting folds using physical principles and computation. Computational predictions are now very accurate for many proteins, but they must still be checked against experiments.

Each method has strengths and limits. Crystallography gives atomic detail but needs crystals, which some proteins do not form. NMR works in solution but is limited in the size of protein it can study. Predictions are fast but can be wrong, especially for regions that are flexible or that form only in the presence of partners.

Students should note that a protein's shape is usually reported as an average or as a model that fits the data. The true shape in a living cell may vary, and it may depend on the partners and the conditions. A structure is therefore a useful but partial picture, and it should be interpreted with care.

A useful picture is a map of a city made from several surveys. Each survey shows some streets clearly and others poorly, and combining them gives the most complete map. Protein structure is built in the same way, from several methods working together.

Students who look at a protein structure for the first time should check its quality before drawing conclusions. Reports include measures of resolution and fit, which show how precisely the atoms are placed. A detailed structure with good measures can be trusted for fine questions, while a coarse one is better for broad patterns.

Structures are archived in public databases, where anyone can download them and compare them with others. This openness has made structural biology a shared effort, and it allows researchers to test ideas against a large collection of data.

To summarize, protein shapes were first proposed from geometric reasoning and are now determined by crystallography, NMR and electron microscopy, with computational prediction growing fast. Each method has strengths and limits, and structures must be interpreted with care. This completes the module on protein shape, and the next module examines how enzymes act as catalysts.
