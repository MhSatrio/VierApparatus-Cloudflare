RNA and DNA are built from the same basic kind of unit, the nucleotide. Each has a sugar, a phosphate and a base, and both are linked into long chains with a sugar-phosphate backbone. A reader who has learned about DNA will recognize most of the parts of RNA immediately. Yet RNA behaves very differently in the cell. It is usually short-lived, it is often single-stranded, and it can fold into an enormous range of shapes. These differences make RNA a flexible tool, and they explain why many scientists consider RNA to be one of the most important molecules in the origin and running of life.

The first difference is the sugar. DNA contains deoxyribose, which lacks an oxygen atom on the 2' carbon. RNA contains ribose, which has an oxygen atom, in the form of a hydroxyl group, on that carbon. This small change may look trivial on paper, but it has large consequences. The extra hydroxyl group makes the RNA backbone more reactive, and it also forces the sugar into a slightly different shape. The result is that RNA strands are less stable in the presence of alkaline conditions and are more easily broken down by enzymes that cut RNA.

The second difference is the base. DNA uses thymine, while RNA uses uracil. Both are pyrimidines, and uracil pairs with adenine in the same way that thymine does. The practical difference is that thymine carries a methyl group, a small extra piece of carbon and hydrogen, which uracil lacks. Cells maintain the balance between the two bases carefully, and the presence of uracil in DNA is usually a sign of damage or of a repair process that must be corrected. This is one reason why RNA uses uracil, while DNA keeps thymine as its own signature.

The third difference is the number of strands. DNA is almost always a double helix in cells, with two complementary strands. RNA is usually single-stranded. A single RNA molecule can fold back on itself and pair with parts of its own sequence, forming short double-stranded regions joined by loops and bulges. Mattick and Makunin (2006) describe how this capacity to fold has allowed RNA molecules to take on many functions beyond simply carrying information. The single strand is free to form structures that DNA, held in a fixed helix, cannot easily adopt.

The fourth difference is stability. DNA is designed to last for a long time, and cells protect it carefully. RNA is designed to be made, used and then removed. Messenger RNA molecules may last minutes or hours in a cell, while some small RNAs last only seconds. The short lifetime allows the cell to respond quickly to changing conditions. A gene that is switched off can stop producing its RNA rapidly, and the cell does not need to dismantle the DNA in order to change its behavior.

These differences combine to give RNA a very wide range of jobs. Eddy (2001) argued that a large part of the RNA world, especially the noncoding RNAs that do not code for proteins, had been overlooked for many years. Some RNAs act as catalysts, speeding up chemical reactions. Others act as scaffolds that hold proteins together, or as switches that turn genes on and off. Because RNA can fold, bind and change shape, it can do many jobs that a fixed double helix cannot.

The scale of this RNA activity is surprising. Large-scale studies have found that much more of the human genome is transcribed into RNA than was once thought, including many transcripts that do not encode proteins. The ENCODE Project Consortium (2012) reported that a large fraction of the genome is active in producing RNA or in binding regulatory proteins, which pointed to an unexpectedly dense network of noncoding activity. These findings do not mean that every transcript has a clear job, but they show that the RNA world is much larger than the messages that carry protein instructions.

It is useful to keep a simple picture in mind. DNA is the library, a stable archive that is carefully protected and rarely changed. RNA is more like the set of working notes, copied from the library, used for a purpose, and then discarded. Some of the notes are messages for the factory, some are instructions for assembling the factory, and some are reminders that control how the factory runs. The library stays in place while the notes come and go.

Students sometimes assume that RNA is simply a weaker version of DNA, but that view misses the point. RNA is more reactive and less stable, and those are features the cell uses on purpose. A molecule designed to be short-lived can respond quickly, and a molecule that folds into many shapes can do many things. The two molecules complement each other, with DNA providing a reliable record and RNA providing flexible action.

A further point helps explain why RNA is so central to the origin of life. RNA can both store information, like DNA, and perform chemistry, like a protein. This dual ability led many researchers to propose an "RNA world", an early stage of life in which RNA molecules carried out both the copying and the catalysis that cells now divide between DNA and proteins. The idea is still debated, but it is supported by the fact that some of the most essential reactions in modern cells, such as the making of peptide bonds in the ribosome, are carried out by RNA. An RNA molecule can therefore be thought of as a bridge between the simple chemistry of the earliest life and the complex machinery of today's cells.

Students who first meet RNA often find it surprising that a molecule so similar to DNA can do so much. The explanation is that the differences described in this chapter are small on paper but large in consequence. A slight change in the sugar makes the chain more reactive. A change in the number of strands lets the chain fold. A change in the lifetime makes the molecule suitable for fast responses. Together these changes turn a copying template into a flexible working molecule.

To summarize the differences, RNA contains ribose rather than deoxyribose, uses uracil rather than thymine, is usually single-stranded and is short-lived. Each difference contributes to the wide range of tasks that RNA performs in the cell.
