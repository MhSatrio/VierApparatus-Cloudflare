Messenger RNA, usually shortened to mRNA, is the molecule that carries a gene's instructions out of the place where the DNA is stored and into the place where proteins are made. The name describes its job exactly: it is a messenger, copying a message written in DNA and delivering it to the ribosome. Without messenger RNA, the information in a gene would remain locked in the nucleus, and no protein could be assembled from it.

Messenger RNA is made by the process of transcription. An enzyme called RNA polymerase reads one strand of a gene and builds a complementary RNA strand, adding one ribonucleotide at a time. The RNA has the same sequence as the coding strand of the gene, with uracil in place of thymine. In eukaryotic cells, which are cells with a nucleus, this newly made RNA is processed before it leaves the nucleus. The processing includes adding a protective cap to one end, adding a long tail of adenine bases to the other end, and cutting out segments called introns that are not part of the final message.

A mature messenger RNA therefore has several distinct parts. At the start is a region called the "5' untranslated region", which is not directly translated into protein but helps position the ribosome. Next comes the "coding region", which is read in groups of three bases called codons. Each codon specifies a particular amino acid, or in some cases tells the ribosome to stop. At the end is the "3' untranslated region", which often contains signals that control how long the message survives and how efficiently it is translated. A poly-A tail sits at the very end, and it helps protect the message from breakdown.

The cap at the 5' end is a modified nucleotide that is attached backward, in an unusual way, to the first nucleotide of the RNA. The cap helps the ribosome recognize the message and protects the RNA from certain enzymes. The poly-A tail has a similar protective role. Together these features allow the messenger RNA to be exported from the nucleus and to be translated efficiently in the cytoplasm.

Eddy (2001) discussed how the traditional view of RNA as a simple messenger has changed over time. Scientists once thought that only a small fraction of the genome was transcribed into messenger RNA, and that the remainder was inactive. More recent work has shown that many more regions are transcribed, and that not all transcripts code for proteins. Messenger RNA remains the best understood of these classes, but it is now seen as one part of a larger family of RNA molecules.

Mattick and Makunin (2006) argued that noncoding RNAs are as important as protein-coding messages in many biological processes. This view has changed how scientists think about the messenger itself. Some messenger RNAs contain regions that control their own translation, and some are regulated by small RNAs that bind to them. The message is therefore not simply a passive copy of a gene. It can be modified, delayed or destroyed in response to the needs of the cell.

The lifetime of messenger RNA is an important part of its job. A message that lasts for hours can produce many copies of a protein, while a message that is quickly destroyed produces only a few. The cell can adjust the level of a protein by changing either the rate at which the message is made or the rate at which it is removed. This second control is often overlooked by beginners, but it is just as important as transcription in setting the amount of each protein.

The ENCODE Project Consortium (2012) described the human genome as containing a very large number of transcribed regions, including many that produce messenger RNA. Their work suggested that the complexity of gene expression is greater than a simple one-gene, one-message picture would predict. A single gene may give rise to several different messages through alternative processing, and these messages may differ in their untranslated regions and in their stability.

One helpful way to picture a messenger RNA is as a recipe card. The card has a title, a list of ingredients and a set of instructions, and it can be copied, sent to another room and read many times before it is thrown away. The recipe itself is the coding region, the notes at the top and bottom are the untranslated regions, and the card's wear and tear corresponds to the message's lifetime in the cell. The cook, who is the ribosome, reads the card and assembles the dish.

Students often ask why the message is not simply kept for ever, since the instructions would still be useful. The answer lies in control. A cell that is making a protein for one purpose may need to stop that production quickly when conditions change. Short-lived messages allow fast responses, and they prevent the cell from producing proteins it no longer needs. The short life is therefore a feature, not a flaw.

It is also important to keep in mind that the messenger RNA is not the protein. The message contains the instructions, but the protein is built from those instructions by the ribosome and its partners. The next chapter looks at transfer RNA, the molecule that brings the right amino acid to the ribosome for each codon, and that makes the translation of the message possible.

The size of a messenger RNA also matters. A typical human messenger RNA is a few thousand bases long, but the coding region that specifies the protein may be only a fraction of that length. The rest is made up of untranslated regions and, in the nucleus, of intron sequences that have been removed by splicing before the message is exported. This means that the final messenger RNA is much shorter than the gene that produced it, even though the gene may contain many kilobases of DNA. Splicing removes the introns and joins the remaining pieces, a process that is covered in detail in a later module.

Finally, it is worth noting that a messenger RNA can be read more than once. Several ribosomes can translate the same message at the same time, following one another along the RNA like cars along a road. This is why a single messenger RNA can produce a large number of protein molecules before it is broken down. The efficiency of the message is therefore multiplied, and a small number of copies of the RNA can supply a large amount of protein.

To summarize the main points, messenger RNA is made by transcription, processed in the nucleus, exported to the cytoplasm and read by ribosomes in groups of three bases. Its cap, poly-A tail and untranslated regions help control how long it lasts and how well it is used. Its relatively short life allows the cell to adjust protein levels quickly.
