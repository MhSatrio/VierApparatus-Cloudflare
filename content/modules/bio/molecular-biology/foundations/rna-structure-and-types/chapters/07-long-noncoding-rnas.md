Some RNA molecules are longer than two hundred bases and yet do not code for any protein. Scientists call them long noncoding RNAs. They are among the least understood parts of the RNA world, because their sequences do not reveal a clear function in the way a protein-coding gene does. Over the last two decades, however, researchers have shown that many of them play real roles in controlling genes, in shaping chromosomes and in the development of tissues. This chapter introduces what they are and how scientists are studying them.

The name describes their two main features. "Long" means that they are longer than most small regulatory RNAs, often many thousands of bases. "Noncoding" means that they do not carry the instructions for a protein. In the early days of genome research, such transcripts were often dismissed as accidental products of transcription, with no purpose. That view has changed as more evidence has accumulated, although many long noncoding RNAs still have unknown functions.

Long noncoding RNAs are made by RNA polymerase II, just like messenger RNA, and they are often capped and polyadenylated. This means that they look like ordinary messages in many ways, and they can be difficult to distinguish from them without careful analysis. Some are spliced, and some are not. They may be found in the nucleus, where they are often close to the genes they affect, or in the cytoplasm, where they may interact with proteins and other RNAs.

One way that a long noncoding RNA can act is by influencing the chromatin around its gene of origin. The RNA can recruit proteins that modify histones, adding or removing chemical marks that make the local chromatin more open or more closed. In this way, the RNA can help switch a region of the genome on or off, without changing the DNA sequence itself. This action is sometimes described as acting in cis, because it affects the nearby genome.

A second way is through acting in trans, which means that the RNA can affect genes elsewhere in the genome. The RNA can serve as a scaffold, bringing together proteins that would not otherwise meet, or it can bind to a protein and change its activity. The RNA may also act as a decoy, holding a protein away from its normal target. These functions show that a long noncoding RNA can work like a flexible platform on which regulatory machines are assembled.

Mattick and Makunin (2006) describe the growing evidence that noncoding RNAs of many kinds contribute to the complexity of gene control. They point out that the number of noncoding transcripts seems to grow with the complexity of an organism, which suggests that these molecules help to build complicated regulatory networks. The idea has been influential, although it is still being tested.

The ENCODE Project Consortium (2012) found that many long noncoding RNAs are produced from regions that were previously thought to be inactive. Their data showed that these transcripts are often expressed in specific cell types, which suggests that they may have specific roles in particular tissues. The sheer number of such transcripts made clear that the noncoding part of the genome is an active place rather than an empty one.

Eddy (2001) discussed the broader idea that RNA is not limited to carrying information. The long noncoding RNAs are a major example of this point. Their structures, their interactions with proteins and their ability to recognize chromatin make them candidates for many kinds of regulatory work. Much remains to be discovered about how they recognize their targets.

Identifying the function of a long noncoding RNA is hard for several reasons. Their sequences may be poorly conserved between species, so comparing them is not straightforward. They may act at very low levels, which makes their effects difficult to measure. A single RNA may have several functions in different places or at different times. And experiments that remove the RNA can have side effects, because the act of making the deletion can change the nearby chromatin.

Scientists use a variety of methods to study these molecules. They can measure where the RNA is found in the cell, which proteins it binds, and how its removal changes the activity of nearby genes. Each method has limitations, and a convincing result usually needs several independent lines of evidence. This careful approach is typical of the field, and it is one reason why progress is sometimes slow.

Consider a simple analogy. A long noncoding RNA can be thought of as a notice board in a large office. It does not carry a message to a particular machine, but it holds notices, attracts people to meet and sets the tone of the room. Some notices are placed near the machines they affect, while others are posted far away and influence the work through people who read them. The board is not a machine, but it shapes how the machines are used.

It is important not to assume that every long noncoding RNA is functional. Some may be the by-products of transcription, produced as the polymerase passes through a region, without any further role. Scientists must test each candidate carefully before concluding that it has a purpose. Distinguishing the functional from the incidental is one of the main challenges in this area.

A practical lesson for researchers is that long noncoding RNAs are often harder to study than proteins, because their levels in a cell can be very low and their effects can be indirect. A common approach is to compare cells that produce the RNA with cells in which it has been removed, and then to see which genes change. The results must be interpreted carefully, because removing an RNA can alter the chromatin or the transcription of neighboring regions in ways that have nothing to do with the RNA's own job. Good experiments therefore test more than one approach before drawing a conclusion.

To summarize, long noncoding RNAs are transcripts longer than two hundred bases that do not encode proteins, and many of them help to control genes through effects on chromatin, through binding to proteins or through acting as scaffolds. Their functions are difficult to establish, but evidence suggests that they form an important layer of gene regulation.
