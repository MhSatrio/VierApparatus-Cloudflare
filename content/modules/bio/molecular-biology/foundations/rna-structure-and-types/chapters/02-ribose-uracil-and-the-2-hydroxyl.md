The chemistry of RNA begins with its sugar. Ribose is a five-carbon sugar, very similar to the deoxyribose found in DNA. The two sugars differ by a single oxygen atom, located on the carbon at position 2'. In ribose, this carbon carries a hydroxyl group, which is an oxygen atom bonded to a hydrogen atom. In deoxyribose, the same position carries only a hydrogen atom. The name "deoxy" literally means that an oxygen has been removed. This difference is small in size, but it changes the behavior of the whole molecule.

To picture the difference, imagine two identical chairs, one with an extra arm attached. The extra arm does not change the basic design of the chair, but it does change how the chair sits and how it can be moved. In the same way, the 2' hydroxyl does not change the basic backbone of the nucleic acid, but it changes how the backbone bends, how it reacts and how it interacts with other molecules.

The most important consequence of the 2' hydroxyl is its effect on stability. In the presence of alkaline conditions, the hydroxyl group can take part in a chemical reaction that breaks the backbone of the RNA molecule. Its oxygen atom can attack the neighboring phosphate, forming a ring intermediate and cutting the chain. This makes RNA much more vulnerable to breakdown than DNA, which has no 2' hydroxyl to start the reaction. The cell uses this vulnerability to its advantage, because it allows RNA to be destroyed quickly when it is no longer needed.

Because of this reactivity, RNA does not last long in a cell. Enzymes called ribonucleases cut RNA at specific or general sites, and many RNA molecules are degraded within minutes to hours. This short life is part of how cells control gene expression. A message that is quickly removed can only deliver its instruction for a limited time, so the cell can switch off a protein by stopping the production of its message. DNA, by contrast, is built to be stable, and cells protect it from enzymes that would damage it.

The 2' hydroxyl also changes the shape of the sugar. The sugar ring can adopt slightly different puckered shapes, and in RNA the favored shape places the 2' hydroxyl in a position that makes the backbone more compact and more suitable for forming double-stranded helices of a different kind. Double-stranded RNA tends to adopt an A-form helix, which is more tightly wound and has a deeper major groove than the B-form helix common in DNA. This difference helps explain why RNA double helices behave differently from DNA double helices in the cell.

The structural features of RNA are relevant to its many roles. Mattick and Makunin (2006) describe how RNA molecules fold into shapes that help them bind proteins, recognize other nucleic acids and act as catalysts. The A-form tendency, combined with the flexibility of the single strand, gives RNA a wide range of possible shapes. Some of these shapes are very specific, and they allow RNA to do jobs that DNA cannot easily perform in the same place.

Now turn to the base. RNA uses uracil in place of thymine. Uracil is a pyrimidine, like thymine, and it pairs with adenine through two hydrogen bonds. The two bases differ in the presence of a methyl group, a small carbon atom with three hydrogen atoms, on the ring of thymine. Uracil lacks this methyl group. For the purposes of pairing, the two bases behave almost identically, so an RNA strand can carry the same information as a DNA strand, with uracil replacing thymine at every position.

Why would a cell use uracil in one molecule and thymine in the other? The answer is partly about repair. Cytosine can spontaneously change into uracil through a chemical reaction called deamination. If DNA contained uracil, a repair enzyme would have difficulty telling a damaged cytosine from a true uracil. Because DNA uses thymine, any uracil found in DNA can be recognized as a sign of damage and removed. RNA is short-lived and less protected, so its uracil does not carry the same risk, and the cell has no need to remove it.

The ENCODE Project Consortium (2012) reported a very large amount of RNA activity across the human genome. Their work showed that a great many regions are transcribed, and many of the resulting RNAs are short and unstable. This pattern fits the chemistry of the 2' hydroxyl, because it allows the cell to produce large numbers of short-lived transcripts that can be made and removed quickly, responding to the needs of the moment.

The 2' hydroxyl also makes RNA a useful target for the cell's own chemical modifications. Many RNA bases and sugars are changed after the molecule is made. One common change is the addition of a methyl group to the 2' oxygen, which blocks the hydroxyl from taking part in breakdown and makes the RNA more stable. Cells use such modifications to protect some RNAs, while leaving others free to be broken down quickly. The 2' hydroxyl is therefore both a weakness that can be exploited and a handle that the cell can use to fine tune the lifetime of each RNA.

Eddy (2001) noted that the chemical flexibility of RNA, including its reactive sugar, is part of why RNA could have been an early catalyst in evolution. A molecule that can change shape and take part in chemistry is a natural candidate for the first steps of a living system. Modern cells still rely on this chemistry, from the cutting and splicing of RNA to the reactions of the ribosome, which shows how much of the RNA world survives in present-day biology.

It may help to summarize the ideas in a short list. The 2' hydroxyl makes RNA reactive and less stable. The same hydroxyl shapes the sugar and encourages an A-form helix. Uracil replaces thymine and avoids a repair problem that DNA must avoid. Together these features make RNA a flexible, short-lived, shape-forming molecule, well suited to the many tasks that cells ask it to perform.

A common misconception is that the 2' hydroxyl makes RNA weaker in every sense. That is not quite right. The hydroxyl makes RNA less stable in alkaline solutions, but it also helps RNA form the kinds of structures it needs. In living cells, RNA is protected by many proteins and by the regulated pace of its synthesis and breakdown. The weakness of the chemistry is turned into a strength by the cell's management of RNA.

The next chapter introduces messenger RNA, the form of RNA that carries information from genes to the protein-making machinery. Messenger RNA uses the chemistry described here, and its short life is part of its job.
