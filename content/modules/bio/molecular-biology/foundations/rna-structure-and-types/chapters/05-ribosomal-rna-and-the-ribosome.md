The ribosome is one of the largest and most complex machines in the cell. It reads messenger RNA and links amino acids together to make a protein. Despite its size, the ribosome is built mostly from ribosomal RNA, usually shortened to rRNA, together with many proteins. Ribosomal RNA forms the framework of the machine, shapes its active site and carries out the central chemistry of protein synthesis. This chapter explains the structure of the ribosome and the role its RNA plays.

Every ribosome has two subunits, a large one and a small one. In bacteria, these are called the 50S and 30S subunits, using a measure of how fast they sediment in a centrifuge. In eukaryotes, the subunits are the 60S and 40S. The numbers are not simple sizes, but they are the standard labels used by scientists. The subunits come together around the messenger RNA during translation, and they hold the transfer RNAs in position as amino acids are added to the growing protein.

The small subunit is responsible for reading the messenger RNA. Its ribosomal RNA positions the message so that codons can pair with the anticodons of transfer RNAs. The large subunit carries out the chemistry. It contains the peptidyl transferase center, the site where the bond between two amino acids is made. This center is formed largely from ribosomal RNA, and it is the reason that the ribosome is often described as a ribozyme, a catalytic RNA.

This surprising fact deserves careful attention. For many years, scientists assumed that proteins were the only catalysts in the cell, but research showed that the peptidyl transferase reaction is catalyzed by RNA. Eddy (2001) described the broader point that RNA can perform chemistry, not just carry information. The ribosome is a major example of this, and it supports the idea that RNA played an important role in the early history of life.

Ribosomal RNA is also remarkably abundant. A large fraction of the RNA in a typical cell is ribosomal RNA, because a cell needs a very large number of ribosomes to keep up with protein synthesis. This abundance is one reason why ribosomal RNA is easy to isolate, and it is one reason why the genes for ribosomal RNA are among the most highly transcribed in the genome. The cell makes ribosomal RNA continuously during periods of growth, and it adjusts the production rate to the needs of the cell.

The organization of ribosomal RNA genes differs between organisms. In many species, the genes for the main ribosomal RNAs are arranged in repeated clusters, which are transcribed together as a single long precursor. The precursor is then cut into the separate ribosomal RNAs. This cutting and folding of the precursor is an example of RNA processing, a theme that recurs throughout the study of RNA. The ribosomal RNAs are then assembled with ribosomal proteins to form the subunits.

Mattick and Makunin (2006) describe ribosomal RNA as one of the most extensively studied classes of noncoding RNA. Their discussion emphasizes that ribosomal RNA does not carry information for a protein. Instead, its value lies in its shape and in the chemistry it makes possible. This is a useful reminder that many RNA molecules work through their structure rather than their sequence in the way a messenger does.

The ENCODE Project Consortium (2012) reported that the regions of the genome that produce ribosomal RNA are among the most active. Their work confirmed that the ribosomal RNA genes are transcribed at very high levels, consistent with the enormous demand for ribosomes in dividing and growing cells. The pattern illustrates how the cell matches its investment in RNA to its demand for protein.

A helpful way to imagine the ribosome is as a workshop. The small subunit is the reading desk where the instructions are checked, and the large subunit is the assembly bench where the parts are joined. The ribosomal RNA is the workbench itself, the frame that holds everything in place and provides the surface where the work is done. The proteins of the ribosome are like the tools and fixtures that help the workbench function.

It is worth noting the cooperation between the RNA and the protein components. The ribosomal proteins stabilize the RNA structure and help the subunits assemble, but the central catalytic reaction belongs to the RNA. This division of labor shows that a large molecular machine can combine different kinds of molecule, each doing the job it does best. Scientists still study the ribosome closely, because it is a target of many antibiotics. Drugs that bind the ribosome can block protein synthesis in bacteria, which is one reason the structure of the ribosome has been studied in such detail.

Students sometimes confuse ribosomal RNA with messenger RNA. The two are very different. Messenger RNA carries a changing message, while ribosomal RNA stays in the ribosome and provides a constant structure. A messenger RNA is consumed as it is read, while ribosomal RNA is reused over and over. Keeping these roles clear helps when reading about protein synthesis, which involves all three major types of RNA working together.

The ribosome also shows how a cell balances its resources. Making ribosomes is expensive, because each one requires a large amount of ribosomal RNA and many ribosomal proteins to be made and assembled in the correct order. A growing cell therefore coordinates the production of ribosomal RNA with the production of ribosomal proteins, so that the two arrive in the right proportions. When conditions become poor and growth slows, the cell reduces ribosome production quickly, saving resources for other needs. The ribosome is thus both a machine and an indicator of how fast the cell is growing.

Mattick and Makunin (2006) also drew attention to the way ribosomal RNA sequences have been preserved across very different organisms. This conservation reflects the central importance of the ribosome, and it has made ribosomal RNA one of the most useful molecules for studying the relationships between species.

To summarize, ribosomes are built from ribosomal RNA and proteins, with a small subunit that reads the message and a large subunit that carries out the chemistry of peptide bond formation. The catalytic reaction is performed by RNA, which is a key fact in understanding the early history of biochemistry.
