A single strand of RNA does not stay straight. Its bases are complementary to one another, so a stretch of the chain can pair with another stretch further along the same molecule. When this happens, the strand folds back on itself, forming double-stranded regions called stems and unpaired regions called loops. The result is a three-dimensional shape that can be far more complicated than the simple chain it started from. This folding is central to how RNA works, and it is one of the features that distinguishes RNA from DNA in the cell.

The simplest folded structure is a hairpin. A hairpin forms when a segment of the RNA pairs with a nearby segment of the same strand, with a short loop of unpaired bases at the tip. The paired segments form the stem. Hairpins are common in many RNAs, and they often mark important signals. For instance, some hairpins help to stop transcription at the end of a gene, and others help proteins recognize particular RNA sequences.

Folding follows the same base pairing rules that govern DNA. Adenine pairs with uracil and guanine pairs with cytosine in RNA, and an additional pair, guanine with uracil, can form in some structures. The pairs are held by hydrogen bonds and stabilized by stacking, just as in the DNA double helix. A folded RNA is therefore held together by many small interactions, each weak but together strong enough to maintain the structure.

The stability of RNA folding depends on several factors. The sequence matters, because G and C pairs are stronger than A and U pairs. The presence of positively charged ions, especially magnesium, is important because it neutralizes the negative charges along the backbone and allows the strands to come close together. The temperature also matters, because heat can unfold the structure in the same way that it melts the DNA double helix described in an earlier module.

Eddy (2001) noted that the folded shapes of noncoding RNAs can be recognized and compared even when their sequences have changed. This is because the pattern of pairs, rather than the exact sequence, is often conserved in evolution. Two RNAs with very different sequences may fold into the same shape, and they may therefore perform the same function. This principle is used by scientists who search for new RNAs in genomes, because it allows them to recognize structures that would be missed by sequence comparison alone.

Mattick and Makunin (2006) emphasized that the function of many noncoding RNAs depends on their three-dimensional structure. An RNA may act as a scaffold, a catalyst or a binding platform only when it is folded into the correct shape. This is an important reason why RNA molecules are so versatile. The folding gives them a surface and a set of pockets that can interact with other molecules in specific ways.

The ribosome described in an earlier chapter offers a striking example. The ribosomal RNA folds into a large and complex structure, with many helices and loops, and the active site for peptide bond formation is shaped by the folding. The correct folding is essential for the ribosome to work, and misfolding can prevent the ribosome from making proteins. The architecture of the ribosome is, in effect, a three-dimensional blueprint encoded in RNA.

The ENCODE Project Consortium (2012) noted that many transcribed regions of the genome produce RNAs with structural features that may be important for their function. Their analysis supports the idea that structure is an important part of the story, even when the sequence itself does not reveal a clear function. Structure can help explain why an RNA binds a particular protein or why it is stable or unstable in the cell.

Scientists study RNA structure in several ways. Chemical probing uses reagents that react differently with paired and unpaired bases, revealing which regions are folded. Computational methods predict likely structures from sequence, using the rules of pairing and the energies of stacking. Some methods use X-ray crystallography or electron microscopy to see the full three-dimensional shape. Each method has strengths and weaknesses, and combining them gives the most reliable picture.

It is useful to think of RNA folding as a kind of origami. A flat sheet of paper, which is the single strand, can be folded into many shapes by making creases at particular places. The creases are the pairs, the folds are the stems and the loops are the corners. A skilled folder can make a crane or a boat from the same paper, and the final shape determines what the object can do. In the same way, a single RNA sequence can fold into several shapes, and the shape determines the function.

Not every RNA folds into a stable structure. Some are intentionally flexible, changing shape as they interact with different partners. Others fold only when they bind a protein or a metal ion. The shape of an RNA is therefore not fixed, but responds to its surroundings, which gives the cell a way to control RNA function through conditions as well as through sequence.

The shape of an RNA also determines how it is handled by the cell's quality control systems. A correctly folded RNA is recognized as a normal molecule, while a misfolded one may be targeted for degradation. Cells therefore have a stake in folding the RNA correctly, and some proteins act as chaperones that help RNA molecules reach their proper shape, much as they help proteins do. The RNA world and the protein world are linked in this way, because both depend on folding to work.

Eddy (2001) summarized the general principle that RNA function is often encoded in its structure, and that structure can be conserved even when the sequence changes. This principle is one of the most important lessons of the module, because it explains how one RNA sequence can serve many purposes and how a new RNA can acquire a function by forming a useful shape.

To summarize this module, RNA differs from DNA in its sugar, its base uracil and its usually single-stranded form. These differences allow RNA to take on many roles. Messenger RNA carries information to the ribosome, transfer RNA adapts codons to amino acids, ribosomal RNA forms the catalytic core of the ribosome, small regulatory RNAs guide protein complexes to targets, and long noncoding RNAs influence chromatin and gene expression. All of these functions depend, in one way or another, on the folding of the RNA into a particular shape.
