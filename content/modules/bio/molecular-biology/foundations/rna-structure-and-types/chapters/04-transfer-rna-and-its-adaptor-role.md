Messenger RNA carries its message in a language of four letters, but proteins are built from twenty different amino acids. Somewhere in the cell there must be a translator that can read the four-letter codons and bring the correct amino acid to the right place. That translator is transfer RNA, usually shortened to tRNA. Transfer RNA acts as an adaptor, with one end that reads the message and another end that carries the matching amino acid. Without it, the ribosome would have no way to link codons to amino acids.

Transfer RNAs are small molecules, typically about seventy to ninety nucleotides long. Like all RNAs, they are single-stranded, but they fold back on themselves to form a precise three-dimensional shape. When the shape is drawn flat, it is often shown as a cloverleaf. The cloverleaf has several loops and stems, each with a particular role. Some stems pair with themselves, creating double-stranded regions, and the loops are unpaired regions that expose bases for recognition by enzymes and by the ribosome.

The cloverleaf has three loops that matter most for translation. One loop, called the anticodon loop, contains three bases that pair with the codon of the messenger RNA. The anticodon is therefore the part of the adaptor that reads the message. A second loop contains a sequence that helps the transfer RNA bind to the ribosome, and a third loop carries modified bases that stabilize the structure. At the opposite end of the molecule from the anticodon, the acceptor stem holds the amino acid, attached by a chemical bond at its three-base end.

The pairing between codon and anticodon is the heart of translation. A codon such as AUG, which encodes methionine, is read by a transfer RNA whose anticodon is complementary, with the correct bases in the correct direction. Because the two strands run in opposite directions, the anticodon is read backward compared with the codon. This is a direct application of the antiparallel rule that was described in the DNA module, and it shows that the same pairing logic governs many different molecules in the cell.

Mattick and Makunin (2006) discuss transfer RNA as one of the most ancient and widespread classes of noncoding RNA. Their review places it alongside other RNAs whose functions depend on their structure rather than on the sequence of a protein. Transfer RNA shows clearly how a folded RNA can perform a precise chemical role, and it is a standard example of why RNA structure matters.

The attachment of the amino acid is done by a family of enzymes called aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases. Each synthetase recognizes a particular transfer RNA and a particular amino acid, and it joins them together. This step is critical, because the ribosome does not check the amino acid that a transfer RNA carries. It only checks the codon-anticodon pairing. If a synthetase attached the wrong amino acid, the wrong protein would be built, even though the codon and anticodon matched perfectly. Accuracy therefore depends on the synthetases as much as on the ribosome.

There are more transfer RNA genes in a genome than there are amino acids, because several different transfer RNAs can carry the same amino acid. This is called redundancy, and it reflects the fact that the genetic code has more codons than amino acids. Many amino acids have several codons, and the cell may need more than one transfer RNA to read them efficiently. The organization of these genes and their modifications is an active area of research.

The ENCODE Project Consortium (2012) reported many transcribed regions in the human genome, and transfer RNA genes are among the most highly transcribed. Their scale of transcription shows how central these molecules are to the cell. Because transfer RNA is needed for every protein made, the cell maintains large amounts of it, and the production of transfer RNA is tightly linked to the growth rate of the cell.

It is useful to picture the transfer RNA as a two-handed tool. One hand grips the messenger RNA through the anticodon, and the other hand holds the amino acid. The tool is held in a fixed shape by its folded structure, so the two hands stay the correct distance apart. When the ribosome places the tool at the right codon, the amino acid is in the right position to be added to the growing protein.

A frequent question from students concerns the number of transfer RNAs needed for all the codons. Because of a pairing flexibility called wobble, a single transfer RNA can recognize more than one codon. The third base of a codon can pair in a less strict way with the anticodon, so the cell does not need a separate transfer RNA for every codon. This economy is an elegant feature of the translation system, and it is one of the reasons that the genetic code is read so efficiently.

Transfer RNA also carries chemical modifications. Many of its bases are changed after the molecule is made, and these changes affect its stability, its shape and its recognition by enzymes. Modified bases can make the anticodon more precise, and they can protect the molecule from damage. The study of these modifications has shown that transfer RNA is much more dynamic than a simple adaptor would be.

Eddy (2001) placed transfer RNA among the best examples of how a small RNA can perform precise chemistry through its shape alone. Because a transfer RNA must be read by one set of enzymes and by the ribosome at the same time, its structure has to satisfy several demands at once. The molecule must be recognized by the synthetase that loads it, bound by the factors that deliver it to the ribosome and paired correctly with the codon. The cloverleaf and its three-dimensional fold satisfy all of these demands, and this is one of the reasons transfer RNAs have been conserved so strongly across life.

The transfer RNA also has an important role in the reading of messages that are not perfectly regular. Some codons are rare, and the transfer RNAs that read them may be present in small amounts. A cell can therefore influence the speed at which particular proteins are made by changing the abundance or modification of the relevant transfer RNAs, which is one more way that the translation system can be tuned.

To summarize, transfer RNA is a small, folded molecule with a cloverleaf shape, an anticodon that reads the messenger RNA, and an acceptor end that carries an amino acid. It acts as the adaptor between the four-letter code of messages and the twenty amino acids of proteins. Its accuracy depends on the enzymes that attach the amino acid and on the careful pairing of the anticodon.
