Some of the most surprising RNA molecules in the cell are also the smallest. Small regulatory RNAs are typically only twenty to thirty bases long, far shorter than messenger RNA or ribosomal RNA. Despite their size, they can switch genes off, shape the development of tissues and defend the cell against viruses. Their discovery over the past few decades has changed how biologists think about gene control, because it revealed a system in which RNA itself, rather than a protein, carries out the regulation.

The two best known families of small regulatory RNAs are microRNAs and small interfering RNAs. Both are short double-stranded RNAs that are processed into a single guiding strand, which is then loaded into a protein complex. That complex searches the cell for messenger RNAs that carry a matching sequence. The matching is based on the same complementary pairing rules that govern DNA and transfer RNA, which is why the chapter on base pairing is so useful here.

Consider the mechanism step by step. A small RNA is made from a longer precursor, which folds into a hairpin shape. Enzymes cut the hairpin to release a short duplex. One strand of the duplex, the guide, is retained by a protein called Argonaute, which forms part of a larger complex. The guide strand then directs the complex to target messages with complementary sequences. Once the complex binds its target, the message is either blocked from being translated or is degraded.

The matching does not have to be perfect for microRNAs. Often only a short region of about seven to eight bases, called the seed region, must pair exactly with the target. The rest of the pairing may be loose. This flexibility allows a single microRNA to regulate many different messages, and it helps explain why these small RNAs can affect whole networks of genes at once. Small interfering RNAs, by contrast, usually match their targets perfectly, and they often cut the message directly.

Mattick and Makunin (2006) explain why these regulatory RNAs are so important in the broader picture of noncoding RNA. They argue that the ability of small RNAs to recognize targets through pairing gives the cell a powerful and adjustable system of control. The system works in addition to transcription and to the proteins that regulate it, and it can refine gene expression with great precision.

Eddy (2001) also discussed small RNAs in the context of the wider RNA world. The discovery that RNA can regulate other RNA helped to show that many noncoding transcripts have functions beyond providing structural support or acting as messages. The point is not just that RNA exists in many forms, but that these forms can talk to one another through their sequences, forming a kind of internal communication network.

The ENCODE Project Consortium (2012) found that a large number of regions in the genome produce short RNAs, and that many of these regions are not simply accidental. Their data suggested that the cell makes an extensive set of small RNAs, some with clear regulatory roles and others of unknown function. This finding helped to establish small RNAs as a central part of the genome's activity.

A useful analogy is a set of keys and locks. The small RNA is the key, carrying a specific shape of bases. The messenger RNA is the lock, carrying a complementary shape. When the key fits the lock, the complex attached to the key can shut down the message. The cell does not need one protein for every gene. It needs a relatively small number of guiding machines that can be given many different keys.

Small regulatory RNAs have also become important tools in the laboratory. Scientists can design small interfering RNAs to target a particular message, and so reduce the amount of a particular protein in a cell. This method, called RNA interference, has been used to study the function of many genes. It is an example of how basic discoveries about RNA have been turned into practical methods for research.

The control provided by small RNAs is often subtle. A microRNA may reduce the production of a protein by a moderate amount, rather than turning a gene completely off. Such fine tuning helps cells maintain balance, respond to changes in their environment and make the transitions that occur during development. The effect is often best understood as a dimmer switch rather than an on-off switch.

Students sometimes ask why cells would bother with such a complicated system when proteins can also regulate genes. The answer is that small RNAs offer speed, economy and flexibility. They can be made quickly in response to a signal, they are easy to design in evolution because they depend on sequence pairing, and one small RNA can affect many messages. The system complements the protein-based controls described in later modules.

The regulation carried out by small RNAs is also limited in time and place. A small RNA is produced in particular cells or at particular stages of development, and its effect can be reversed when the RNA is degraded or when the target message is made in larger amounts. This makes the system self-adjusting in many cases. A message that is strongly suppressed may be made in extra copies to compensate, and the balance between the small RNA and its target can settle at a new level. Such feedback loops help cells keep their internal conditions steady.

It is also worth remembering that small RNAs can be produced from repeated or unusual sequences, and that they can sometimes come from regions that are not themselves genes. This means that small RNA production is part of a larger system of genome defense and regulation, and it helps the cell keep transposable elements and viral sequences under control.

To summarize, small regulatory RNAs such as microRNAs and small interfering RNAs are short molecules that guide protein complexes to matching messages, usually reducing translation or causing degradation. Their action depends on base pairing, and their wide reach allows them to fine tune networks of genes.
