Every DNA molecule is written in an alphabet of four letters. The letters are A, T, G and C, which stand for the four bases adenine, thymine, guanine and cytosine. Each base is attached to a sugar and a phosphate to form a nucleotide, and the nucleotides are linked into long strands. The order of the bases along a strand is the sequence, and the sequence is the information that the cell reads. This chapter introduces the four bases and their role.

The bases differ in their chemical structure. Each is a ring-shaped molecule that contains nitrogen atoms, and each has a particular arrangement of atoms on its edges. These edges are the parts that touch the bases on the opposite strand when the helix forms. Because the edges have different patterns of hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, each base can pair only with a particular partner. This is the chemical basis of the pairing rules.

Watson and Crick (1953) described the four bases as the letters that make up the message in the double helix. They proposed that the sequence of bases along one strand could be read in any order, because the backbone does not depend on which base is present. This separation of a constant backbone from a variable sequence is one of the most important ideas in molecular biology.

Franklin and Gosling (1953) provided X-ray evidence for the regular structure of DNA that makes this arrangement possible. Their images showed a repeating pattern consistent with bases stacked along a central axis. The bases themselves are too small to be seen individually in those images, but the spacing they revealed tells us that the bases are arranged at regular intervals.

The four bases are often sorted into two families. Adenine and guanine are purines, which have two rings fused together, while thymine and cytosine are pyrimidines, which have a single ring. The next chapter explains this classification and why it matters. For now, the key fact is that the four bases together give the DNA alphabet its full range of letters.

Why is a four-letter alphabet enough? The answer is that information is carried by the order of letters, not by the letters alone. A sequence of two letters can take four times four, or sixteen, forms. A sequence of three letters can take sixty-four forms, and a sequence of ten letters can take more than a million. Because genes are hundreds or thousands of bases long, the number of possible sequences is enormous. A four-letter alphabet can therefore encode a very large amount of information.

The bases are also the part of the nucleotide that changes. The sugar and the phosphate are the same in every nucleotide of DNA, and only the base differs. A nucleotide with adenine is called deoxyadenosine monophosphate, and the names of the others follow the same pattern. In laboratory work these are often shortened to dAMP, dGMP, dCMP and dTMP, or simply to the letters A, G, C and T.

Yakovchuk (2006) studied how the bases contribute to the stability of the helix. The bases differ in their stacking strength and in their hydrogen bonding, so a sequence rich in G and C is more stable than one rich in A and T. This means that the choice of bases affects the physical properties of DNA, as well as the information it carries.

A useful habit for beginners is to write out short sequences using the four letters. Try writing a sequence of ten bases, then changing one letter and asking whether the meaning of the sequence has changed. This simple exercise shows how the identity of each base can matter, and it prepares you for the chapters on pairing and copying.

To summarize, DNA is written in four bases, adenine, thymine, guanine and cytosine. Each base sits on the nucleotide and differs in its chemical edges, which determine how it pairs. The sequence of bases carries the information, and the four letters can encode a vast number of messages. The next chapter divides the bases into purines and pyrimidines.
