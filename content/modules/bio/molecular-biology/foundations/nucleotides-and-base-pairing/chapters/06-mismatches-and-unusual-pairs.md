The Watson-Crick rules describe the normal pairs, but DNA does not always follow the rules perfectly. A base may be placed opposite the wrong partner during copying, or a chemical change may alter a base so that it pairs differently. A pair that does not follow the rules is called a mismatch. Mismatches are important, because they are the starting point of many mutations and because the cell has systems to detect and remove them.

A mismatch distorts the double helix. Because the bases in a wrong pair do not form the correct hydrogen bonds, the pair is less stable, and the shape of the helix is disturbed. The distortion can be detected by repair proteins, which recognize the irregular geometry rather than the sequence itself. This is an elegant system: the cell does not need to know which base is correct in every case, only that the pair does not fit.

Watson and Crick (1953) based their model on the regular shape of the helix, and they recognized that any departure from the regular pairs would produce irregularity. Their model therefore implied that a mismatch would be distinguishable from a normal pair, and this idea underlies much of modern DNA repair research.

Franklin and Gosling (1953) provided X-ray evidence for the regular helix. Their images describe the average structure of a large number of molecules, and mismatches would appear as local disturbances that the average hides. The regularity they saw is the normal condition, and mismatches are local exceptions to that condition.

Mismatches matter for mutation. If a mismatch is not repaired before the next round of copying, one strand will carry the wrong base, and the next copy will fix that wrong base permanently. The original mismatch then becomes a mutation in the descendant molecules. The fate of a mismatch therefore depends on whether the repair systems find it in time.

Yakovchuk (2006) studied how the stability of a pair depends on its composition and neighbors. A mismatch is generally less stable than a correct pair, because it lacks the right hydrogen bonds and disturbs the stacking. The reduced stability makes mismatches more likely to be found by repair enzymes, which can detect the weakened region.

There are also unusual pairings that can occur under special conditions. Some base forms can pair in alternative ways, for example when a base is temporarily altered by a shift in its chemical state. Such pairs are rare in normal DNA, but they can occur during replication and may contribute to errors. Scientists study these forms because they help explain how mutations arise.

Some DNA sequences can form structures that do not use the normal pairing. Certain sequences, rich in alternating purines and pyrimidines, can adopt the left-handed Z form, and some short sequences can form three-stranded structures. These structures are not mismatches, but they show that the rules of pairing are one of several possible arrangements, and that the cell can use alternative structures in special circumstances.

It helps to think of a mismatch as a misplaced piece in a jigsaw puzzle. The wrong piece does not fit the pattern, and its edges clash with its neighbors. An observer who knows the pattern can see the error, even without knowing the picture on the box. Repair systems behave in a similar way, using the shape of the mismatch to find and fix it.

Students should remember that a mismatch is not the same as a mutation. A mismatch is an error in the pairing that may or may not be repaired. A mutation is a change that has been fixed in the sequence of a molecule. The distinction is important, because most mismatches are corrected before they become mutations, and this correction is one reason the mutation rate is so low.

To summarize, a mismatch occurs when two bases fail to pair according to the rules. Mismatches distort the helix and are usually recognized and repaired, but unrepaired mismatches can become mutations. Rare alternative pairings and structures show that the normal pairing rules are one of several possibilities. The next chapter describes how base pairing is used in copying and reading DNA.
