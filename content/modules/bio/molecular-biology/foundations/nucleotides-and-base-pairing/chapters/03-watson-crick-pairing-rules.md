The pairing rules of DNA are named after James Watson and Francis Crick, who proposed them in 1953. The rules state that adenine pairs with thymine, and guanine pairs with cytosine. No other pairing fits the regular structure of the double helix. These two pairs are the only ones that occur in normal DNA, and they are the foundation of how the molecule stores and copies its information.

The rules can be written in a short form: A with T, and G with C. Each pair is a purine with a pyrimidine, as the previous chapter explained. The pair A and T is held together by two hydrogen bonds, and the pair G and C is held by three. The difference in hydrogen bonds affects the stability of the pairs, which is discussed in the next chapter.

Watson and Crick (1953) reasoned that these pairings were the only ones consistent with the measurements and chemistry known at the time. Their model required the base pairs to stack regularly inside the helix, and only these pairs could do so with the correct geometry. The rules were therefore not chosen arbitrarily. They followed from the shapes of the bases and from the need for a uniform helix.

Franklin and Gosling (1953) provided the X-ray evidence that supported a regular helix of this kind. Their measurements were consistent with bases stacked at a fixed spacing and with a diameter that matches a purine paired with a pyrimidine. The combination of the X-ray pattern and the chemistry gave strong support to the pairing rules.

A consequence of the rules is that complementary strands are predictable. If you know the sequence of one strand, you can write the sequence of the other. Each A on one strand faces a T on the other, each T faces an A, each G faces a C and each C faces a G. The two strands therefore carry the same information, written in two complementary forms.

Consider a short example. Suppose one strand reads ATGCTA when written from its 5' end to its 3' end. The complementary strand, written from its own 5' end, is found by replacing each base with its partner and then reading in the opposite direction. The result is TAGCAT. Working through such examples is the best way to become confident with complementarity, and it is a skill used throughout molecular biology.

Yakovchuk (2006) analyzed how the pairing contributes to the stability of the helix. The paired bases are not only held together by hydrogen bonds, but their stacking also depends on the correct pairing. A mismatched base would disturb the stacking, which lowers the stability of the helix. The rules therefore protect the structure as well as the information.

Complementarity also makes DNA a self-checking system. If one strand is damaged, the intact partner can serve as a template for repair, because it specifies which base should be present. A mismatch created by damage or by an error in copying is detected because it does not fit the partner. This is one of the reasons that accurate copying is possible, and it is the basis of many repair pathways described in later modules.

Students sometimes confuse complementary with identical. The two strands of DNA are not the same, because each base is paired with a different base. They are complementary, which means that each is the correct partner of the other. The complementary strand is the one needed to rebuild the original, and that is why one strand can serve as the template for the other.

The rules are also useful in the laboratory. Scientists design short pieces of DNA, called primers and probes, with sequences that pair with a target region. The pairing rules determine which target will bind, and the design depends on writing out the complementary sequence correctly. Errors in writing the complement are a common source of failed experiments.

To summarize, the Watson-Crick rules pair adenine with thymine and guanine with cytosine. The rules make complementary strands predictable, provide a basis for repair and underlie laboratory methods. The next chapter explains the hydrogen bonds that hold each pair together.
