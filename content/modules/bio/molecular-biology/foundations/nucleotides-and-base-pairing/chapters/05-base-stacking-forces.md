Hydrogen bonds hold the two strands of DNA together across the helix, but they are not the whole story. The bases also stack on top of one another along the length of the helix, like a pile of coins. This stacking is a separate source of stability, and it plays a large part in keeping the double helix intact. Beginners often overlook stacking, because it does not show up in a simple diagram of base pairs.

Each base is a flat aromatic ring. When two rings sit close together, a weak attraction forms between them, arising from the way their electron clouds interact. This attraction is called stacking, and it is strongest when the rings are parallel and separated by about the thickness of a few atoms. In the DNA helix, neighboring base pairs are held at exactly this spacing, which is why the stacking is so effective.

Watson and Crick (1953) placed the base pairs at a regular spacing along the axis of the helix. Their model implied that neighboring pairs stack on one another, and this regular stacking helps to explain the uniform diameter of the helix. The X-ray evidence of Franklin and Gosling (1953) also showed a repeat spacing along the fiber axis, consistent with pairs stacked at a fixed distance.

The forces that favor stacking have several sources. Some come from the electrical attraction between the faces of the rings, and some come from the tendency of the rings to avoid contact with water. When a base is stacked, its flat face is shielded from the surrounding water, which lowers the energy of the system. Stacking is therefore favored by the hydrophobic character of the bases.

Yakovchuk (2006) measured the thermal stability of DNA and separated the contributions of stacking and pairing. The study showed that stacking makes a substantial contribution, and that the strength of stacking depends on the sequence. Some neighboring pairs stack more strongly than others, so the order of bases along a strand affects its stability in a way that goes beyond the simple count of G and C pairs.

Stacking is also important in the way DNA bends and flexes. Because the stacks are held in a regular way, the helix has a characteristic stiffness, and it can bend at particular points when proteins bind. Breaking the stack at one point, for example when a protein inserts into it, changes the local shape of the helix. This explains why some proteins can bend DNA sharply.

The interplay of stacking and pairing gives the helix its strength. Hydrogen bonds fix each pair in place across the helix, while stacking links the pairs along the helix. Because each pair is supported by both forces, the helix is more stable than either force would make it alone. Removing one force weakens the structure, and removing both would collapse it.

It may help to picture a staircase. The rungs are the base pairs, and the hydrogen bonds hold each rung in place. The stacking is like the way the steps sit one above the other, supported by the pressure of the stairs above them. A staircase needs both its rungs and its stacked steps to stand firm.

Students should be aware that the stacking forces change with conditions. Higher temperatures weaken them, and so do some chemical agents that disturb the flat rings. Salt concentration also affects the forces, by changing how strongly the charges on the backbone interact. These dependencies mean that the stability of a DNA region is a property of its environment as well as its sequence.

To summarize, the bases of DNA stack on top of one another along the helix, supported by weak attractions between their flat rings and by the avoidance of water. Stacking adds strongly to the stability of the helix, and it is affected by sequence and by conditions. The next chapter describes what happens when the pairing rules are broken.
