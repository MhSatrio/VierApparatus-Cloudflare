The most important use of base pairing is in the copying of genetic information. When a cell divides, its DNA must be duplicated so that each daughter cell receives a complete set. The two strands are separated, and each serves as a template for a new partner strand. Base pairing ensures that the new strand is complementary to the template, so that the copy carries the same sequence as the original. This chapter describes how the pairing rules guide that process.

A template strand works like a mould. Enzymes read the bases of the template one at a time, and for each base they add the correct partner to the growing new strand. An A on the template receives a T, a G receives a C, and so on. Because each new base is chosen by its pairing partner, the new strand is complementary to the template, and when the two are separated and copied again, the sequence is restored.

Watson and Crick (1953) proposed this copying mechanism in their paper, noting that the specific pairing suggested a way for genetic material to be copied. Their idea has been confirmed by many experiments, and it remains the basis of how we understand DNA replication today.

Franklin and Gosling (1953) provided X-ray evidence for the regular structure that makes templating possible. The regular spacing of the bases means that each base in the template is presented in a predictable position, which the copying enzyme can read. The uniformity of the helix is therefore useful not only for stability but also for information transfer.

Transcription uses the same pairing principle, with one difference. The template is still DNA, but the new strand is RNA, and uracil replaces thymine in the RNA. When RNA polymerase reads a gene, an A in the template receives a U in the RNA, while G and C pair as before. The RNA therefore carries the same information as the coding strand of the gene, written in the RNA alphabet.

Accurate pairing is essential for keeping information intact. Enzymes that copy DNA are highly accurate, and they check each new base against the template. A wrong base distorts the new strand, and the enzyme often removes it and tries again. Some errors escape this checking, and these become mutations, but the overall accuracy is very high.

Yakovchuk (2006) noted that the stability of pairs affects copying. Enzymes must separate the strands before they can read the template, and the stability of a region determines how easily this happens. A region rich in G and C is harder to separate, so the copying machinery may need more energy or more help to proceed through it.

Pairing also guides the reading of genes by proteins other than polymerase. Some regulatory proteins recognize sequences by fitting into the grooves and making contacts with the bases, and their recognition depends on the pattern of pairs. The pairing rules therefore influence not only copying but also the control of gene activity.

It helps to picture the template as a set of instructions on a card, and the new strand as a copy that must be written in the same order. The copyist looks at each instruction, writes its partner and moves to the next. The pairing rules are the key that tells the copyist which partner to write. Without such a key, copies would be random.

Students should note that the pairing rules are used in both directions. The original and the copy each serve as templates for the other. This symmetry is one of the strengths of the double helix, because the information is present in both strands, and loss of one strand does not necessarily mean loss of the message.

To summarize, base pairing guides the copying of DNA, with each strand serving as a template for a new partner. The same rules guide transcription, with uracil in the RNA copy. Accurate pairing keeps information intact, and the stability of pairs affects how easily the template can be read. The final chapter of this module describes how base pairing is used in laboratory methods.
