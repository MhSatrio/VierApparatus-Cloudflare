A base pair is held together by hydrogen bonds. A hydrogen bond is an attraction between a hydrogen atom that carries a small positive charge and an oxygen or nitrogen atom that carries a small negative charge. Each hydrogen bond is weak, far weaker than the covalent bonds that join atoms inside a molecule. A base pair is stable because it contains several hydrogen bonds at once, and because the bonds are arranged in a precise pattern.

The number of hydrogen bonds in each pair is the key to its strength. An A and T pair forms two hydrogen bonds, one between the adenine nitrogen and the thymine oxygen and another between the adenine amino group and the thymine nitrogen. A G and C pair forms three, using the extra group on cytosine and guanine. The third bond makes the G and C pair noticeably harder to separate than an A and T pair.

Watson and Crick (1953) described the pairs as held together by these bonds, and their model placed the hydrogen bonding at the center of the helix. The arrangement of the bonds is what makes the pairing specific. Only the correct combination of donor and acceptor atoms fits the bonding pattern, and an incorrect pair would fail to form the same bonds.

Franklin and Gosling (1953) did not measure hydrogen bonds directly, but their evidence for a regular helix supports the idea that the bases are held in a fixed position. The bonds fix the bases in the correct plane, and the regularity of the helix reflects the consistency of the bonding throughout the molecule.

Because the G and C pair has three bonds, a region of DNA rich in G and C is harder to pull apart than a region rich in A and T. This is measurable. When DNA is heated, the strands separate at a temperature that depends on the balance of the two kinds of pair. A sequence with more G and C pairs melts at a higher temperature, and scientists use this property to predict how stable a region will be.

Yakovchuk (2006) studied the thermal stability of DNA and showed that hydrogen bonding is only part of the story. The stacking of bases also contributes, and the two effects combine. Hydrogen bonds hold the pairs together across the helix, while stacking holds the pairs together along the helix. Both are needed for a stable double helix.

The strength of a hydrogen bond depends on its geometry and on the surrounding solvent. In water, hydrogen bonds between bases are partly replaced by bonds with water molecules, which weakens the pairing. This is why the helix is more stable in certain salt conditions than in pure water. The environment can therefore change the effective strength of the pairs.

A practical way to remember the difference is to think of the A and T pair as a two-point grip and the G and C pair as a three-point grip. A three-point grip holds more firmly, and it takes more force to release. The analogy is not exact, because the physics is more complex, but it captures the main point.

Students should not be misled into thinking that a G and C pair is strong in an absolute sense. Each hydrogen bond is weak, and the pair is stable only because the molecule contains many of them. A single pair can be broken by thermal motion, and the cell uses this fact when it opens DNA for copying or reading. The point is that the balance of bonds determines how easily a region opens.

To summarize, hydrogen bonds hold each base pair together. A pair has two bonds for A and T and three for G and C. The number of bonds, together with stacking and the surrounding conditions, determines how stable a region of DNA is. The next chapter describes base stacking in more detail.
