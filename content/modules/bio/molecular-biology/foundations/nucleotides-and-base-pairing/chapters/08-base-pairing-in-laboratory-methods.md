Because base pairing is so specific, it can be used as a search tool. A short piece of DNA with a known sequence will bind only to its complementary sequence, and this property is the basis of many laboratory methods. Scientists use base pairing to find genes, to detect infections, to amplify particular regions and to test for mutations. This chapter describes the main ways pairing is used in the laboratory and the care needed to design the tools.

A probe is a short single-stranded piece of DNA or RNA, labeled so that it can be detected. When the probe is added to a sample, it binds to any strand with a complementary sequence. The binding is called hybridization. If the probe glows or changes color after binding, the target has been found. Probes are used to locate genes on chromosomes, to detect viral genomes and to check for specific mutations.

Watson and Crick (1953) described the complementarity that makes probes possible. Their description of the pairing rules explains why a probe finds only its partner, and why a probe with a sequence matching the target will bind firmly. The principle is simple, but its application requires careful design.

A primer is a short piece of DNA that starts the copying of a region. In the polymerase chain reaction, two primers are designed to bind to opposite strands at the ends of the region to be copied. Each primer provides a starting point for a DNA polymerase, and repeated cycles of heating and copying produce many copies of the region. The specificity of the reaction depends on the primers pairing only with their intended targets.

The design of primers and probes requires attention to temperature. A short sequence with many G and C pairs binds more strongly, and it has a higher melting temperature than a sequence rich in A and T. Yakovchuk (2006) showed that the stability of a pair depends on its composition and neighbors, and the same reasoning applies to the design of laboratory tools. Choosing the right temperature for binding is a key step in any experiment.

Hybridization conditions are adjusted to control specificity. A high temperature or a low salt concentration makes pairing more demanding, so that only very close matches bind. A lower temperature allows more loosely matched sequences to bind. Scientists choose conditions that allow the desired target to bind while excluding unwanted partners.

Franklin and Gosling (1953) provided the structural background that makes such methods understandable. The regular helix, with its uniform spacing and predictable pairing, gives the basis for designing sequences that will fit together. Without a regular structure, the design of probes and primers would be far less reliable.

Mistakes in design are a common source of failed experiments. A primer that is written with the wrong complement will bind to the wrong place, or will not bind at all. Students should check every designed sequence carefully, writing out the complement and reading it in the correct direction. Software can help, but the principles must be understood to judge the results.

Base pairing methods have become central to modern medicine and to forensic science. Tests for infections detect viral sequences by hybridization or amplification, and identification of individuals uses short regions that vary between people. In each case, the reliability of the test depends on the accuracy of the pairing rules and on the care taken in the design and execution of the method.

It helps to think of a probe as a key cut to fit a specific lock. The key only works in its own lock, and the shape of the key must match the lock exactly. A primer is like a key that starts a machine, and the machine copies the region between two such keys. Both depend on the pairing rules to find their partners.

To summarize, base pairing is used in the laboratory through probes that bind their complementary targets and primers that start the copying of specific regions. Melting temperature and hybridization conditions control the specificity of these tools, and careful sequence design is essential. These laboratory uses show how the pairing rules of the double helix have become a practical tool for biology and medicine.
