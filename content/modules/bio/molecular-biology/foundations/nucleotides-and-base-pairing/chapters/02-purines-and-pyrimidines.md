The four bases of DNA fall into two families. Purines have a double-ring structure, made of a six-membered ring fused to a five-membered ring. Pyrimidines have a single six-membered ring. Adenine and guanine are purines, and cytosine and thymine are pyrimidines. This simple classification is more than a matter of naming. It explains the width of the double helix and why the bases pair in the way they do.

To see the difference, picture the bases as shapes on a page. A purine is a larger shape made of two linked rings, while a pyrimidine is a smaller shape made of one ring. When a purine on one strand faces a pyrimidine on the other, the combined width is roughly constant along the whole helix. If two purines faced each other, the helix would be too wide, and if two pyrimidines faced each other, it would be too narrow to hold the backbones in their regular positions.

Watson and Crick (1953) recognized this constraint as central to their model. The width of the helix that Franklin and Gosling (1953) measured in their X-ray images is consistent with pairing a purine with a pyrimidine. The regularity of the diffraction pattern supports the idea that the helix has a uniform diameter, which the pairing of two families of different sizes provides.

The rule that a purine pairs with a pyrimidine means that every base pair contains one large and one small base. Adenine, a purine, pairs with thymine, a pyrimidine. Guanine, also a purine, pairs with cytosine, a pyrimidine. Students sometimes ask whether there could be a purine that pairs with another purine, and the answer is that the geometry does not allow it in the regular double helix.

The two families also differ in their chemistry. Purines carry a nitrogen atom in an arrangement that gives them a different pattern of hydrogen bond donors and acceptors from pyrimidines. These patterns are what make adenine pair specifically with thymine and guanine pair with cytosine. The family membership therefore reflects both the size and the chemical edges of the base.

Yakovchuk (2006) considered how the ring structure contributes to stacking. The flat aromatic rings of purines and pyrimidines stack on top of one another, and purines tend to stack more strongly because they have an extra ring. This means that a region rich in purines can be more stable than one rich in pyrimidines, although the exact effect depends on the sequence.

Chemists sometimes use the terms purine and pyrimidine for the base alone, and nucleotide-level names for the base attached to sugar and phosphate. A purine nucleotide therefore has a purine base, and a pyrimidine nucleotide has a pyrimidine base. The difference is in the base, not in the backbone, which is the same for every nucleotide of DNA.

It helps to remember the mnemonic that many teachers use. Purines are the bigger bases, and pyrimidines are the smaller ones. In DNA, the larger base always partners with the smaller base. This rule is simple enough to recall in an exam and explains the regular shape of the helix.

The distinction also matters outside DNA. RNA also uses purines and pyrimidines, with uracil replacing thymine as the pyrimidine. Many important molecules in the cell are built from these rings, including the energy carrier ATP, which contains adenine. The family membership of a base therefore influences its role in many biological processes.

To summarize, purines are adenine and guanine, with two fused rings, and pyrimidines are cytosine and thymine, with one ring. Pairing always joins a purine to a pyrimidine, which keeps the helix at a uniform width. The next chapter describes the pairing rules themselves.
