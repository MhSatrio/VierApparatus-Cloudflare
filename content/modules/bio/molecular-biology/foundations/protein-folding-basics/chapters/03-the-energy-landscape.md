Folding can be described as a journey across an energy landscape. Each possible shape of the chain has an energy, and the landscape is the map of these energies. The chain moves across the landscape toward lower energies, and the native fold sits at the lowest point, the global minimum of the energy.

The landscape is rugged, with many valleys and hills. A chain can become trapped in a shallow valley, which corresponds to a misfolded shape that is stable but not the native one. The shape of the landscape therefore affects both the speed and the success of folding.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the native fold is the most stable state under native conditions, which means that it lies at the bottom of the landscape. This idea gave a foundation for the energy landscape view, which has become central to folding research.

Dill and MacCallum (2012) described how the landscape of folding can be funneled. In a funnel, many routes lead toward the native state, and the chain can reach it by several paths. A funnel makes folding faster and more reliable than a rugged landscape with one narrow route.

Pearl and Prodromou (2006) showed that chaperones can reshape the landscape for a client. By binding the client and holding it in certain conformations, the chaperone can help the chain avoid traps and reach the native state more efficiently.

Students should think of the landscape as a description of all possible shapes and their energies, not as a physical place. The folding chain explores this landscape, and its path depends on the energies it encounters.

A helpful picture is a funnel-shaped bowl with a ball rolling down its sides. Many routes lead to the bottom, and the ball settles there. Small bumps may slow the ball, but the overall slope carries it to the native state.

The landscape idea also explains why folding can be fast even though the search space is large. Most of the landscape leads downhill, so a chain that begins near the top quickly finds slopes that carry it toward the bottom. The slow steps come only when the chain must cross a ridge or leave a shallow trap.

Temperature changes the landscape. At high temperatures, the chain has enough energy to climb out of traps, but it also has enough energy to unfold the native state. At low temperatures, the chain may become stuck in traps and fold slowly. Cells maintain temperatures that allow folding to proceed efficiently for their proteins.

A landscape can also be altered by the protein's partners. When a protein binds another molecule, the energies of its shapes change, and the native state may shift. Some proteins fold only when they bind a partner, and others change their shape in response to binding. The landscape therefore is not fixed but can be reshaped by the environment of the protein.

Scientists represent landscapes with simple models. A funnel diagram plots the number of possible shapes against their energy, and the width of the funnel shows how many routes lead to the native state. Such diagrams help researchers compare proteins and predict which ones will fold quickly.

The idea of a landscape also helps explain why some proteins fold in one step while others take many. A protein with a smooth, funnel-shaped landscape has few traps, so most chains reach the bottom quickly. A protein with a rugged landscape has many traps, and its chains spend longer wandering among shapes before they settle. Neither situation is good or bad in itself. Some proteins fold slowly on purpose, because a slow fold allows the cell to check each step before the chain is complete.

To summarize this chapter, folding follows an energy landscape in which the native fold is the lowest point. The landscape may be funneled or rugged, and chaperones can reshape it to help chains avoid traps. The next chapter describes intermediate states along the folding route.
