Misfolding is linked to many diseases. In some conditions, misfolded proteins accumulate into aggregates that damage cells, while in others, a mutation causes a protein to fold incorrectly and lose its function. The consequences range from mild to severe, depending on the protein and the tissue affected.

Some neurodegenerative diseases involve aggregates of misfolded proteins that accumulate in the brain. The aggregates can disrupt the function of nerve cells and eventually lead to their death. The study of these diseases has revealed how misfolded proteins can spread and seed further misfolding.

Anfinsen (1973) provided the basic principle that explains why mutations can cause disease: a change in sequence can change the native fold or make the fold unstable. Such changes may not be visible in the chemistry of the protein but can be seen in its shape.

Dill and MacCallum (2012) observed that the link between folding and disease makes folding research important for medicine. Understanding why some sequences misfold helps scientists design treatments that stabilize proteins or help them fold correctly.

Pearl and Prodromou (2006) noted that chaperones are part of the defense against disease, and that boosting chaperone activity can sometimes reduce the harm caused by misfolded proteins. Research into chaperone-based therapies has grown from this insight.

Students should understand that folding diseases are not caused by a single mechanism. Some involve loss of function, others involve toxic aggregates, and many involve both. Each disease requires its own understanding.

A helpful picture is a machine that jams because a part is bent. The machine may still run partly, but the jam can spread and eventually stop the whole mechanism. Misfolded proteins jam the cell's machinery in a similar way.

Some diseases are caused by mutations that make a protein fold too rapidly into a stable but wrong shape, while others are caused by mutations that make the native fold too unstable. In both cases the protein fails to do its job, and the disease reflects the particular way the fold has been disturbed. Understanding the specific change is therefore essential for designing a rational treatment.

Folding research also offers therapeutic ideas. Small molecules that bind to a misfolded protein can sometimes stabilize its native shape, and drugs that enhance the cell's chaperone capacity are being tested in several conditions. These approaches show how basic knowledge of folding can be turned into practical medicine.

Diseases caused by folding failures often develop slowly. The early stages may produce no symptoms, and the damage builds over years. Early detection and understanding of the mechanism are therefore important goals, and researchers look for markers that show misfolding before it causes lasting harm.

Public understanding of folding diseases has grown as well. Because the conditions can affect memory, movement or organ function, families and patients need clear explanations of what is happening, and clinicians need a shared language to discuss the mechanisms.

The same misfolded protein can cause different symptoms in different people. The reason is not fully understood, but the speed of misfolding, the tissue in which the aggregates form and the other genes that a person carries all appear to influence the outcome. This variability makes diagnosis and treatment difficult, and it is one reason why research aims to understand the general rules of misfolding rather than only the details of a single protein.

Diet, age and environment can also affect how a folding disease progresses, although these factors are usually less direct than a mutation. A cell that is under constant stress from toxins or infection may have less spare chaperone capacity, and the margin for coping with misfolded protein becomes smaller. Such observations help explain why folding diseases are most common later in life.

To summarize this chapter, misfolding contributes to diseases through loss of function and toxic aggregates, and understanding folding helps guide treatment. The next chapter describes how folding is studied in the laboratory.
