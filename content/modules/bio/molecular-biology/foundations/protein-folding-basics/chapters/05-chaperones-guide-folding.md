Chaperones are proteins that help other proteins fold without becoming part of the final structure. They bind partly folded chains, protect hydrophobic surfaces from clumping and give the chain time to find its native shape. Chaperones are essential in the crowded cell, where many chains compete for space.

Hsp90 is one well-studied chaperone. It binds client proteins at intermediate stages of folding and uses cycles of ATP binding and release to hold and release them. The cycle gives the client several chances to reach its correct shape before it is released.

Pearl and Prodromou (2006) described how Hsp90 changes shape during its cycle and how these changes help the client fold. Their structural work revealed the mechanics of the chaperone, showing how it holds and releases its partners.

Anfinsen (1973) established that folding can occur without help in simple cases. Chaperones are particularly important for larger or more complex proteins, which have more ways to misfold and more hydrophobic surfaces that can clump.

Dill and MacCallum (2012) noted that chaperones can reshape the landscape for their clients, guiding the chain away from traps. The help of a chaperone can therefore turn a slow or unreliable fold into a fast and accurate one.

Students should see chaperones as partners in folding. They do not dictate the final shape, but they make the process more reliable and protect the cell from the harm of misfolded proteins.

A helpful picture is a set of guides on a mountain path. The guides do not climb for the hiker, but they keep the hiker from wandering into dangerous ravines and show the safe route to the summit.

Chaperones come in several families. Some form enclosed chambers in which a single chain can fold without touching other proteins. Others bind unfolded chains as they emerge from the ribosome, holding them until more of the chain has been made. Each family handles a different kind of folding challenge, and cells use several together for their proteins.

The use of chaperones is also tied to the cell's stress responses. When proteins are damaged by heat or chemicals, the cell increases the production of chaperones, which helps the damaged proteins refold and protects others from clumping. This response is one of the most conserved programs in biology, found across organisms as different as bacteria and humans.

Chaperones also work with a timing that suits their clients. Some act early, binding the chain as it emerges, while others act later on a nearly complete fold. A client that is handed from one chaperone to another may pass through several stages of help before it reaches the native shape, which is a clear example of folding as a team effort.

Chaperones use energy to do their work in many cases. Cycles of ATP binding and release allow them to open and close their grip on the client, and the cycle can be repeated until the client is folded. This energy cost is one reason that folding in the cell is an active process, not a passive one.

Not every protein needs a chaperone. Small proteins with simple folds often reach the native state without help, and the cell saves its chaperones for proteins that are large, complex or prone to clumping. The cell balances the supply of chaperones against demand, so that the proteins most at risk receive the most help. When demand rises, for example during heat stress, the chaperone supply is raised to match it.

Chaperones also have to be carefully tuned themselves. A chaperone that binds its client too tightly can hold the chain in a dead end, while one that releases too early leaves the client exposed. The balance of binding and release is therefore set carefully, and the cycles of binding and release are timed so that clients are handed on at the right moment.

To summarize this chapter, chaperones guide folding by binding intermediates, protecting them from clumping and providing repeated chances to reach the native shape. Hsp90 is a clear example. The next chapter describes misfolding and its consequences.
