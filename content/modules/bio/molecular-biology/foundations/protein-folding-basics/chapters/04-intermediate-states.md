On the way to its native fold, a chain often passes through intermediate states. These are partly folded shapes that are more stable than the unfolded chain but less stable than the native form. Intermediates can contain helices, sheets or compact clusters of residues, and they can be important steps in the folding process.

Some intermediates are on-pathway, meaning that they lead to the native state. Others are off-pathway, meaning that they are dead ends that must unfold before the chain can continue. Distinguishing the two is a central part of folding research, and it helps explain why some proteins fold quickly and others slowly.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that proteins can refold from unfolded states, but the experiments did not reveal the intermediates in detail. Later studies used more refined methods to observe intermediates, revealing the sequence of events in folding.

Dill and MacCallum (2012) discussed how intermediates fit into the energy landscape. Intermediates are local minima on the landscape, which the chain can visit on its way downhill. The presence of intermediates explains why folding can be slower than a simple downhill slide.

Pearl and Prodromou (2006) noted that chaperones can bind intermediates and prevent them from clumping. An intermediate that exposes hydrophobic surfaces is vulnerable to aggregation, and chaperones protect it while folding continues.

Students should understand that intermediates are normal and often useful. They show that folding is a sequence of steps rather than a single jump, and they provide points where the cell can intervene.

A helpful picture is a road trip with stops at towns along the way. Some towns are on the route to the destination, while others are detours. The traveler stops at the towns, and the route is not a straight line.

Intermediates can be detected in several ways. Changes in fluorescence reveal when a fluorescent residue becomes buried, and changes in the amount of hydrogen exchange reveal which parts of the chain are protected. By combining such measurements with timing, scientists can map the sequence of events during folding and identify the intermediates that appear and disappear.

Some proteins fold through a molten globule, a compact state in which the chain has taken up roughly the right overall size and hydrophobic packing but without the precise arrangement of side chains. The molten globule is an important example of an intermediate, because it shows that folding can proceed by first forming a compact core and only later refining the details.

Intermediates can also become permanent in a sense. A chain that reaches a stable intermediate may stay there if the path to the native state is blocked, and such trapped chains are a source of misfolding. Understanding which intermediates are productive and which are traps helps explain why some proteins fold reliably and others do not.

The concept of intermediates has also helped in the design of proteins. Engineers who want a protein to fold quickly may stabilize a productive intermediate, while those who want to prevent aggregation may destabilize a dangerous one. Knowledge of the folding route is therefore useful in biotechnology.

Intermediates also differ in how long they last. Some exist for only a fraction of a second, while others persist for minutes or hours before they convert to the native fold. A short-lived intermediate is hard to see and must be studied with fast methods, while a long-lived one can be isolated and examined in detail. Each kind of intermediate tells researchers something different about the route the chain takes.

For a student, the key point is that a chain does not have to pass through every possible intermediate. Many possible routes exist, and the chain tends to use the ones that are lowest in energy and least crowded by traps. This is why the intermediates seen in an experiment may differ from one protein to another, even when the final fold is the same.

To summarize this chapter, folding often passes through intermediate states, some on the pathway to the native fold and some off it. Intermediates are stable local minima on the energy landscape and are protected by chaperones. The next chapter describes how chaperones guide folding.
