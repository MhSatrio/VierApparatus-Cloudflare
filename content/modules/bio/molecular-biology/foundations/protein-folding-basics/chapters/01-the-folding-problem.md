Folding is the process by which an unfolded chain of amino acids acquires its native, functional shape. The chain starts as a random coil, with no fixed structure, and it ends as a compact, specific fold. The problem is to understand how the chain reaches its final form, and how it does so in a short time.

The problem is hard because the number of possible shapes a chain can take is enormous. If a chain tried every possible arrangement one at a time, it would take far longer than the age of the universe to find the correct one. Yet real proteins fold in fractions of a second to minutes, which shows that folding follows a guided route rather than a random search.

Anfinsen (1973) established the basic principle that the sequence contains the information for the correct fold. His experiments showed that a protein unfolded by chemical treatment could refold spontaneously when the treatment was removed, returning to its original shape. This result suggested that folding is determined by the sequence alone under the right conditions.

Dill and MacCallum (2012) reviewed the folding problem fifty years after its formulation. They described the progress made in understanding folding, while noting that predicting the fold from sequence remains difficult. Their review highlights how the search for the correct shape is constrained by physical forces.

Pearl and Prodromou (2006) pointed out that in the cell, folding is often assisted. The crowded environment and the risk of clumping mean that many chains need help, and chaperones provide it. Folding in the cell is therefore a cooperative process between the chain and its helpers.

Students should understand that folding is a physical process guided by forces, not a conscious search. The chain follows the forces toward lower energy, and the path it takes is shaped by the sequence. Understanding that path is the goal of folding research.

A helpful picture is a ball rolling down a hilly landscape. The ball does not check every valley; it follows the slopes downhill, and it settles into a low point. The landscape is the energy of the chain, and the valleys are stable shapes.

Early researchers tried to imagine folding as a step-by-step process in which the chain tests one arrangement after another until it finds the right one. Cyrus Levinthal pointed out that such a search would be impossibly slow, and this paradox, known as Levinthal's paradox, pushed scientists to think about folding as a guided process. The paradox does not mean that folding is mysterious; it means that the chain cannot be searching blindly, and that physical forces must steer it.

The folding problem also has a practical side. Biotechnology often produces proteins in bacteria or in other cells, and the new proteins must fold correctly to be useful. Engineers and scientists therefore study folding to improve the yield of active proteins, and failure to fold is one of the most common obstacles in making medicines and industrial enzymes.

Folding also depends on timing. Some chains must fold while they are still being made, because the part already built may begin to pack before the rest of the chain exists. Other chains fold only after they are complete. The cell adjusts its machinery to suit each protein, so that folding happens at the right moment and in the right place.

To summarize this chapter, folding is the process by which an unfolded chain adopts its functional shape. The problem is hard because of the enormous number of possible shapes, yet folding is fast because it follows guided routes. Chaperones help in the crowded cell.
