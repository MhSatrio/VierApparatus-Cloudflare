Several forces act together to drive folding. The most important is the hydrophobic effect, which buries nonpolar side chains away from water. Hydrogen bonds stabilize helices and sheets, salt bridges link charged residues and disulfide bridges lock some regions in place. The balance of these forces determines where the chain settles.

The hydrophobic effect is usually the strongest driver. When hydrophobic residues are exposed to water, the surrounding water molecules are forced into ordered arrangements, which costs energy. The chain folds to bury these residues, releasing the water and lowering the total energy of the system.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that these forces, acting on the sequence, produce the native fold. The forces are weak individually, but together they stabilize one shape strongly enough to make it the most stable form of the protein.

Dill and MacCallum (2012) explained that the forces are not independent. Hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic packing and electrostatics influence one another, so that changing one alters the others. This interplay makes folding a collective phenomenon rather than a sum of separate effects.

Pearl and Prodromou (2006) noted that the cell's environment changes these forces. Salt, pH and temperature affect electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonds, which means that a protein may fold differently in different conditions. Cells keep their conditions within a range that favors the correct folds of their proteins.

Students should note that the forces are most effective when they work together. A helix stabilized by hydrogen bonds is more likely to pack against a hydrophobic core, and that packing makes the helix more stable. The cooperation of forces gives folding its robustness.

A helpful picture is a set of magnets and springs that settle together. Each magnet pulls in its own direction, and the springs add tension, but the final arrangement is the balance of all the pulls. The fold is the resting point of that balance.

Electrostatic forces also matter. Charged residues attract opposite charges and repel like charges, and these interactions can help or hinder folding depending on where they lie. Charges on the surface of a protein are usually well hydrated and contribute little to stability, while charges buried in the core are costly and are generally avoided unless they serve a specific function.

Some forces are weaker and more subtle. Van der Waals contacts occur when atoms pack closely together, and they add up across a large core. Aromatic rings can stack on one another, and the stacking adds stability in some folds. None of these forces is strong alone, but many small contributions together give a protein its firm shape.

Mutations show how these forces matter. Replacing a buried hydrophobic residue with a polar one can leave a cavity or expose a surface that should be hidden, and the protein may fold poorly or not at all. Replacing a surface residue often has little effect, because the surface is where the forces are weakest and most flexible.

Water is central to every one of these forces. Because the hydrophobic effect depends on water, changes in the amount of water or in its structure near the protein can alter folding. This is one reason that the chemistry of the cell's interior, which is far from pure water, shapes how proteins fold.

Researchers often test the role of each force by changing one of them and measuring the effect on stability. Adding salt to a solution, for example, weakens some electrostatic interactions, and the change can be detected by measuring how much heat or chemical denaturant is needed to unfold the protein. Experiments of this kind show that no single force decides the fold, and they help separate the contribution of each one from the others.

To summarize this chapter, folding is driven by the hydrophobic effect, hydrogen bonds, salt bridges and disulfide bridges, which act together. Environmental conditions change the balance. The next chapter describes the energy landscape that folding follows.
