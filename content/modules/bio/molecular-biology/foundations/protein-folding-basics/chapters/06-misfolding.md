Misfolding occurs when a chain adopts a shape that is not its native fold. A misfolded protein may be stable but nonfunctional, or it may be unstable and prone to clumping with other misfolded chains. Misfolding is a constant risk in the cell, because the folding process is complex and the environment is crowded.

Misfolded proteins often expose hydrophobic surfaces that would normally be buried. These surfaces can stick to one another, forming aggregates. Aggregates can be harmful because they trap other molecules and disrupt the normal organization of the cell.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the native fold is the most stable under native conditions, but misfolded forms can be trapped in local minima. Once trapped, a chain may need help or may need to be destroyed before it can be replaced.

Dill and MacCallum (2012) described misfolding as a consequence of the rugged energy landscape. Chains that fall into the wrong valley are unable to reach the native state on their own, and the risk grows when conditions change.

Pearl and Prodromou (2006) noted that chaperones help prevent misfolding by binding intermediates and by providing another opportunity for the chain to fold correctly. When chaperones are overwhelmed, misfolding can increase.

Students should know that misfolding is not always the result of a mutation. Changes in temperature, pH or the concentration of proteins can all increase the risk, and cells must cope with these pressures continually.

A helpful picture is a jigsaw puzzle assembled with a piece in the wrong place. The wrong piece may fit loosely, but the picture is incomplete and the puzzle may fall apart. Misfolding is like a piece that has been forced into the wrong position.

Cells have several ways to deal with misfolded proteins. Some are refolded with the help of chaperones. Others are tagged for destruction, and specialized machines break them down into their amino acids, which the cell can reuse. A third route is to sequester aggregates into compartments where they cannot harm the rest of the cell. The balance among these routes depends on the type and extent of the misfolding.

Misfolding is also linked to aging. Over a long life, the cell's quality control systems can become less effective, and misfolded proteins can accumulate. This gradual loss of protein quality is one of the processes thought to contribute to the decline of tissues with age.

Misfolding can also spread between molecules. In some diseases, a misfolded protein can induce normal copies to adopt the wrong shape, creating a chain reaction. This template-like spreading is unusual among proteins, and it has made misfolding an important subject for research on the transmission of disease.

Not every misfolded protein is harmful. Some are cleared quickly, and some cause no visible effect. The harm depends on how much misfolded material builds up, where it accumulates and how well the cell can remove it.

Misfolding can also be shaped by the concentration of the protein itself. In a crowded cell, a chain that is made at high rates has more chances to meet another partly folded chain, and the two can stick together before either has folded. This is one reason why overproduction of a protein, whether from a faulty gene or from an unusual stress, can increase the risk of aggregation even when the protein's sequence is normal.

Researchers detect misfolding with the same kinds of measurements used to study folding in general. Thioflavin dyes, for example, bind to the regular pattern of many aggregates and light up, while a change in the way a protein scatters light or binds antibodies can reveal that its shape has changed. These signals help scientists decide whether a sample contains healthy protein, misfolded protein or a mixture of both.

To summarize this chapter, misfolding produces nonfunctional or aggregating proteins, which arise from rugged landscapes and crowded conditions. Chaperones reduce the risk, but when they are overwhelmed, misfolding increases. The next chapter describes how misfolding contributes to disease.
