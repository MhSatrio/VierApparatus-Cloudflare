A mutation rate describes how often mutations arise. It is usually expressed as the number of new mutations per base pair per generation, or per cell division. Because a genome contains billions of base pairs, even a very small rate produces many new mutations in each generation. Knowing the mutation rate is therefore essential for understanding how genetic variation accumulates, how fast genomes change and how often mutations contribute to disease.

Measuring a mutation rate is challenging. A mutation is a rare event, and it must be detected against the background of ordinary variation. One common approach is to compare the genomes of parents and their children, looking for differences that are not present in either parent. Such differences are new mutations, and counting them across many families gives an estimate of the rate per generation. This approach requires very accurate sequencing, because sequencing errors can look like mutations.

Nachman and Crowell (2000) produced an influential estimate of the mutation rate per nucleotide in humans. They used comparisons of sequences to estimate how many changes arise per base pair in each generation. Their work showed that the rate is small, but that it applies to an enormous number of bases, so the total number of new mutations in each person is considerable.

Lynch (2010) reviewed the human mutation rate in the light of later data and considered how it compares with rates in other organisms. He highlighted that the rate varies between organisms and that it is influenced by factors such as the age of the parents, the efficiency of repair systems and the life history of the species. The human rate is one value among many, and it reflects the biology of our species.

A mutation rate also depends on the type of change. Substitutions, insertions and deletions arise at different rates, and each rate depends on different processes. Some regions of the genome mutate more often than others, because of their sequence or their chromatin state. These differences mean that a single average rate is a useful summary but does not describe every part of the genome.

Mutation rates matter for several reasons. They determine how fast new variants appear in a population, which affects how much variation is available for natural selection to act upon. They influence the rate at which harmful mutations accumulate, which can affect the health of a population over time. And they set the background against which disease-causing mutations are recognized, because a variant that arises at a high rate is less likely to be a rare cause of disease.

Sunyaev (2003) showed how the mutation rate interacts with natural selection and genetic drift. The balance between the rate at which new mutations arise and the rate at which they are removed or spread determines the pattern of variation. A high mutation rate can maintain variation even in the face of selection, while a low rate limits the supply of new variants. The rate is therefore central to the way populations evolve.

It helps to think of the mutation rate as the number of typing errors made by a copyist working on a very long book. Each error is rare, but over many pages the total is large. Some errors are noticed and corrected by the proofreader, while others remain in the final copy. The copyist's rate depends on the care taken, and the book's contents determine which errors matter most.

Scientists also compare mutation rates across species. Organisms with short generation times often accumulate more mutations per year, because they copy their DNA more often in a given period. Organisms with long generation times may have lower rates per year but similar rates per generation. The comparison helps to explain why some species evolve faster than others, and it highlights the difference between rates per generation and rates per year.

Students should note that the mutation rate is not the same as the rate of disease. Most new mutations are neutral or harmful only in small ways, and the ones that cause severe disease are a small fraction of the total. The rate sets the supply of mutations, but the consequences depend on selection, on the function of the affected region and on the individual's genetic background.

Estimating the rate in practice requires attention to several sources of error. Sequencing machines can make mistakes that look like mutations, so researchers must compare reads from the same DNA several times and require agreement before calling a change. Some true mutations are also missed if they lie in regions that are hard to sequence, such as long repeats. Careful studies therefore report the fraction of the genome that could be examined reliably, along with the estimated rate.

Mutation rates also vary between the two sexes. Because germ cells in males divide many more times over a lifetime than those in females, paternal age often has a larger effect on the number of new mutations passed to a child. This is one reason why the rate per generation can differ between parents and why the age of the father is linked to some new mutations in children.

The concept of a rate also helps explain why very old mutations are difficult to date. A mutation that arose long ago may have spread through many lineages, and its age can be estimated only by comparing the patterns of nearby variation. Such estimates depend on assumptions about the rate, and they are refined as better data and better models become available. Honest reporting of the assumptions is part of good science, because it lets others judge how much weight an estimate can bear.

To summarize, a mutation rate is the frequency with which new mutations arise, usually per base pair per generation. Rates are estimated by comparing sequences across generations, and the human estimates show that each person carries many new mutations. Mutation rates shape the variation available to evolution and the background for disease. The next chapter separates spontaneous mutations from those induced by external agents.
