Mutations come in many forms, and the form determines much of their effect. The simplest mutations change a single base, while more complex ones add, remove or rearrange longer stretches of DNA. Biologists classify mutations according to what happens to the sequence, because the type of change helps predict whether it will alter a protein, shift a reading frame or disrupt a regulatory region. This chapter describes the main types and the reasons they matter.

The most common kind of mutation is a point mutation, a change of a single base. A point mutation that replaces one base with another is called a substitution. Substitutions are divided into two kinds. A transition replaces a purine with a purine or a pyrimidine with a pyrimidine, such as A to G. A transversion replaces a purine with a pyrimidine or the reverse. Transitions are more common, partly because some changes are chemically easier than others.

A substitution can have different effects depending on where it occurs. If it lies in a region that does not code for protein, it may have no effect at all. If it lies in the coding region, the effect depends on the genetic code. Because several codons specify the same amino acid, some substitutions are silent. Others change one amino acid for another, which may or may not alter the function of the protein. A few create a stop codon, which ends the protein early.

Lynch (2010) described the spectrum of substitutions in human DNA and the way it differs across the genome. His analysis showed that some kinds of change are much more frequent than others, and that the spectrum reflects both the chemistry of mutation and the repair processes that act on it. The pattern of substitutions gives clues about the sources of damage and error.

Insertions and deletions add or remove bases. A small insertion or deletion that is not a multiple of three bases shifts the reading frame of a gene, so that every codon after the change is read incorrectly. Such frameshift mutations often produce a non-functional protein, because the sequence downstream is completely altered. An insertion or deletion of three bases, by contrast, adds or removes one amino acid without shifting the frame.

Duplications copy a segment of DNA so that it appears twice. A duplication can increase the dose of a gene, and it can also provide a spare copy that may later acquire a new function. Duplications are an important source of new genes in evolution, because the extra copy is free to change without harming the original. They are also linked to some diseases, when the extra dose of a gene is harmful.

Inversions flip a segment of DNA so that it is read in the opposite direction. An inversion does not change the sequence of bases, but it changes their order along the chromosome. Inversions can affect gene regulation, because they move regulatory sequences relative to the genes they control. They can also interfere with the pairing of chromosomes during meiosis, which can reduce fertility.

Translocations move a segment from one chromosome to another. They can be balanced, meaning that no DNA is lost or gained, or unbalanced, meaning that some material is missing or extra. Translocations are important in cancer, where they can join a growth-promoting gene to a new regulatory region and cause it to be overactive.

Sunyaev (2003) considered how these types of mutation contribute to variation in human populations. Substitutions are the most common and provide most of the variation that can be studied with genetic markers, while larger changes are rarer but can have stronger effects. The balance between these types affects how natural selection acts on the genome, because larger changes are more likely to be harmful.

Nachman and Crowell (2000) emphasized that estimates of mutation rate depend on the type of change being measured. Substitutions can be counted directly by comparing sequences, while insertions, deletions and rearrangements require more elaborate methods. A complete picture of mutation therefore requires measurements of every type, not only the simplest.

It helps to think of the genome as a sentence. A substitution changes one letter, an insertion or deletion adds or removes letters, a duplication repeats a phrase, an inversion reverses a phrase and a translocation moves a phrase to another paragraph. Each kind of edit changes the meaning in a different way, and the meaning may be preserved, altered or lost depending on the edit.

Students should remember that the type of mutation and its effect are linked but not identical. A substitution may be silent, a deletion may be harmless and a duplication may be beneficial. The classification helps predict effects, but the actual consequence depends on the context. A full understanding requires knowing both the type of change and the gene it affects.

Mutations can also be described by how far they reach. A change confined to one base is a local event, while a large deletion or duplication can involve hundreds of genes at once. The reach of a mutation matters, because the more genes it touches, the more likely it is to disturb something important. Large changes are therefore rarer than small ones in the population, since most of them are removed by selection before they can spread.

Some mutation types also come in clusters. Repeated sequences can cause small deletions and insertions to appear in the same region, and a stretch of DNA that is unstable in one generation may be unstable again in the next. Recognizing such clusters helps researchers understand why some regions of the genome change more than others.

When several kinds of change occur in the same gene, their combined effect can differ from the sum of the parts. A frameshift followed by a second frameshift may restore the reading frame, leaving an almost normal protein. Such combinations are rare, but they show that the outcome of mutation depends on the full sequence of events, not only on each change taken alone.

To summarize, the main types of mutation are point substitutions, insertions, deletions, duplications, inversions and translocations. Each type changes the sequence or arrangement of DNA in a different way and has a different range of possible effects. The next chapter describes how mutation rates are measured and why they matter.
