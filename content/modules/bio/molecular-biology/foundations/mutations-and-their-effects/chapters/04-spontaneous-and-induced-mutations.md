Mutations have different origins. Some arise from the normal chemistry of the cell, from errors in copying the DNA and from the spontaneous loss of chemical groups from bases. Others are caused by agents outside the cell, such as ultraviolet light, ionizing radiation and certain chemicals. Biologists call the first kind spontaneous mutations and the second kind induced mutations. The distinction helps us understand where mutations come from and how their rates might be changed.

Spontaneous mutations arise without an obvious external cause. The most important source is the errors made by DNA polymerases during copying. These enzymes are very accurate, but they sometimes insert the wrong base, and they sometimes slip on repeated sequences, adding or removing units. The cell has proofreading and repair systems that correct many of these errors, so the mutations that remain are those that escaped correction.

Spontaneous damage to bases also occurs. Cytosine can lose an amino group and become uracil, adenine and guanine can lose their chemical links to the sugar and create abasic sites, and bases can be altered by reaction with water or by small molecules within the cell. Each of these changes can be corrected by repair enzymes if they are detected, but some escape detection and become mutations after the next round of copying.

Lynch (2010) noted that spontaneous mutations are the main source of new variation in humans, because the background rate of copying error and chemical damage is steady and cannot be avoided. His work emphasized that the spectrum of spontaneous mutations reflects the chemistry of these processes, and that repair systems shape which mutations persist.

Induced mutations are caused by external agents. Ultraviolet light, especially from the sun, can cause adjacent pyrimidines on the same DNA strand to become linked, forming a distortion that disrupts copying. Ionizing radiation, such as X-rays, can break the DNA backbone, causing double-strand breaks that must be repaired. Chemicals can add groups to bases or cross-link the two strands, changing the way the DNA pairs.

The effect of an induced mutation depends on the agent and on the dose. A low dose may cause few lesions, which the cell repairs without difficulty, while a high dose may overwhelm the repair systems. Some agents produce characteristic patterns of change that can be recognized in the sequence of a tumor or of a population exposed to a particular source. Such patterns provide evidence about the causes of mutation.

Nachman and Crowell (2000) estimated the rate of mutation in humans, and their work sets a background against which the effect of external agents can be judged. A rise above the background rate after exposure to a mutagen would indicate an induced effect. Careful comparison of exposed and unexposed groups is needed to tell induced mutations from spontaneous ones.

Cells defend against both kinds of mutation. Proofreading by DNA polymerases reduces copying errors, mismatch repair fixes mispaired bases, and excision repair removes damaged bases and their neighbors. Double-strand breaks are repaired by recombination or by joining the ends. These systems are highly developed, but they are not perfect, and their failure can increase the mutation rate dramatically.

Hanahan and Weinberg (2000) described how mutations that disable repair systems or that alter growth control can contribute to cancer. Their review emphasized that cancer requires several changes, and that some of these changes may be induced by external agents while others arise spontaneously. The combination of the two sources helps explain why cancer risk increases with age and with exposure to certain chemicals.

It helps to think of spontaneous mutations as the wear of a machine during normal use, and of induced mutations as damage from an outside hazard. Both wear down the machine, and the maintenance crew repairs as much of the damage as it can. The number of breakdowns depends on the quality of the machine, the intensity of use and the presence of hazards.

Students should note that the distinction between spontaneous and induced mutations is useful but not absolute. Some spontaneous processes are accelerated by the cell's own metabolism, and some external agents act by increasing the rate of spontaneous reactions. The classification is therefore a way of organizing causes, not a sharp division of nature.

The balance between spontaneous and induced sources also varies with the environment. A person who lives in a sunny climate, works outdoors or smokes tobacco is exposed to additional mutagens, and the extra damage adds to the background of copying errors. Public health measures such as sun protection and tobacco control therefore reduce the induced portion of the mutation load, even though the spontaneous portion remains.

Mutagens are also used deliberately in research. Scientists expose cells or organisms to chemicals or radiation to create mutations, and then screen the offspring for changes of interest. This method has identified many genes that control development and disease, and it shows how an understanding of induced mutation can be turned into a tool for discovery.

A simple way to keep the two kinds apart is to ask whether the change would occur in a sealed test tube containing only DNA and the enzymes of copying. Spontaneous changes can occur in such a tube, because they come from chemistry and copying errors alone. Induced changes require an external agent, such as light or a chemical, to be added to the tube before they can occur. This simple test captures the essential difference between the two sources.

The two sources can also add up in the same tissue. A skin cell may accumulate copying errors through normal division and, at the same time, receive ultraviolet damage from sunlight. Counting both sources gives a fuller estimate of the burden of mutation carried by that tissue over a lifetime.

To summarize, spontaneous mutations arise from copying errors and from normal chemical damage, while induced mutations are caused by external agents such as ultraviolet light, radiation and chemicals. Cells defend against both with proofreading and repair, and the balance between damage and repair determines the mutation rate.
