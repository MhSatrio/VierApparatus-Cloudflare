Evolution requires variation, and mutations are the ultimate source of that variation. Every new genetic difference between individuals and between species began as a mutation in some ancestor. Natural selection then acts on the variation that exists, favoring some variants and removing others. The final picture of a species' genome is shaped by the interplay of mutation, selection and chance, and this chapter brings those forces together.

Mutations supply new variants, but they do not determine which variants will spread. A new mutation may be harmful, neutral or beneficial. Harmful mutations are usually removed by natural selection, because the individuals who carry them have fewer offspring. Beneficial mutations may spread quickly through a population if they increase survival or reproduction. Neutral mutations, which have no effect on fitness, may persist or disappear through chance alone.

Sunyaev (2003) examined how these forces act together in human populations. His analysis showed that the pattern of variation depends on the rate of new mutations, the strength of selection against harmful variants and the effects of random drift, which alters allele frequencies in small populations. The balance among these forces explains why some variants are common and others rare.

Genetic drift is a random process. In a small population, the frequency of a variant can change from one generation to the next simply by chance, because only some individuals happen to leave offspring. A neutral mutation can therefore drift to high frequency or disappear altogether without any effect on fitness. Drift is strongest in small populations and weakest in large ones, which means that population size influences the way mutations are retained.

Selection is a directional process. A variant that increases the number of offspring will tend to increase in frequency, while a variant that reduces it will tend to decline. Selection can be strong, acting quickly on a variant with a large effect, or weak, acting slowly on a variant with a small effect. Both kinds of selection shape the genome, and the strength of selection determines how quickly a mutation is removed or spread.

Lynch (2010) emphasized that most new mutations are either neutral or harmful, and that beneficial mutations are rare. This means that most of the variation found in a population is neutral or nearly neutral, and that the influence of selection on the overall pattern is strongest for the rare harmful or beneficial variants. The ratio of these categories helps to explain why genomes look the way they do.

Nachman and Crowell (2000) provided estimates of the rate at which mutations arise per nucleotide, and these estimates help to calculate how many new variants appear in each generation. The supply of new mutations is large in humans, and that supply feeds both the neutral variation that drifts through the population and the rare variants that selection can act upon.

Evolution over long periods results from many such events. A species accumulates changes gradually, and some of the changes become fixed, meaning that every individual carries them. The rate of fixation depends on the balance of forces, and it can be used to estimate how fast genomes change. Comparisons between species reveal the history of these changes and the relationships between their lineages.

Mutations also provide the raw material for new genes. A duplicated gene is free to change, and some of its changes may produce a new function. Over time the duplicate may become a distinct gene with its own role. Duplication followed by mutation is one of the major routes by which life has acquired new functions and new kinds of proteins.

It helps to picture mutation as the supply of new ingredients in a kitchen, and selection as the tasting panel that decides which dishes to serve. The chef keeps adding new ingredients, some of which are improvements and some of which are not. The panel removes the poor dishes and promotes the good ones, while chance decides which of the neutral dishes happen to remain on the menu.

Students should understand that evolution is not simply the accumulation of beneficial mutations. Many changes are neutral, many are harmful and only a few are beneficial. The genome records all of these, and the pattern of variation reveals the forces that shaped it. Reading that record requires comparing genomes and understanding the processes that act on them.

Comparing genomes between species provides a window on this history. Genes that are shared by many organisms often show strong conservation, because changes in them were removed by selection. Genes that differ between lineages may reveal where selection acted differently, or where drift played a larger role. Such comparisons help scientists identify the functional parts of genomes and reconstruct the events that separated one species from another.

Mutation also connects the genome to the origin of new species. Differences in genes that control reproduction or development can build barriers between populations, and over time these barriers can prevent interbreeding. The accumulation of such differences, driven by mutation and selection, is one of the ways that the diversity of life has arisen.

A helpful summary of the chapter is that evolution is a conversation between chance and necessity. Chance supplies the mutations and governs their drift, while necessity, in the form of selection, decides which changes are kept. The genome is the record of that conversation, and reading it reveals both the randomness and the order of life's history. Students who keep this picture in mind will find later evolutionary topics easier to follow.

The story of mutation and evolution also shows the value of patience. Changes that matter for a species may take thousands or millions of years to arise, spread and become fixed. Scientists studying this history must therefore combine evidence from many sources and accept that some conclusions remain tentative.

To summarize, mutations supply the variation on which evolution depends. Natural selection removes harmful variants and spreads beneficial ones, while genetic drift can change the frequency of neutral variants by chance. Most mutations that persist are neutral or nearly neutral, and duplication followed by mutation provides a route to new genes. This completes the module on mutations and their effects, which together with the other modules of the foundations level gives the basic picture of how the molecules of heredity work.
