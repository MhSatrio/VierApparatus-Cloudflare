A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence of an organism. The change may be a single base that has been replaced by another, a small stretch that has been deleted or inserted, or a large region that has been duplicated, inverted or moved to a new place in the genome. Mutations are the ultimate source of all the genetic variation that exists between individuals and between species. Without them, there would be no new versions of genes, and evolution could not occur.

It is important to separate the word mutation from the way it is used in everyday speech. In ordinary language, a mutation is often thought of as something dramatic or monstrous. In biology, most mutations are small and unremarkable. Many change a single base in a region that has no effect. Some change a protein slightly, and only a few have large consequences. The word describes a change in the record, not a judgment about its effect.

A mutation must also be distinguished from ordinary variation. Two people differ in their DNA sequences at millions of positions, and these differences are the normal variation of a species. Each difference was once a mutation that arose in some ancestor and was passed on through generations. A variant that is common in a population is usually described as a polymorphism, while a rare change is often described simply as a variant. The difference is one of frequency rather than of kind.

Lynch (2010) described the human mutation rate in detail and emphasized that mutations are the basis of genetic variation. His analysis showed that new mutations arise in every generation, and that the number of such changes in each person is substantial. Mutations therefore occur continuously, and the variation that exists in a population is the sum of the changes that have accumulated over time.

Nachman and Crowell (2000) estimated the rate at which mutations arise per nucleotide in humans. Their work provided an important measure for comparing the rate of change with the rate of other processes. The estimate depends on careful comparison of sequences across generations, and it helped to establish a baseline for later studies of mutation.

Mutations arise from several sources. Some are the result of errors during DNA copying, when the copying enzyme inserts the wrong base or slips on a repeated sequence. Others come from damage to DNA by chemicals, radiation or the normal chemistry of the cell, which can alter bases so that they pair incorrectly. Cells have repair systems that find and fix much of this damage, so the mutations that remain are those that escaped repair.

Mutations can occur in two kinds of cell with very different consequences. A mutation in a germ cell, which produces sperm or eggs, can be passed to the next generation. A mutation in a body cell affects only that cell and the cells that descend from it. Body cell mutations are not inherited, but they can contribute to disease, especially cancer, as described in later chapters of this module.

Sunyaev (2003) examined how mutation interacts with natural selection and genetic drift to shape variation in human populations. His analysis showed that the pattern of variants in a population reflects both the rate at which new mutations arise and the forces that determine which of them persist. A mutation that is harmful is usually removed by selection, while a neutral one may drift to high frequency by chance.

It helps to picture the genome as a long text that is copied many times. Each copy is almost identical to the last, but errors creep in, and some errors are fixed by proofreading while others remain. The errors that survive are the mutations, and over many generations they accumulate into the differences between people and between species.

Students should note that a mutation is not automatically harmful. The effect of a change depends on where it occurs, what it does to the product of the gene and how the organism uses that product. Many mutations are silent, some are mildly harmful and a few are beneficial. Judging the effect of a particular change requires evidence, and that evidence is one of the central tasks of genetics.

The concept of mutation also has a long history in biology. Early geneticists observed sudden changes in traits, such as a fruit fly with a different eye color or a plant with a new leaf shape, and they called these sudden changes mutations. Only later did it become clear that these traits were produced by changes in DNA, and that the changes could be detected at the molecular level. The modern definition therefore combines the old observation of a sudden new form with the molecular understanding of a changed sequence.

Mutation is also a property of the whole population as well as of the individual. When a mutation arises in one germ cell, it starts as a single copy in a single person. If it is passed on, it may spread through the population over many generations, or it may be lost. The fate of a mutation depends on chance and on its effects, and this dynamic is one of the central topics of population genetics.

A practical way to think about a mutation is to ask three questions. Where in the genome did the change happen? What exactly changed in the sequence? And what happens to the product of the gene as a result? Answering these questions in order gives a clear picture of almost any mutation, and it is the approach used by geneticists who study it. Each answer narrows the field, and together they make the mutation understandable.

To summarize, a mutation is a change in the DNA sequence that can range from a single base to a large region of the genome. Mutations arise continuously from copying errors and from damage, they are the source of all genetic variation and they are distinguished from ordinary variation by their origin rather than by their frequency.
