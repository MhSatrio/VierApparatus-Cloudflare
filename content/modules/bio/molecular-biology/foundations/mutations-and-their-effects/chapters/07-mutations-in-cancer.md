Cancer is a disease of the body's own cells, which have gained the ability to grow and divide without the normal controls. In most cancers, this change is driven by mutations that accumulate in a single lineage of cells over a lifetime. Unlike inherited disease, cancer mutations are usually acquired after birth and are present only in the tumor. This chapter explains how mutations lead to cancer and why several changes are usually needed.

Hanahan and Weinberg (2000) described the hallmarks of cancer, a set of capabilities that most tumor cells acquire. These include self-sufficiency in growth signals, insensitivity to growth inhibition, resistance to cell death, limitless replication, sustained blood supply and tissue invasion. Each capability can be acquired through changes in the genes that control it, and the mutations that produce them are the drivers of tumor development.

Cancer genes can be divided into two broad classes. Oncogenes promote growth, and they become cancer-causing when they are mutated so that they are overactive. A single changed copy can be enough to push a cell toward growth, which means that oncogene mutations often act in a dominant way. Tumor suppressor genes restrain growth, and they cause cancer when both copies are lost or inactivated. Losing a tumor suppressor usually requires two hits, one in each copy.

The distinction between these classes explains why cancer is common in some families and rare in others. An inherited mutation in a tumor suppressor gene means that one copy is already lost in every cell, so only one further hit is needed to disable the second copy. Such families have a higher risk of cancer, because the second hit can occur in many different cells over a lifetime.

Lynch (2010) discussed how the rate of mutation in body cells affects the risk of cancer. Because the genome is large and cells divide many times, the number of new mutations that arise in a tissue over a lifetime is considerable. Most of these mutations are harmless, but some hit the genes that control growth, and the chance that a cell accumulates the right combination increases with age.

Nachman and Crowell (2000) helped establish the background rate of mutation, which sets a baseline for the number of new changes that arise in each cell division. The rate of acquired mutation in a tissue depends on how often its cells divide and on the efficiency of their repair systems. Tissues with high turnover, such as the lining of the gut, have more opportunities for mutations to arise and for cancers to develop.

Cancer usually requires several mutations because the cell has many protective barriers. Growth signals must be unbalanced, the checkpoints that stop damaged cells from dividing must be disabled, and the program that triggers death must be avoided. Each barrier can be overcome by a mutation, but overcoming all of them usually takes a series of changes. This is why most cancers are rare in young people and become more frequent with age.

The mutations in a tumor can be compared with those in healthy tissue. Tumor sequencing reveals the changes that were acquired in the cancer lineage, and it can show which genes were hit and in what order. Many tumors carry hundreds or thousands of mutations, but only a few of them are drivers that promote growth. The rest are passengers that happened to arise along the way.

The pattern of mutations in a tumor can also reveal the causes of the cancer. Exposure to ultraviolet light, to tobacco smoke or to other agents leaves characteristic signatures in the DNA. By examining these signatures, researchers can link the mutations in a tumor to the environment that shaped them. Such evidence supports prevention and helps to explain the geography of some cancers.

It helps to picture a tumor as a company whose workers have gradually changed the rules. Some changes make the company expand faster, some remove the safety inspectors and some stop the company from closing its failing branches. No single change is enough, but together they create a business that grows without limit. The mutations are the staff memos that record each change, and the tumor's history is written in them.

Students should note that not every mutation in a cancer contributes to its growth. The distinction between drivers and passengers is important, and it requires evidence to establish. Researchers test the effect of a candidate mutation in cells or in animals, checking whether it promotes growth or survival. Careful testing is needed because the sheer number of mutations in a tumor can mislead.

Cancer mutations also explain why treatments work for some patients and not for others. A drug that blocks an overactive oncogene is most effective in tumors that depend on that oncogene. A tumor with a different set of drivers may not respond. Sequencing a tumor can therefore guide the choice of therapy, and the mutations in the tumor become the basis for personalized treatment decisions.

Mutations in cancer can also change over time. A tumor that responds to treatment may later develop new mutations that make it resistant. The evolution of the tumor under treatment is itself a process of mutation and selection, and it shows the same principles that act on populations of organisms in nature.

Students should also note that cancer is not a single disease. Tumors arising in different tissues carry different sets of mutations, and even tumors of the same type can differ between patients. This variety makes cancer research demanding, but it also means that each tumor's mutations can reveal something specific about its origin and behavior.

Patients and families often ask whether a cancer is inherited or caused by the environment. The mutation evidence helps answer this question. Some tumors carry an inherited mutation in every cell of the body, while most carry only acquired changes that are confined to the tumor. Knowing which situation applies guides both screening and advice.

To summarize, cancer arises when mutations accumulate in body cells, disabling the controls that restrain growth and death. Oncogenes become overactive, and tumor suppressors are lost, usually through several hits. The total number of changes is large, but only a few are drivers, and their pattern reveals the history of the tumor. The next chapter describes how mutations shape evolution.
