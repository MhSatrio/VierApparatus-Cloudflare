Many diseases have their origin in mutations. Some are caused by a single change in one gene, while others arise from combinations of changes and from the influence of the environment. Inherited diseases pass from parent to child because the mutation is present in the germ cells, and the pattern of inheritance depends on how many copies of the gene must be changed for the disease to appear. This chapter explains how mutations cause disease and how the cause is traced.

A single-gene disorder results from a mutation in one gene that has a strong effect on health. The disorder may be dominant, meaning that a single altered copy is enough to cause the disease, or recessive, meaning that both copies must be altered. Dominant disorders are often caused by changes that produce a harmful protein or that reduce a protein below the level the body needs. Recessive disorders are often caused by loss of function, where both copies fail to produce enough working protein.

The difference between dominant and recessive inheritance follows from the way proteins work in the body. Many genes produce proteins that the body can use in only half the amount of a normal gene, so losing one copy is harmless. Others produce proteins whose faulty form interferes with the normal one, so a single faulty copy is enough to cause disease. Understanding which mechanism applies is important for predicting the pattern of inheritance.

Sunyaev (2003) examined how harmful mutations are removed from populations by selection, and how some persist at low frequency. Disease alleles that are strongly harmful tend to be rare, because they are removed each generation, while those with milder effects may remain at higher frequency. The balance between mutation and selection determines how common a disease allele is in a population.

Lynch (2010) described the consequences of human mutation in detail and noted that most new mutations are either neutral or mildly harmful. Disease-causing mutations are a small fraction of the total, but because the genome is so large, each person carries several harmful changes. Most of these are recessive and do not cause symptoms unless two copies are present.

Nachman and Crowell (2000) helped establish the rate at which new mutations arise in each generation. This rate sets the baseline for how often new disease mutations appear, even in families without a history of the disease. Some disease cases arise from new mutations that occur in a parent's germ cells, and they can appear suddenly in a family with no earlier cases.

Finding the mutation that causes a disease is a challenge. Researchers compare the DNA of affected and unaffected family members, looking for a change that is present in all affected individuals and absent from the others. In small families, the search may focus on a region of the genome where affected members share a stretch of DNA. In larger studies, the sequences of many individuals are compared, and statistical methods help to pick out the likely cause.

Once a candidate mutation is found, it must be tested. Researchers may create the same mutation in cells or in model organisms, and they check whether it produces the expected change in the protein or in the cell. Confirmation requires careful experiments, because a variant that is simply associated with a disease may not be the actual cause.

Mutations also contribute to complex diseases, which involve many genes and the environment. In such diseases, each mutation may increase risk slightly, and the disease appears only when many risk factors combine. These diseases are harder to understand than single-gene disorders, because their causes are spread across the genome and interact with lifestyle and environment.

It helps to think of inherited disease as a fault in one part of a machine, where the effect depends on how many backup parts the machine has. If a part has a spare, a fault in one copy may go unnoticed, but if the part has no spare, a single fault can stop the machine. The pattern of dominant and recessive disease reflects this difference in redundancy.

Students should understand that a mutation does not guarantee disease. Penetrance is the proportion of people with a mutation who show the disease, and it can be less than one hundred percent. Expressivity describes how severe the disease is in those who have it. Both depend on other genes, the environment and chance, and they make the connection between genotype and disease less direct than it might appear.

Genetic testing is one of the most important practical uses of mutation research. A person can be tested for known disease variants, which helps with diagnosis, with the planning of family decisions and with the monitoring of risk. Test results must be interpreted with care, because a variant may be benign, harmful or of unknown effect, and the meaning of a result depends on the strength of the evidence behind it.

Families affected by an inherited disease often need support as well as information. Genetic counselors explain the pattern of inheritance, the chance of passing on the mutation and the options available, and they help families understand results that may be difficult to accept. This human side of genetics is an important part of the work of medicine.

The study of disease mutations also reveals how the body normally works. A gene whose loss causes disease must be doing something important in healthy people, and the disease tells us what that job is. In this way, the mutations that cause illness have contributed greatly to the understanding of normal biology. Every disease gene studied this way adds a piece to the map of human function.

To summarize, mutations cause inherited disease when they change the function of a protein or its amount. Dominant and recessive patterns reflect whether one or two copies must be changed. Researchers find disease mutations by comparing affected and unaffected individuals and by testing candidates experimentally.
