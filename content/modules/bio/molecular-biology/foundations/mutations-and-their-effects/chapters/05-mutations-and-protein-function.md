A gene carries instructions for a protein, and a mutation in the gene can change those instructions. The consequences for the protein depend on what the change does to its amino acid sequence, its folding and its amount. Some mutations have no effect on the protein at all, some make it slightly less efficient, and some destroy it. This chapter explains how mutations act on proteins and why the effects vary so much.

The most direct effect is a change in the amino acid sequence. A substitution that changes one codon to another may replace one amino acid with a different one. The new amino acid may have similar chemistry, in which case the protein may function almost normally, or it may have very different chemistry, which can change the shape or the binding properties of the protein. Whether such a change matters depends on the position of the amino acid in the protein and on its role.

Some positions in a protein are more sensitive than others. Amino acids in the active site of an enzyme, which carry out the chemistry, are usually critical, and changing them can abolish activity. Amino acids that hold the protein in its folded shape may also be important, because a change can disrupt the fold. Amino acids on the outside of the protein, far from its active site, are often more tolerant, and they can change with little effect.

Sunyaev (2003) considered how natural selection acts on such sensitive and tolerant positions. Because harmful changes at critical positions are removed, the variation that survives in a population tends to lie at positions where changes are tolerated. This pattern of conservation is one of the main clues that biologists use to identify the functional parts of proteins.

A change that creates a stop codon can be especially damaging. The ribosome stops translation early, producing a truncated protein that is often unstable or non-functional. The cell may also detect the faulty message and destroy it through a quality control pathway. Stop mutations are therefore frequently harmful, although their effect depends on how much of the protein is still produced and what it lacks.

A frameshift mutation is even more disruptive, because it changes every codon after the affected site. The resulting protein is usually a different sequence altogether, often ending early, and it is typically non-functional. Frameshifts are among the most severe changes that can affect a protein-coding gene, and they are often responsible for dominant or severe recessive disease.

Some mutations do not change the protein's sequence at all but change its amount. A mutation in a promoter, an enhancer or a splice site can reduce the production of the protein, even though the protein itself is normal. The effect depends on how much of the protein the cell needs. Many genes can tolerate a reduction by half, while others are sensitive to a much smaller drop. The dosage of a protein is therefore an important determinant of its function.

Lynch (2010) discussed how the consequences of mutations depend on the context of the gene, the organism and the environment. His analysis pointed out that the effect of a change on a protein is not fixed but depends on other genes and on conditions. A mutation that is harmless in one setting may be damaging in another, which means that the effects of mutations cannot always be predicted from sequence alone.

Nachman and Crowell (2000) provided the baseline rates needed to understand how often such changes occur. Knowing the rate of substitutions and insertions helps researchers estimate how many of these changes arise in each person, and how many of them could affect a protein. The statistics of mutation and the biology of protein function must be combined to understand the health impact.

It helps to picture a protein as a machine with many parts. A bolt that holds the frame together is critical, and a loose screw in the paint is not. A change in a critical part can stop the machine, while a change in a cosmetic part makes little difference. The task of the molecular biologist is to identify which parts are critical and which are not, using sequence comparisons, structural information and experiments.

Students should note that a mutation's effect can be subtle. A protein may work at ninety percent of its normal rate, and the organism may not notice. Such a small change can still matter in combination with other changes or under stress. The consequences of mutation therefore range from invisible to severe, and they depend on many factors.

A protein's function also depends on its interactions with other molecules. A mutation may leave the active site intact but change the surface where the protein binds a partner, which can disrupt a pathway without affecting the chemistry itself. Such changes can be just as harmful as changes at the active site, and they can be harder to detect because the protein looks normal when tested alone.

The effect of a mutation can also depend on the protein's location in the cell. A protein that must reach a particular compartment may fail to arrive if a mutation disrupts the signal that directs it there. In that case the protein may be perfectly folded and active, yet still non-functional in the cell. Mutations of this kind show that the function of a protein depends on its whole life, from making to delivery.

Researchers often test the effect of a mutation by making a protein with the change and measuring its activity in the laboratory. If the activity falls, the change is likely to be harmful, although the test must be designed to reflect the conditions in the cell. Such experiments are an essential bridge between the sequence of a mutation and its consequences for the organism.

To summarize, a mutation can alter a protein's amino acid sequence, its folding or its amount. The effect depends on the position of the change, on the type of mutation and on the context in which the protein works. Stop and frameshift mutations are often severe, while changes at tolerant positions may have little effect. The next chapter describes how mutations contribute to inherited disease.
