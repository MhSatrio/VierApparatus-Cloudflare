Nobody has ever seen a single DNA molecule with the naked eye, and no ordinary microscope can show the bases of one strand. Yet scientists know a great deal about the shape and size of DNA. They know its width, the spacing of its bases, the twist of its helix and the positions of its atoms. This chapter explains the main methods used to measure and image DNA, and shows how the evidence from these methods fits together into the model described in earlier chapters.

The oldest of these methods is X-ray diffraction. When a beam of X-rays passes through a crystal or a regular fiber, the rays scatter in a pattern that depends on the arrangement of the atoms. The pattern can be recorded on film, and it can be analyzed mathematically to work out the spacing and arrangement of the molecules. Franklin and Gosling (1953) used this method on fibers of DNA. Their images showed a pattern of spots that could only be explained by a helix with a regular repeat, and the spacing of those spots gave the dimensions of the molecule.

Diffraction works best on ordered material. A single DNA molecule in solution is too disordered to give a clear pattern, so the fibers used by Franklin and Gosling were aligned in a way that brought many molecules into a similar orientation. This is an important lesson for anyone studying molecular structure. The method reveals the average structure of many molecules, and the quality of the result depends on how well the sample is ordered.

Watson and Crick (1953) combined these X-ray measurements with chemical knowledge and built physical models. They adjusted the models until they matched the measured spacings and the known sizes of the bases and backbone. This process shows how structural biology works in practice. Measurements constrain the possible shapes, and the researcher builds a model that satisfies all the constraints at once.

Much more detail became possible when researchers could determine the position of every atom in a molecule. This is done with a technique that also uses X-ray diffraction, but on crystals of the molecule rather than fibers, and with more advanced analysis. In 1997, Luger and colleagues used this approach to determine the structure of the nucleosome, the basic unit of DNA packaging, at a resolution of 2.8 Å. At this level of detail, the positions of individual atoms could be traced, and the path of the DNA around the histone core could be mapped.

It is useful to understand what the unit Å means. The Ångström is a unit of length equal to one ten-billionth of a meter. Atoms are typically a few Ångströms across, so a resolution of 2.8 Å means that the structure can show details at about the size of individual atoms. Lower resolution gives only a general outline, while higher resolution shows the chemical features in detail. Choosing the right resolution for a question is part of good structural work.

Microscopes provide a different kind of view. Electron microscopes can image very thin samples of DNA and chromatin with great detail, and they revealed the regular beads of nucleosomes in the early work described in an earlier chapter. Atomic force microscopes use a very fine tip that feels the surface of a sample, building up an image of its height. Both methods can show individual molecules, which is something X-ray diffraction cannot do directly.

Fluorescence methods offer a third view. DNA can be stained with dyes that glow under light of a particular color. When these stained molecules are viewed under a fluorescence microscope, they appear as bright lines or spots, and their length and position can be measured. This approach is widely used in teaching laboratories and in research, because it is relatively simple and does not require specialized equipment.

Each method has strengths and limitations. X-ray diffraction gives exact measurements of structure, but it needs ordered samples. Electron and atomic force microscopy show individual molecules, but they usually require the sample to be dried or fixed. Fluorescence is easy and gives information about position and length, but it does not show atomic detail. A good study of DNA often combines several methods, so that the strengths of one make up for the weaknesses of another.

For a beginner, the most important point is that scientific knowledge about DNA comes from many independent lines of evidence. The double helix was not seen in a single picture. It was built from X-ray patterns, chemical measurements, physical models and later from many other techniques. Each new method either agrees with the model or reveals something the model did not predict. This is how the structure of DNA has been refined over more than seventy years.

A modern example shows how the same ideas are used today. Luger et al. (1997) determined the nucleosome structure by growing crystals of the complex and then recording the diffraction patterns of those crystals. The crystals had to be made from carefully purified protein and DNA, and the analysis required the researchers to fit atomic models into the electron density maps they produced. The result was far more detailed than the fiber studies of the 1950s, and it allowed scientists to ask questions about individual contacts between the DNA and the histones.

Measurement also depends on careful sample preparation. A common source of error in DNA imaging is the drying of samples, which can change their shape. Researchers therefore compare results from several methods and check that the features they see are not produced by the preparation itself. This habit of cross-checking is one of the most important skills for anyone who wants to interpret structural data correctly.

To summarize, the structure of DNA has been measured by X-ray diffraction of fibers and crystals, by electron and atomic force microscopy and by fluorescence methods. Each method provides a different kind of evidence, and together they give a consistent picture of the molecule's size, shape and organization.
