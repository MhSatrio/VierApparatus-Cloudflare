A DNA double helix must be stable enough to protect the genetic information for an entire lifetime, yet it must also be able to open when the cell needs to copy or read it. These two demands seem to conflict, but the structure of DNA handles both. In this chapter you will see which forces keep the strands together, how strands are separated in practice, and why the balance between stability and separation is important.

The strands are held together by three main kinds of interaction. The first is the hydrogen bonding between paired bases, which was described in an earlier chapter. The second is the stacking of base pairs on top of one another, which arises because the flat rings of the bases attract each other when they are close. The third is the influence of the surrounding water and ions, which affects how easily the negatively charged backbones can be held near each other. Yakovchuk (2006) analysed the thermal stability of DNA and found that both the pairing and the stacking contribute to the total stability, which means neither can be ignored.

The stacking interaction deserves special attention, because it is often underestimated by beginners. The bases are flat, and when they sit on top of each other inside the helix they form a column that is held together by attraction between the electron clouds of their rings. Stacking is strongest between certain combinations of bases, so the sequence of a stretch of DNA affects its stability as well as its composition. A region with many G and C pairs has both more hydrogen bonds and often stronger stacking, which makes it more stable overall.

Scientists measure the stability of DNA by heating it. As the temperature rises, the hydrogen bonds break and the stacking weakens. At a certain point, the two strands separate. This process is called "melting", and the temperature at which half of the molecules have separated is called the "melting temperature". The melting temperature depends on the length of the DNA, on its sequence and on the salt concentration of the solution. A short, A and T rich sequence melts at a lower temperature than a long, G and C rich one.

Melting is not just a laboratory trick. In cells, the strands must be separated at specific places and times. Transcription requires a short region of the helix to be opened so that RNA polymerase can read the template. Copying requires the whole molecule to be opened along a replication fork. Enzymes called helicases do much of this work, using energy from chemical fuel to pull the strands apart. Other proteins bind to the separated strands and keep them from rejoining until copying is complete.

This leads to a helpful point about the design of DNA. The double helix is stable enough to resist accidental separation in the cell, because the bases are held together by many small interactions. Yet it is flexible enough to be opened locally when needed, because the interactions are weak individually. The cell can therefore open only the region it needs, which makes DNA a very efficient storage system. The stability protects the information, and the local opening makes the information usable.

The sequence of bases also has an effect on how easily the strands separate in a particular place. The A and T rich regions are the first to open, because they have fewer hydrogen bonds and less stacking. This is one reason why many promoters, the signals that start transcription, contain A and T rich stretches. These stretches open more easily, which allows RNA polymerase to enter the DNA. Later modules will describe this in detail.

Laboratory scientists use the same principles every day. In the polymerase chain reaction, DNA is heated to separate the strands, then cooled so that short primers can bind to the target sequence, and then the enzymes copy the region. The temperatures used depend on the sequence, because the primers must bind strongly enough to find their target but must not bind to the wrong place. Choosing these temperatures requires a good understanding of melting and stability.

Watson and Crick (1953) noted that the strands of DNA can be separated and that each one can serve as a template. Their model therefore pointed toward a method of copying that depends on the strands being separable. Franklin and Gosling (1953) provided structural evidence that the helix has a regular, repeating form, which would be disrupted when the strands separate. Together, these early studies set the stage for the later work on how the strands are opened and rebuilt.

A common misunderstanding is that DNA is either fully double-stranded or fully single-stranded. In reality, DNA can be partly open, with a small region of separated strands surrounded by intact double helix. Such bubbles occur naturally during transcription and copying, and they are short-lived. They are one of the clearest examples of how the flexibility of DNA is used by the cell.

Chemicals can also weaken the double helix. Urea and formamide disrupt the hydrogen bonds between bases and lower the melting temperature, and this property is used in laboratory methods that need strands to separate at lower temperatures. Changing the acidity of the solution has a similar effect, because extreme pH values alter the chemical state of the bases and can break the pairs. These effects show that the stability of DNA is not a fixed property of the molecule, but one that depends on its environment as well as its sequence.

Salt concentration matters for a related reason. The negatively charged backbones repel each other, and positively charged ions in the solution shield that repulsion. At low salt, the strands repel one another more strongly and separate more easily. At higher salt, the repulsion is reduced and the strands are held more firmly. Cells maintain a particular ionic environment, and this helps set the stability of their DNA. Laboratory protocols adjust salt concentration carefully for the same reason.

To summarize, the double helix is held together by hydrogen bonds and by base stacking, both of which depend on the sequence and on the surrounding conditions. Heating causes the strands to separate, a process called melting, and the melting temperature depends on sequence and length. Cells open the helix locally, using enzymes that break the interactions in a controlled way.
