A DNA molecule is a very long chain made of small repeating units called "nucleotides". A single nucleotide is small enough to be drawn in a few seconds, but when millions of them are linked in order they form a strand that can carry a complete set of genetic instructions. To understand DNA, it helps to start with one nucleotide and see how it is built.

Each nucleotide has three parts. The first is a sugar molecule with five carbon atoms, called "deoxyribose". Because it is numbered from 1' to 5', the carbon atoms of this sugar are written with a prime mark. The second part is a phosphate group, which contains one phosphorus atom bonded to oxygen atoms. The third part is a "base", a flat ring-shaped molecule that contains nitrogen. The base is the part that carries the information, and it is the only part that differs from one nucleotide to the next.

There are four bases in DNA. Two of them, adenine and guanine, are "purines", which have a double-ring structure. The other two, cytosine and thymine, are "pyrimidines", which have a single ring. The four letters A, G, C and T are the usual shorthand for the bases. A sequence such as ATGCCTA is a simple way of writing the order of bases along a strand. The name of each nucleotide comes from its base, so we speak of the adenine nucleotide or the thymine nucleotide.

Nucleotides link together through their sugar and phosphate parts. The phosphate group attached to the 5' carbon of one sugar forms a bond with the 3' carbon of the next sugar, through a short bridge of oxygen and phosphorus atoms. This bond is called a "phosphodiester bond". Each link creates a repeating backbone made of sugar, phosphate, sugar, phosphate, and so on. The bases stick out from this backbone, like the rungs of a ladder that has been bent into a helix, and they do not take part in the backbone itself.

Because of how the bonds form, every strand of DNA has a direction. One end has a free phosphate group attached to the 5' carbon of the sugar. This is called the "5' end". The other end has a free hydroxyl group on the 3' carbon of the sugar, which is called the "3' end". Chemists describe the strand as running from 5' to 3'. This directionality is not a small detail. Enzymes that build and read DNA can only add new nucleotides to the 3' end, so the direction determines how copying is carried out.

The backbone is also strongly charged. Each phosphate group carries a negative charge at the pH found in cells. This means that DNA is an acid, and that it is strongly attracted to positively charged molecules such as certain proteins. This attraction is one reason why DNA can be packaged so tightly inside the cell nucleus. The negatively charged backbone binds to the positively charged tails of histone proteins, which is part of the story told in later modules on chromosomes.

It is helpful to picture the nucleotide as a handle, a spacer and a message. The sugar and phosphate form the handle and the spacer that link to the next unit, and the base is the message. The handle and spacer are the same in every nucleotide of DNA. Only the message changes. This division of labor helps explain why DNA can store so much information in a very simple structure.

Watson and Crick (1953) relied on this chemical knowledge when they built their model. They needed to know exactly how the sugar and phosphate linked together, so that the backbone could be placed on the outside of the helix with the bases pointing inward. Franklin and Gosling (1953) provided diffraction evidence consistent with a regular, repeating structure of this kind. Together the chemical facts and the X-ray evidence pointed to a backbone of repeating units, with bases in the middle.

Yakovchuk (2006) considered how the bases interact with each other and with the backbone. This work shows that the stability of DNA depends on the bases being arranged in a regular way along the chain, and that the arrangement of the backbone helps to hold them in position. Even a beginner can see that these two parts of the molecule depend on each other. The backbone gives the molecule its shape, and the bases give it its information.

A useful practice for beginners is to draw a single nucleotide and label its three parts. Then draw two nucleotides joined by a phosphodiester bond, and mark which end is 5' and which is 3'. Once you can do this, the structure of a strand becomes much easier to follow. The next chapter will look at the bases in more detail, and show how they pair across the helix.

Students often ask why the names of the parts matter so much. The answer is that biology uses these names to describe where a change happens. A change at the base alters the message, a change in the sugar alters the stability of the strand, and a change in the phosphate alters the charge and the way proteins bind. When a researcher writes that a mutation has occurred at the 3' end of a gene, or that a drug binds the phosphate backbone, these terms identify exactly which part of the nucleotide is involved. Learning the parts is therefore a foundation for reading almost every later chapter of molecular biology.

It is also worth noting that the sugar in DNA is deoxyribose, which lacks an oxygen atom at the 2' position compared with the sugar in RNA. This small difference has consequences for the stability of DNA and for how enzymes treat it. A later module on RNA structure returns to this point. For now, the key facts are that the sugar and phosphate form a repeating backbone, that each strand has a direction from 5' to 3', and that the bases are the part of the nucleotide that changes from one unit to the next.
