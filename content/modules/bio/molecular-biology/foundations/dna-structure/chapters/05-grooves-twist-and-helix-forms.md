So far we have looked at DNA as a ladder with rungs made of base pairs. A real double helix is not a flat ladder, though. It is a three-dimensional spiral, and its shape has features that matter for how proteins and other molecules interact with it. In this chapter you will learn about the grooves on the surface of the helix, the twist of the strands and the different forms that DNA can take.

When the two strands wind around each other, they leave two channels running along the outside of the helix. These are called "grooves". The larger one is the "major groove", and the smaller one is the "minor groove". The grooves exist because the two backbones are not placed opposite each other. Instead, they are offset, so that one backbone leaves a wide opening and the other leaves a narrow one. Watson and Crick (1953) described this feature in their model, and it has been confirmed by later measurements.

The grooves are important because proteins that read DNA usually touch the bases from inside these channels. A protein can feel the chemical pattern of the bases through the edges of the base pairs, which are exposed in the grooves. The major groove is wider and shows more of the chemical features of each base pair, so many DNA-binding proteins contact it. The minor groove is narrower and has a different pattern of chemical groups, and some proteins and small molecules recognize DNA through it. This is one of the reasons why the shape of the helix matters for the control of genes.

The helix also has a twist. As you move along the molecule, each base pair is rotated slightly relative to the one before it. In the common form of DNA, the rotation is about ten base pairs for each complete turn of the helix. This twist is a regular feature, and it is part of what makes the structure so stable. The twist allows the bases to stack neatly on top of each other, while keeping the backbones on the outside.

The twist is not fixed, however. When the molecule is wound more tightly or more loosely, the helix changes shape in response. This property is called "supercoiling" when DNA is twisted beyond its normal state. Supercoiling is important in cells, because the enzymes that copy and read DNA have to untwist or add twist to the molecule as they work. Certain enzymes called topoisomerases manage this, by cutting and rejoining the strands to relieve the strain.

Franklin and Gosling (1953) provided X-ray evidence that the molecule has a regular repeating structure, and the spacing they measured is part of what defines the common form of the helix. The regularity seen in those diffraction patterns is a direct sign of the repeating twist and the grooves. The models built from that evidence show how the bases and backbones fit together in space.

Scientists now recognize several forms of DNA. The most common form in cells is called "B-DNA", which is the right-handed double helix described by Watson and Crick. Under some conditions, DNA can adopt a different form called "A-DNA", which is also right-handed but more compact, with a different twist and a shallower major groove. Another form, called "Z-DNA", is left-handed and has a zigzag backbone. Z-DNA is less common, but it can form in sequences that alternate between purines and pyrimidines, and some researchers think it may play a role in regulating genes.

The existence of different forms teaches an important lesson. DNA is not a rigid object. Its structure responds to its environment, including the amount of water around it, the presence of salts and the attachment of proteins. A molecule that looks simple in a drawing is actually flexible and dynamic in a living cell. This flexibility allows proteins to bend the DNA, to open its strands and to read its sequence, all of which are required for transcription and copying.

Yakovchuk (2006) considered how the stability of the helix depends on its structure, including the stacking of base pairs. A regular, well-stacked helix is more stable than a distorted one, and this helps explain why B-DNA is the usual form in cells. The study supports the idea that small changes in the geometry of the helix can have measurable effects on how strongly the strands hold together.

For a beginner, a good image to keep in mind is a spiral staircase with a handrail on each side. The steps are the base pairs, the handrails are the backbones, and the two spaces between the handrails are the grooves. When the staircase is twisted more tightly, the steps change their angle, and when it is untwisted, they change again. Proteins that visit the staircase touch the steps through the open space of the wider groove, or through the narrower gap on the other side.

Proteins do more than read the grooves. Some proteins actively bend the helix. A classic example is the TATA-binding protein, which is part of the general transcription machinery described in a later module. When this protein binds the TATA box of a promoter, it bends the DNA sharply, by roughly eighty degrees, so that the strand is pushed into a new shape. Such bending brings distant parts of the machinery into contact and helps prepare the promoter for transcription. The flexibility of the helix is therefore not a passive property but something the cell uses directly.

Bending and twisting are closely linked. A stretch of DNA that is bent must also be under strain, and the cell manages this strain with the same supercoiling machinery that handles twist during copying. Because the helix is flexible, a short segment can be bent around a protein without breaking, and the protein can then hold the bent shape long enough to carry out its function. This combination of rigidity along the length of the molecule and flexibility at specific points is one of the most important features of DNA in living cells.

To summarize, DNA is a twisted double helix with a major and a minor groove, a repeating twist of about ten base pairs per turn in its common form, and the ability to adopt several forms. These features are not decorative. They shape how proteins recognize the molecule and how the cell manages its twist during copying. The next chapter turns to what keeps the helix stable and what happens when its strands separate.
