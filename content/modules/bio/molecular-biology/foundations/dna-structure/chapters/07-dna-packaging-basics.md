Think about the length of a human DNA molecule. If the DNA in one cell were stretched out in a straight line, it would be about two meters long. Yet the nucleus of a human cell is only a few micrometers across. The DNA must therefore be folded and packed very tightly, and it must remain organized so that the right parts can be read at the right times. This chapter explains the basic packaging unit, the nucleosome, and why the packaging depends on the chemistry of DNA described in earlier chapters.

The packaging begins with a simple problem. DNA is a long, thin molecule, and it tends to tangle. A cell cannot leave it loose, because a tangled molecule would be impossible to copy or read. The solution is to wrap the DNA around proteins, which hold it in compact units. The basic unit of this wrapping is called the "nucleosome". Each nucleosome contains a short length of DNA wound around a core of small proteins, and the nucleosomes are spaced along the molecule like beads along a string.

The proteins at the core of the nucleosome are called "histones". There are four main types, named H2A, H2B, H3 and H4, and two copies of each form the core, making an eight-part structure called an "octamer". The DNA wraps about one and three-quarter turns around this octamer. The total length of DNA in each nucleosome is about 147 base pairs. Between one nucleosome and the next there is a short stretch of linker DNA, which is not wrapped in the same way.

Olins and Olins (1974) described the particles that later became known as nucleosomes. They observed, using electron microscopy, small rounded bodies along chromatin, the material formed by DNA and proteins. Their work showed that chromatin has a regular repeating structure, rather than being a random tangle. This was an important early step in understanding how DNA is organized in the nucleus.

The detailed structure of the nucleosome was revealed much later, in 1997, when Luger and colleagues determined it at high resolution by X-ray crystallography. Their structure showed exactly how the DNA wraps around the histone core, and how the histones are arranged in pairs. It helped explain how the DNA can be packed so tightly while still allowing parts of it to be opened when needed, a point that later modules will return to in detail.

Why is the negatively charged DNA attracted to histone proteins? The answer lies in the chemistry described in the earlier chapter on nucleotides. The phosphate groups in the backbone carry negative charges, and the histone proteins have many positively charged amino acids, especially in their flexible tails. The opposite charges attract each other, which allows the DNA to wrap closely around the histone core. This attraction is one of the main reasons DNA can be packed so tightly inside a small nucleus.

The packaging has a second purpose as well. Compact packing protects the DNA from damage. Chemical and physical stresses can break the strands, and wrapped DNA is less exposed to those stresses. Packaging also helps to keep the genome organized, so that different regions can be placed in different parts of the nucleus, and so that the cell can control which regions are active at a given time.

Watson and Crick (1953) described the double helix as a simple, regular structure. Packaging adds a further level of organization on top of this basic form. The helix is wound around histones, and the wound structure is itself folded into larger units. Each level of folding depends on the one below it, and each adds compactness and control. The double helix is therefore only the first level of a much larger organization.

A useful way to picture the nucleosome is a spool of thread. The DNA is the thread, and the histone octamer is the spool. The thread is wound around the spool, and the spool is small enough to handle easily. Many spools are strung along the thread with short gaps between them. The result is a structure that is far more compact than the thread alone, while still allowing any part of the thread to be reached.

Packaging also has consequences for how genes are read. When the DNA is tightly wound, the enzymes that read it may not be able to reach the sequence they need. This is why cells must make regions of the genome more open when a gene is needed. A later module on chromatin will explain how cells achieve this, including the chemical modification of histones and the action of machines that move the nucleosomes along the DNA.

It is important for beginners not to think of packaging as a passive storage process. The arrangement of nucleosomes is dynamic. It changes from one cell type to another, from one stage of development to another, and in response to signals from outside the cell. Packaging is therefore part of the regulation of genes, not merely a way of fitting DNA into a box.

The detailed picture confirmed by Luger et al. (1997) shows that the DNA is not wrapped in a random way. It makes about one and two-thirds turns around the histone octamer, and it is held in place at many points by contacts between the protein core and the DNA backbone. The structure also shows that the histones are arranged in two copies of each of four types, forming a disk-shaped core that the DNA follows. This level of detail helps explain how a cell can compact its genome so efficiently while still leaving some parts of the DNA accessible to proteins.

Packaging has a size-related benefit as well. Because the nucleosome is a compact unit, a long chromosome can be shortened several-fold just by adding this first layer of organization. Higher layers of folding then shorten the chromosome further. Each layer builds on the one below, which is why the first unit, the nucleosome, is such an important place to start when studying how the genome is organized.

To summarize, DNA must be packaged because a cell's genome is much longer than the nucleus that contains it. The basic unit of packaging is the nucleosome, in which about 147 base pairs of DNA wrap around a core of eight histone proteins. Attraction between the negatively charged DNA and the positively charged histones makes this wrapping possible. The next chapter looks at histones and their tails in more detail, and at how the packaging is organized at higher levels.
