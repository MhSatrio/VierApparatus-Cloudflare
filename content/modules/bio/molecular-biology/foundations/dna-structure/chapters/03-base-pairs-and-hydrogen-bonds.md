The two strands of a DNA molecule are not held together at random. Each base on one strand is paired with a specific base on the other strand. This rule is called "base pairing", and it is one of the most important ideas in biology. It explains how DNA stores information, how the molecule is copied, and why the sequence on one strand tells us the sequence on the other.

The rules are simple. Adenine (A) pairs with thymine (T), and guanine (G) pairs with cytosine (C). These are called "Watson-Crick pairs", after the two researchers who proposed them in 1953. Watson and Crick (1953) described the pairing as a result of the shapes of the bases, which allow them to fit together in a regular way inside the helix. An adenine on one strand always faces a thymine on the other strand, and a guanine always faces a cytosine. No other combinations fit the regular structure they proposed.

The pairs are held together by "hydrogen bonds". A hydrogen bond is a weak attraction between a hydrogen atom that carries a small positive charge and an oxygen or nitrogen atom that carries a small negative charge. Each individual hydrogen bond is weak, much weaker than a normal chemical bond between atoms. The strength of a base pair comes from having several hydrogen bonds at once. Hydrogen bonds are weak, but there are many of them along the length of the molecule.

An A and T pair forms two hydrogen bonds. A G and C pair forms three. This difference has a real effect on how DNA behaves. A stretch of DNA that is rich in G and C is harder to pull apart than a stretch rich in A and T, because there are more hydrogen bonds to break. Scientists often describe the "GC content" of a DNA sequence, meaning the fraction of bases that are G or C, and they use it to predict how stable the sequence will be. This is useful in laboratory work, for example when choosing the temperature for a reaction.

The pairing rules also explain why the two strands of DNA are "complementary". If you know the sequence of one strand, you can write down the sequence of the other with complete certainty. For example, a strand reading ATGC on one side must pair with a strand reading TACG on the other side, when both are read in the correct direction. This complementarity is the basis for the copying mechanism proposed by Watson and Crick, and it is one of the most useful predictions in molecular biology.

Yakovchuk (2006) looked more closely at how base pairs and base stacking together contribute to the stability of DNA. The study shows that the hydrogen bonds between paired bases matter, but that the stacking of one base pair on top of the next also makes a major contribution. This means that the stability of DNA is shared between two kinds of interaction. The pairs hold the strands together across the helix, and the stacks hold the structure together along it.

Why does it matter that the pairing is so specific? Imagine what would happen if any base could pair with any other base. The helix would be irregular, and the sequence on one strand would tell us nothing about the other. Copying would be unreliable, and genetic information would be lost or scrambled. The strict pairing rules keep the structure regular and make the copying process accurate. This accuracy is one of the reasons that inherited information can be passed from cell to cell with few errors.

Base pairing also explains a practical tool used in every genetics laboratory. A short piece of single-stranded DNA with a known sequence can be used as a probe, because it will bind only to its complementary sequence on another strand. This method, called hybridization, is used to find specific genes in a sample, to detect infections and to test for mutations. The probe finds its target because of the same pairing rules that hold the double helix together.

Students sometimes confuse the number of hydrogen bonds with the total strength of a DNA molecule. The hydrogen bonds between paired bases are important, but they are not the only forces that keep DNA together. The stacking of bases, the attraction between the backbone and the surrounding water, and the presence of positively charged ions and proteins all contribute. A helpful way to think about it is that the pairs are the rungs of the ladder and the stacking is the way the rungs sit on top of one another.

A useful exercise is to write out a short DNA sequence and then write its complement. Remember that the two strands run in opposite directions, so the complement must be read in reverse order to match the correct direction. Practice with a few short sequences makes the idea of complementarity feel natural. It will be important again when we discuss how the cell copies its DNA in the next module.

The stacking of the bases also fits the regular pattern that Franklin and Gosling (1953) observed. Their X-ray images showed a repeat spacing of about 3.4 Å along the axis of the fiber, which corresponds to the distance between neighboring base pairs stacked on top of one another. A base pair stacked at this regular distance, and rotated slightly from the one before it, produces exactly the kind of periodic pattern that the images contained. This is one of the clearest examples of how a physical measurement and a chemical model support each other.

Base pairing also makes DNA a self-correcting system in a limited sense. When a mistake occurs and the wrong base is placed opposite a template base, the mismatch disturbs the regular shape of the helix. Repair enzymes can detect this distortion and remove the wrong base, using the intact partner strand as the reference for the correct sequence. This process depends entirely on the pairing rules, because only a strand with a known partner can serve as a template for correction. Later modules describe these repair systems in detail.

To summarize, adenine pairs with thymine through two hydrogen bonds, guanine pairs with cytosine through three, and the pairs stack regularly inside the helix. These rules make the structure predictable, make copying accurate, and make laboratory techniques such as hybridization possible.
