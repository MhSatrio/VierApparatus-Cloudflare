In the previous chapter, you learned that the bases on one strand pair with the bases on the other strand. Now consider a question that is easy to overlook: how are the two strands arranged along the length of the helix? The answer is that they run in opposite directions. The term used by biologists is "antiparallel". The two strands are parallel in the sense that they lie side by side, but they are antiparallel because one runs 5' to 3' and the other runs 3' to 5' when both are read from the same end of the molecule.

Think of two people walking side by side along a path, but facing opposite ways. One walks forward and the other walks back. Each strand of DNA is like one of these walkers. The 5' end of one strand sits next to the 3' end of the other, and the 3' end of the first strand sits next to the 5' end of the second. When you read a DNA double helix, you always read one strand in one direction and the partner strand in the opposite direction.

Watson and Crick (1953) recognized this arrangement as part of their model. They proposed that the two chains run in opposite directions, which allowed the bases to pair neatly across the middle of the helix. If the strands ran in the same direction, the bases would not fit together in the regular way that the X-ray images suggested. The antiparallel arrangement was therefore not an extra detail, but an essential feature of the structure.

Franklin and Gosling (1953) reported X-ray measurements that were consistent with a regular helix having a repeating spacing along its length. Their diffraction patterns did not show the direction of each strand directly, but they did fit a structure with two chains and a fixed pattern of repetition. The combination of chemical knowledge and diffraction evidence led Watson and Crick to the antiparallel model, which has since been confirmed by many other experiments.

The reason for the opposite directions lies in the chemistry of the backbone. Each phosphodiester bond links the 3' carbon of one sugar to the 5' carbon of the next. A strand therefore has a 5' end and a 3' end, and the strand runs from one to the other. The partner strand has its own 5' and 3' ends, arranged so that its 5' end lines up with the 3' end of the first strand. The two strands are therefore mirror images in direction, though not in sequence, because the bases must pair with their complements.

Why does direction matter so much? The answer is in the enzymes that copy and read DNA. Almost all of them can add new nucleotides only to the 3' end of a growing strand. This means that a copy is always built in the 5' to 3' direction. Because the two strands of a double helix run in opposite directions, a copying machine must handle them differently. One strand can be copied continuously, while the other must be copied in short pieces that are later joined together. This difference is the origin of the "leading" and "lagging" strands, which a later module will describe in detail.

The same directional rules apply to the reading of genes. When RNA polymerase transcribes a gene, it reads the template strand in the 3' to 5' direction while building the RNA in the 5' to 3' direction. The enzyme must therefore follow the correct strand, which is one of the reasons that promoters and other signals must be oriented in a particular way on the DNA. Ignoring the direction of a sequence can lead to a completely wrong interpretation of a gene.

Yakovchuk (2006) studied the stability of DNA helices, and this stability also depends on the antiparallel arrangement. When the strands are held in opposite directions, the bases are stacked in a regular way that favors a stable double helix. The study highlights that the pairing and stacking are most effective in this arrangement, which is one reason the antiparallel double helix is the preferred form of DNA in living cells.

A practical tip helps when reading DNA sequences. A sequence is always written from its 5' end to its 3' end, unless the text says otherwise. When you want to find the partner strand, you must write the complementary bases and then reverse their order. Many errors in laboratory work come from forgetting this step. A sequence that looks correct when written in the wrong direction can lead to a primer that does not work, or to a gene that is read backward.

It is also worth noticing that the two strands are not identical. They are complementary, which means that one strand's sequence determines the other's. This is why the double helix is both a storage system and a copying system. Each strand carries the full information needed to rebuild its partner, and the antiparallel arrangement makes this rebuilding possible in a reliable way.

The antiparallel arrangement has another useful consequence. Because the two strands run in opposite directions, the 5' end of one strand is always paired with the 3' end of the other at the same position along the helix. The two ends of any double-stranded DNA molecule are therefore different from each other, even when the sequence is symmetric. A linear DNA molecule has one end that carries a free 5' phosphate and another that carries a free 3' hydroxyl on each strand, and these distinct chemical ends can be recognized by enzymes that process or join DNA.

Antiparallel pairing is also found in the double-stranded forms of RNA, where a short segment of one strand can fold back and pair with another region of the same molecule. The directional logic is the same. Pairing always joins a 5' to 3' segment with a 3' to 5' segment, so the orientation of a hairpin or a duplex must be considered whenever its sequence is analyzed. Students who learn this rule for DNA find it easy to apply to RNA later in the course.

To summarize, the two strands of DNA run in opposite directions, one 5' to 3' and the other 3' to 5'. This antiparallel arrangement allows the bases to pair neatly and stabilizes the helix. It also determines how enzymes copy and read the molecule, because they can only add to the 3' end of a growing strand.
