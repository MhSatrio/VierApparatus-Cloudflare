For many years, scientists knew that genes were carried on chromosomes, and that chromosomes contained a large molecule called "deoxyribonucleic acid", usually shortened to DNA. What they did not know was the shape of that molecule. A shape matters, because a molecule's structure often explains what it can do. In 1953, two researchers at the University of Cambridge, James Watson and Francis Crick, proposed a model that changed biology. They described DNA as a "double helix", which means two strands wound around a shared central axis, like a twisted ladder.

Their model did not come from a single experiment. It drew on many sources of evidence, and one of the most important was the work of Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling at King's College London. Franklin and Gosling produced X-ray diffraction images of DNA fibers. In these images, the way the X-rays scattered revealed regular patterns that could only be explained by a helical structure with a repeating spacing. Franklin and Gosling (1953) reported these measurements in a short paper published in the same year as the model, and their data helped confirm the basic shape.

Watson and Crick built their model using physical cardboard and metal parts, adjusting the pieces until they fitted the measurements and the known chemistry. Their key insight was that the two strands are held together by specific pairs of bases. The base adenine always pairs with thymine, and the base guanine always pairs with cytosine. This pattern of pairing meant that each strand could act as a template for the other. Watson and Crick (1953) wrote that this specific pairing suggested a possible copying mechanism for genetic material.

The idea of a template is central to the whole model, so it is worth pausing on it. If you know the sequence of bases on one strand, you can predict the sequence on the other strand with complete certainty. The two strands are complementary. This means that the information is stored twice, once on each side of the ladder. If the molecule is split, each half can be used to rebuild the missing partner. Many people find this idea surprisingly elegant, because it provides both a storage system and a copying method in a single structure.

The double-helix model also explained why DNA is so stable. The bases stack on top of each other inside the helix, and this stacking helps hold the structure together. Yakovchuk (2006) studied how the stacking of bases and the pairing between them contribute to the thermal stability of DNA. The study shows that both effects matter, and that together they help the helix remain intact at the temperatures found in living cells, while still allowing the strands to separate when the cell needs to copy them.

Two further clues mattered as much as the X-ray images. The first came from the chemist Erwin Chargaff, whose analyses in the late 1940s and early 1950s showed that in DNA from many organisms the amount of adenine roughly equals the amount of thymine, and the amount of guanine roughly equals the amount of cytosine. This regularity, known as Chargaff's rule, was a strong hint that the bases come in matching pairs. Watson and Crick used it as a guide when they worked out how the bases must fit together.

The model was not accepted without challenge. Linus Pauling had proposed a three-stranded helix earlier in 1953, and it contained chemical errors, including an incorrect treatment of the phosphate groups. Watson and Crick's two-stranded model, with the bases on the inside and the backbones on the outside, fitted the chemistry, the X-ray evidence and Chargaff's rule at the same time. Its ability to explain several independent observations at once is a large part of why it was accepted so quickly.

It is worth noting what the discovery did not solve at once. Watson and Crick showed the general shape, but they did not describe every detail of how DNA behaves inside a cell. Over the following decades, researchers learned that DNA can adopt several slightly different forms, that its shape can change in response to proteins and to chemical signals, and that it is packaged tightly around proteins in the nucleus. These later findings built on the 1953 model rather than replacing it.

Several other researchers contributed to the story, and their work is worth knowing as an example of how science is built. Maurice Wilkins, who worked with Franklin in London, provided some of the early diffraction images that informed the model, and he shared the 1962 Nobel Prize with Watson and Crick. Franklin's own contribution, including her precise measurements and her work on the two forms of DNA, was not recognized with a prize during her lifetime, because the Nobel Prize is not awarded posthumously and she died in 1958. Modern historians of science use this episode to discuss how credit is assigned in research, and it is a good reminder that a single discovery usually rests on many people's efforts.

The discovery also changed how biologists thought about heredity. Before 1953, many researchers had doubts about whether DNA could carry the enormous range of information that genes must hold. The double helix showed that a simple repeating structure could, in principle, encode a very large number of different sequences. The information lies not in the shape of the backbone, which is the same along the whole molecule, but in the order of the bases that sit along it. This separation of a constant framework and a variable sequence is one of the most useful ideas in all of molecular biology.

Finally, the story of the double helix is a reminder that science often moves forward through the combination of different kinds of work. Experimental images, chemical knowledge and physical models were all needed. The model was tested by its ability to explain observations that had puzzled researchers for years. As later chapters will show, the structure of DNA continues to be tested by new measurements, and each new result either fits the model or refines it. This habit of testing and revising is a central part of how biology progresses.
