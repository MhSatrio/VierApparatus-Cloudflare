The ribosome is perhaps the largest and most complex machine in the cell. It reads messenger RNA and builds proteins, moving along the message one codon at a time and adding amino acids in order. Its work involves many shape changes, the coordinated movement of transfer RNAs and the careful checking of each step.

The ribosome has two subunits that come together around the messenger RNA. The small subunit reads the message, and the large subunit carries out the chemistry of bond formation. The subunits open and close as the cycle proceeds, and the ribosome changes shape with each addition of an amino acid.

Steitz (2008) described the ribosome as a dynamic machine whose structure changes during the cycle. The movement of transfer RNAs through its sites, and the shift of the messenger RNA by one codon, are driven by these changes. The ribosome is not a fixed factory but a moving assembly line.

Factors called translocases help drive the forward movement of the ribosome. They bind to the ribosome, use the energy of GTP and shift the messenger RNA and the transfer RNAs into their next positions. The factors are therefore part of the machine's cycle.

The ribosome also has checks built into its cycle. It verifies that the correct transfer RNA has arrived before the peptide bond forms, and it can reject a wrong one. These checks slow the machine slightly, but they greatly improve accuracy.

Vale and Milligan (2000) noted that motor proteins also depend on cycles of binding and release. The ribosome shares this design principle, although its cargo is a growing chain rather than a vesicle. The comparison shows how different machines may use similar strategies.

Students should appreciate that the ribosome combines the features of a motor, a catalyst and a reader. It moves along a track, it catalyzes the bond and it reads the code. The combination makes it one of the most remarkable machines in biology.

A helpful picture is a weaving loom. The thread is the messenger RNA, the shuttle carries the transfer RNA with its amino acid, and the loom moves forward one row at a time. Each row adds a new link, and the fabric grows steadily.

The ribosome also shows how a machine can be stopped and started. When the cell is under stress, it can reduce the number of active ribosomes or slow their cycles. Because each cycle is controlled, the cell can adjust the output of its protein factory quickly, matching production to the supply of building blocks and energy.

Vale and Milligan (2000) described the value of coupling movement to chemistry, a principle that the ribosome also uses. Each step of the ribosome is driven by energy and checked before the next one begins, which is the same logic that gives motors their precise stepping.

Pearl and Prodromou (2006) showed that machines of many kinds share the feature of cycling between states. The ribosome's cycle is one of the most elaborate, but it follows the same general plan: bind, change shape, act and release.

Students who want to understand the ribosome in more depth can imagine each of its cycles as a sequence of small mechanical events, each one preparing the next. Seeing the cycle this way makes the machine easier to follow.

Each full cycle of the ribosome takes only a fraction of a second in a fast-growing bacterium, which means that the machine repeats its steps many thousands of times in a single cell division.

To summarize this chapter, the ribosome is a large machine that moves along messenger RNA, uses shape changes and energy from GTP to advance, and checks each step for accuracy. Its cycle combines motion, catalysis and reading. The next chapter describes chaperones, which help other proteins fold.
