Motor proteins are machines that move along tracks inside the cell, carrying cargo such as vesicles, organelles and other proteins. They are powered by ATP and walk along filaments of the cytoskeleton, which act as roads. Their movement is essential for transport, for cell division and for many other tasks, and cells contain several families of motors.

The best-known motors are kinesin and dynein, which walk along microtubules, and myosin, which walks along actin filaments. Each family has its own structure and its own direction of movement. Kinesin usually moves toward the outer edge of the cell, while dynein usually moves toward the center. Myosin is used for contraction and for moving material along actin.

Vale and Milligan (2000) reviewed the mechanics of these motors and showed how each step is produced by a cycle of ATP binding and release. The motor's two heads or arms alternate their contacts with the track, so that the motor advances without falling off. The arrangement is like walking with two legs, where one foot is always planted.

The steps of a motor are small, often measured in nanometers. A single molecule of kinesin may take many steps before it detaches, carrying a cargo for a considerable distance. Measuring these steps has been one of the great achievements of single-molecule biophysics.

Motors can also carry out tasks other than transport. Myosin in muscle generates force by pulling on actin filaments, and this pulling makes muscles contract. Dynein helps position the spindle during cell division, pulling chromosomes into place. The same basic mechanism is adapted for many purposes.

Motors must be regulated. A motor that moves cargo must release it at the right place, and it must stop when the cargo arrives. Cells control motors by changing their attachment to cargo, by activating or inhibiting them and by directing them to particular tracks.

Students should realize that motors are part of the cell's internal transport system. Without them, vesicles would not reach their destinations, and the organization of the cell would collapse. Motors are therefore essential, not optional, for life.

A helpful picture is a delivery truck that drives along a road and unloads at stops. The truck is the motor, the road is the cytoskeleton, and the packages are the cargoes. The truck must drive, unload and return, and its route is set by the road and the orders it receives.

Pearl and Prodromou (2006) pointed out that chaperones, like motors, rely on ATP to switch between states, and that the cycle of their subunits is coupled to the release of their clients. The comparison shows that many different machines use the same underlying strategy of energy-driven shape change, even when their jobs are very different.

Motors are also studied in living cells. Researchers label cargo with fluorescent tags and watch it travel along the cell's tracks under the microscope. Such observations show how motors respond to signals, how they share tracks and how traffic jams can form when too many cargoes compete for the same route.

Steitz (2008) noted that machines in the cell often have to coordinate with one another. A motor that carries a vesicle must deliver it to a machine that will take it up, and the timing of the two is controlled by signals. Coordination of this kind keeps the cell's internal transport running smoothly.

To summarize this chapter, motor proteins such as kinesin, dynein and myosin walk along cytoskeletal tracks, powered by ATP, and carry cargo or generate force. Their stepwise movement depends on cycles of binding and release, and their activity is tightly controlled. The next chapter describes the ribosome as a large molecular machine.
