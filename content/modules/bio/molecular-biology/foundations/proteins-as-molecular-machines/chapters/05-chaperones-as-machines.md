Chaperones are proteins that help other proteins fold correctly. They do not carry the final shape in themselves, and they do not add information to the chain. Instead, they bind partly folded or unfolded proteins, protect them from clumping and give them time and space to reach their native form. Many chaperones are machines that cycle through states, using ATP to drive the process.

The chaperone Hsp90 is a well-studied example. It works with a group of partner proteins and cycles through states as it binds, holds and releases its client proteins. The cycle uses ATP to close the chaperone around the client, which helps the client reach its active shape before the chaperone opens and releases it.

Pearl and Prodromou (2006) described how the subunits of Hsp90 move during this cycle and how the nucleotide controls the changes. Their review showed that the chaperone is a dynamic machine whose shape changes are coupled to ATP binding and release.

Chaperones also protect the cell. Without them, many proteins would clump together, especially in the crowded cytoplasm. By binding exposed hydrophobic surfaces, chaperones prevent such clumping and allow the proteins to fold without interference. Some chaperones form large barrel-shaped cages in which a single protein can fold in isolation.

The work of chaperones is not always successful. A client may be released in an incorrect shape and then return to the chaperone for another try. Repeated cycles give the protein several chances to reach its correct form, and the cell can destroy proteins that cannot be rescued.

Vale and Milligan (2000) noted that machines of many kinds use ATP to power their cycles, and chaperones follow the same principle. The energy of ATP is used to switch the chaperone between states, which in turn switches the state of the client.

Students should understand that chaperones are helpers, not templates. They help the chain find its own shape, and they can be used repeatedly. Their help is essential in cells where many proteins are made at once and where folding must be fast and accurate.

A helpful picture is a set of supports used while a building is erected. The supports hold the parts in place until the structure can stand on its own, and then they are removed. Chaperones play a similar role for proteins, holding the chain until it folds.

Chaperones are especially important in stressful conditions. When temperatures rise, proteins are more likely to unfold and clump, and the cell increases the number of chaperones it makes. The extra helpers protect the proteins that would otherwise be lost, which is why chaperones are sometimes called heat shock proteins.

Vale and Milligan (2000) described machines that respond to the needs of the cell, and chaperones fit this pattern. Their activity rises and falls with the load of unfolded proteins, so that the cell's folding capacity is matched to its demand.

Steitz (2008) pointed out that the making of proteins and the folding of proteins must be coordinated. Chaperones often bind a chain as it leaves the ribosome, so that folding begins before the whole chain is complete. This timing reduces the risk of misfolding in the crowded cell.

Students should think of chaperones as a support system for the protein factory. Without them, the factory would produce many faulty goods, and the cell would waste resources on repairs.

To summarize this chapter, chaperones are machines that help other proteins fold by binding them, protecting them from clumping and cycling through states powered by ATP. Hsp90 is a clear example. The next chapter describes pumps and transporters, which move molecules across membranes.
