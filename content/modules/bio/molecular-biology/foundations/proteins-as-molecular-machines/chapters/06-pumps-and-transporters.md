Membranes separate the inside of a cell from its surroundings, and many molecules must cross them. Transporter proteins move molecules across membranes, and pumps move them against their gradients using energy. Both are machines that change shape in cycles, opening to one side of the membrane and then the other.

A transporter binds its cargo on one side of the membrane, changes shape so that the binding site faces the other side, and releases the cargo there. The cycle is repeated as the transporter returns to its starting shape. Transporters that move molecules down a gradient do not need an energy source, while those that move against a gradient do.

A pump uses energy, often from ATP, to move ions or molecules across the membrane against their concentration difference. The energy drives the shape changes that carry the cargo, and the pump maintains concentration differences that the cell needs for many purposes, including nerve signaling and the uptake of nutrients.

Vale and Milligan (2000) described machines that move cargo in steps powered by chemical energy, and transporters and pumps follow a similar principle. Their shape changes are driven by binding and release, and their cycles move cargo in one direction.

Pumps are important in medicine. Some drugs work by blocking a pump, and some diseases involve pumps that do not work correctly. The understanding of pump mechanisms has led to treatments for conditions affecting the heart and the kidneys.

Chaperones and pumps share a common theme: both use cycles of shape change powered by energy. Pearl and Prodromou (2006) showed the cycle in a chaperone, and the same cyclic logic governs many membrane machines. The similarity helps scientists see patterns across different kinds of protein.

Students should note that transporters are highly selective. Each transporter recognizes a particular class of molecule, and it ignores others. Selectivity is achieved by the shape and chemistry of the binding site, which is the same principle that gives enzymes their specificity.

A helpful picture is a set of revolving doors in a wall. Each door has a shape that fits certain people, and it turns to let them through from one side to the other. A pump is like a door that needs a motor to turn it, pushing people through even when the crowd on the other side is larger.

Pumps help create the electrical and chemical differences across membranes that cells rely on. The difference in ion concentrations between the inside and outside of a nerve cell, for example, is maintained by pumps, and it makes the nerve able to send signals. Without pumps, the gradients would slowly fade and the nerve would fall silent.

Vale and Milligan (2000) described how movement can be produced by changes in shape driven by chemical events, and pumps show the same principle in a membrane. Each cycle of the pump moves ions one at a time, and the rate of the cycle sets how fast the gradient is maintained.

Pearl and Prodromou (2006) showed that the shape changes of chaperones are coupled to ATP, and the same coupling is found in many pumps. The shared design shows how evolution has reused a good solution for different tasks.

Students should remember that the membrane is a barrier, and pumps and transporters are the gates that control traffic across it. The gates open and close in a sequence set by the machine's structure, letting exactly the right molecules through.

To summarize this chapter, transporters and pumps are membrane machines that move molecules across membranes through cycles of shape change, with pumps using energy to move against gradients. They are selective and medically important. The next chapter describes machines that copy and assemble.
