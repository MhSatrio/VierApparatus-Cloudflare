The shape changes of a molecular machine need energy. Without energy, the machine would simply settle into one state and stay there, or it would drift randomly through its possible shapes. Energy makes the machine move in a particular direction, and the most common source is the breakdown of ATP, the energy carrier of the cell.

When ATP is broken down, the energy released can be used to drive a shape change. The machine binds the ATP, uses its energy to move to a new state, and then releases the products of the breakdown. The binding and release steps are often what tell the machine when to move, so the chemistry and the mechanics are linked.

Vale and Milligan (2000) explained that motor proteins use this link between chemistry and motion. The binding and breakdown of ATP at the motor's active site cause the motor to change shape, and the shape change moves the motor along its track. Each cycle of ATP use moves the motor one step.

Steitz (2008) described how the ribosome uses energy in a related way. Transfer RNAs are loaded with energy-rich groups, and factors that bind GTP drive the movement of the ribosome along the messenger RNA. The energy is used to move the machine forward, rather than to build bonds directly.

Pearl and Prodromou (2006) showed that the chaperone Hsp90 also uses ATP. Binding and breaking down ATP drive the subunits to close and open around the client protein, which is a shape change that helps the client fold. The energy is used to control the timing of the cycle.

Machines can be powered by other sources too. Some are driven by the flow of ions across a membrane, and some by gradients of protons that store energy. The principle remains the same: an energy source is coupled to a shape change, and the coupling gives the machine its direction.

Efficiency matters. A machine that wasted much of its energy would be costly for the cell, so many machines have evolved tight coupling between chemistry and movement. The energy of each fuel molecule is channeled into the intended motion, with little lost as heat.

Students should understand that energy coupling is what makes a machine different from a random mover. The chemical event is timed to the mechanical event, and the timing is set by the machine's structure. This coupling is a central idea in understanding how cells do work.

A helpful picture is a wind-up toy. The spring stores energy when wound, and it releases the energy slowly as the toy moves. The fuel of the cell plays the role of the winding, and the machine converts the stored energy into controlled motion.

The cell keeps a large store of ATP, and its levels influence how fast machines run. When ATP is plentiful, the cycles proceed quickly, and when energy is scarce they slow down. Machines are therefore tied to the cell's energy supply, and their activity reports the state of the cell's metabolism.

Some machines use the energy of ATP in a more subtle way. Instead of driving a single large movement, they use small energy bursts to make repeated adjustments, each one nudging the machine a little further. Such fine control allows machines to respond to delicate signals and to adjust their position with precision.

A useful question to ask about any machine is where its energy enters the cycle, and where its energy leaves. Answering it reveals the logic of the machine and the points where the cell can adjust its activity.

To summarize this chapter, molecular machines are powered by chemical energy, usually from ATP, which is coupled to shape changes that move the machine in one direction. Tight coupling makes machines efficient. The next chapter describes motor proteins in more detail.
