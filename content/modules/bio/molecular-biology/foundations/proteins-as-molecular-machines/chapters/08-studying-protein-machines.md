Protein machines are studied with methods that reveal their shapes and their movements. Structural methods such as X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy capture snapshots of the states a machine adopts. Single-molecule methods follow the movement of individual machines, and biochemical methods measure their speed and energy use.

Single-molecule experiments have been especially valuable for motors. Scientists attach tiny beads or fluorescent markers to a motor and watch it move along a track, measuring each step and the force it produces. These experiments have revealed the stepping mechanism in detail.

Vale and Milligan (2000) reviewed how such methods have shown the mechanics of motor proteins, combining structure, biochemistry and single-molecule observation. Their review shows that machines are best understood when many methods are used together.

Steitz (2008) described how structural studies of the ribosome revealed its moving parts and the sequence of its states. Snapshots of different states, captured at different stages of the cycle, allowed researchers to assemble a movie of the machine at work.

Pearl and Prodromou (2006) described structures of the chaperone Hsp90 in different states, which showed how its subunits move during the ATP cycle. The structures helped explain how the chaperone holds and releases its clients.

Each method has limits. Snapshots show states but not the timing of the moves, while single-molecule methods show timing but may disturb the machine. Good studies combine methods, so that the strengths of one make up for the weaknesses of another.

Students should understand that studying machines is like studying an engine. You can look at the parts, measure the output, watch it run and test what happens when a part is changed. Each approach gives a different kind of understanding.

A helpful picture is a detective piecing together a sequence of events from photographs, witness reports and timed measurements. No single clue tells the whole story, but together they reveal how the machine works.

Mutations provide another route to understanding machines. When a change in a protein alters one step of its cycle, the machine may stall in a particular state, and that state can be studied in detail. Such mutants have helped researchers identify the parts that drive each movement, because removing a single part can reveal what it normally does.

Computer simulation adds further insight. Models of a machine's shape can be run on a computer to predict how the parts move and which contacts form and break. The predictions are then tested in the laboratory, and the agreement or disagreement guides the next experiment.

The study of the chaperone cycle shows how structural and biochemical data can be combined to explain a machine's behavior, as the work of Pearl and Prodromou illustrates. The combination of methods gave a picture that no single approach could have provided alone.

Steitz (2008) noted that the study of the ribosome has depended on many techniques working together, from crystallography to chemical probing. Each technique revealed part of the machine's operation, and the full story emerged from their combination.

Students should understand that machines are often studied across several scales at once, from individual atoms to the behavior of whole cells. Connecting these scales is one of the great challenges of modern biology.

A practical lesson for students is that every method has a blind spot. Photographs cannot show motion, and movies made from single molecules cannot show the full cell. Reading the literature critically means asking which method produced each claim and what it could and could not reveal.

To summarize this chapter, protein machines are studied with structural snapshots, single-molecule observations and biochemical measurements, and combining these methods gives the best understanding.
