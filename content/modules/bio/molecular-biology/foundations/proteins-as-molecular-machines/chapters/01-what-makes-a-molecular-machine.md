A molecular machine is a protein or a complex of proteins that carries out a task by changing shape in a repeated, controlled way. Unlike a simple enzyme that catalyzes one reaction and returns to its starting state, a machine moves through a cycle of states, and each state performs part of the job. The cycle is often driven by energy from chemical fuel.

What separates a machine from an ordinary protein is the cycle. A machine has at least two stable shapes, and it switches between them in a way that does work. The switch may move a load, pull a strand, open a channel or join two parts together. The movement is directed, so that the machine repeats its task rather than wandering randomly.

Vale and Milligan (2000) described the motor proteins as a clear example of this idea. Their review showed how a motor binds to a track, changes shape and releases, moving a cargo along the track in steps. The same logic applies to other machines, although the details differ.

Pearl and Prodromou (2006) described the chaperone Hsp90 as a machine that cycles through states. Its subunits change arrangement as they bind and release a client protein, and the cycle helps the client reach its correct shape. The chaperone is therefore a machine that works on other proteins.

Steitz (2008) described the ribosome as a machine that moves along a messenger RNA, adding one amino acid at each cycle. The ribosome changes shape as transfer RNAs enter and leave, and it steps forward one codon at a time. Its cycle is one of the most complex in the cell.

Machines in the cell often work together. A group of machines may pass a product from one to the next, forming an assembly line. Such linked machines can carry out long sequences of tasks efficiently, and the cell uses many of them in parallel.

Students should think of a molecular machine as a protein that does work, not merely as a protein with a shape. The work is the movement, and the movement depends on the energy that drives each cycle. Without energy, the machine stops in one state.

A helpful picture is a revolving door. The door has several positions, and it moves from one to the next as people pass through. Each passage is a cycle, and the door keeps moving in one direction. A molecular machine works in a similar way, with its states arranged so that each cycle moves the system forward.

The idea of a machine also helps explain why a protein's shape must be precise. A machine with parts in the wrong places would jam, and a protein whose cycle is disturbed by a mutation may stop working even though its chemistry is intact. Machines depend on the accuracy of their folds as much as on their catalytic sites, and the folding rules described in earlier modules apply to them too.

Scientists studying machines often compare them to engineered devices. An engine, a clock and a loom all convert energy or information into movement, and each has parts that move in a cycle. Thinking about proteins in this way is a useful model, although the cell's machines are far smaller and are built from folded chains rather than metal.

Learning to picture machines in motion is a valuable skill, because it turns a list of parts into a sequence of events.

To summarize this chapter, a molecular machine is a protein or complex that carries out a task by cycling through shapes. Machines differ from simple enzymes by their repeated, directed movement, and they often work in linked groups. The next chapter describes how their shape changes are powered.
