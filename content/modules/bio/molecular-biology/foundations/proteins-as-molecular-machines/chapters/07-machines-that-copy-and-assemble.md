Some protein machines copy genetic material or assemble other structures. The copying machines, such as DNA and RNA polymerases, move along their templates and add new building blocks, changing shape with each addition. Assembly machines join subunits into larger structures, guided by the shapes of their parts.

The machines that copy DNA work in a cycle much like the ribosome. They bind the template, select the correct building block, add it to the growing strand and move forward. Their accuracy depends on checks similar to those described for the ribosome, and they often correct errors as they go.

Steitz (2008) emphasized that the ribosome shares principles with other nucleic acid machines, including the polymerases that copy DNA and RNA. The movement along a template and the step-by-step addition of building blocks are common themes across these machines.

Assembly machines build structures such as the cytoskeleton, the viral capsid and the complexes that make up organelles. They may act by bringing subunits together in the right orientation, by providing a template for the structure or by catalyzing the joining of parts. Their shape changes guide the assembly.

Vale and Milligan (2000) described how motor proteins move along filaments that are themselves being built and disassembled. The cytoskeleton is a dynamic structure, and the machines that build and move along it help determine its shape and the placement of cargo.

Pearl and Prodromou (2006) described how chaperones help assemble complexes by holding their parts in the right arrangement. The chaperone does not join the parts itself, but its cycle makes assembly more reliable and less prone to error.

Students should realize that copying and assembly machines are essential for growth and reproduction. A cell that could not copy its DNA or build its structures would not survive. These machines therefore sit at the center of the cell's life.

A helpful picture is a printing press that copies pages and a bookbinder that joins them into a volume. The press moves along the master, the binder brings the pages together and the resulting book is the assembled structure. Both machines depend on precise steps.

Copying machines are very accurate, but accuracy has a cost in time. A polymerase that checks each new building block slows down, while one that checks less often copies faster and makes more errors. Cells balance speed and accuracy according to the job, using proofreading in places where mistakes would be costly.

Assembly machines also face the problem of order. Subunits must join in the right sequence, and each join must be stable before the next is added. Chaperones and other helpers often guide this sequence, so that the structure grows correctly and does not collapse into an unusable mass.

Vale and Milligan (2000) noted that filaments in the cell are built and dismantled constantly, and the machines that move along them depend on this dynamic structure. The copying of the cytoskeleton is therefore part of the same system of machines that moves cargo.

Students should see copying and assembly as the creative side of the machine world. They turn stored information and raw building blocks into new working structures, and the cell depends on them every time it grows or divides.

A cell that copies its DNA must do so with great care, because a single unrepaired error can be passed to every later copy. For this reason the copying machines are often paired with repair machines that inspect the new strand and remove mistakes, a partnership that keeps the genetic record intact across many generations.

To summarize this chapter, copying machines move along templates and add building blocks one at a time, while assembly machines join subunits into structures. Their work depends on accurate, step-by-step shape changes. The next chapter describes how protein machines are studied.
