An inhibitor is a molecule that slows or stops an enzyme. Inhibitors are very important in cells, where they help control metabolism, and they are widely used as drugs. Inhibition comes in several forms, depending on where the inhibitor binds and how it affects the enzyme. Some inhibitors compete with the substrate for the active site, while others bind elsewhere and change the enzyme's shape.

Competitive inhibitors resemble the substrate and bind to the active site. Because they occupy the site, they prevent the substrate from binding. The effect can be overcome by adding more substrate, which outcompetes the inhibitor. Many drugs work in this way, blocking an enzyme by occupying its active site with a molecule that binds more tightly than the natural substrate.

Noncompetitive inhibitors bind to a different site on the enzyme. Their binding changes the shape of the enzyme, which reduces its activity even if the substrate can still bind. Because the inhibitor does not compete with the substrate, adding more substrate does not fully overcome the inhibition. Allosteric inhibitors, which bind at a distant site and change the active site, are a common example.

Koshland (1958) recognized that enzymes can respond to molecules that bind away from the active site, and his work on induced fit helped explain how such binding can affect the shape and function of the enzyme. Changes in shape are therefore one of the main ways that inhibitors act.

Benkovic and Hammes-Schiffer (2003) discussed how inhibitors interfere with the energy landscape of a reaction. An inhibitor that stabilizes the substrate or blocks the transition state raises the energy barrier, which slows the reaction. The mechanism depends on the chemistry of the inhibitor and of the active site.

Pauling et al. (1951) showed that the protein fold can be altered by changes in side chains, and such changes can affect the shape of the active site. An inhibitor that binds to the fold can therefore change the geometry needed for catalysis.

Inhibition is an important part of cell control. A pathway may be switched off when its product accumulates, because the product acts as an inhibitor of an early enzyme in the pathway. This feedback inhibition prevents the cell from making more product than it needs, and it keeps the chemistry in balance.

Students should understand that inhibition is reversible in many cases. When the inhibitor is removed, the enzyme recovers its activity. Some inhibitors bind permanently, however, and they are used as poisons or as drugs that must be carefully controlled. The strength and reversibility of inhibition are important features to consider when studying an enzyme.

A useful picture is a turnstile at a station. A competitive inhibitor is like someone standing in the entry, blocking the passage until they move. A noncompetitive inhibitor is like a bolt that jams the turnstile from the side, so that it cannot turn even when people are waiting.

Many medicines are inhibitors. A drug that blocks an enzyme needed by a bacterium or a virus can stop it from growing, and drugs that block enzymes involved in inflammation can reduce pain. Designing such drugs depends on knowing the shape of the enzyme's active site, which is why structural information is so valuable in medicine.

Some natural poisons are also inhibitors. Certain compounds bind so tightly to an enzyme that it cannot work at all, and small amounts can be dangerous. Understanding how inhibitors act helps scientists both to treat poisoning and to design safer treatments.

To summarize this chapter, inhibitors slow enzymes by competing for the active site or by binding elsewhere and changing the enzyme's shape. Inhibition is an important means of control in cells and a basis for many drugs. The next chapter describes how cells control enzymes over longer periods.
