The rate of an enzyme-catalyzed reaction depends on how much substrate is present. At low substrate concentrations, the rate rises roughly in proportion to the amount of substrate, because most enzyme molecules are free to bind the substrate. As the substrate concentration increases, the enzyme becomes busy, and the rate levels off. At very high concentrations, the enzyme is saturated, meaning that all its active sites are occupied and the rate reaches its maximum.

This pattern is described by a relationship between the rate and the substrate concentration. The relationship has two key values. The maximum rate is the speed the enzyme reaches when it is saturated. The Michaelis constant is the substrate concentration at which the rate is half the maximum. A low Michaelis constant means that the enzyme binds its substrate tightly, while a high value means that binding is weaker.

Benkovic and Hammes-Schiffer (2003) discussed how these rate parameters reflect the energy landscape of the reaction. The binding of substrate and the chemistry of the active site both contribute to the observed rate, and the parameters summarize the balance between these steps.

Koshland (1958) recognized that the rate of an enzyme depends not only on its chemistry but also on its shape changes during binding. The induced fit of the enzyme influences how fast it can bind and release its substrate, and this influences the observed rate.

The concept of saturation is important for understanding cells. A cell cannot make an enzyme work faster than its maximum, so the amount of enzyme and the availability of substrate together set the rate of a pathway. A change in either can change the flow of material through the pathway, which is one of the ways cells control their metabolism.

Chothia (1992) emphasized that enzymes in a family often differ in their maximum rates and their affinities for substrates. These differences allow family members to work in different parts of the cell and under different conditions. The variety of rate parameters is part of the diversity of enzymes.

Pauling et al. (1951) described the protein structure that determines where the substrate can bind, and this structure influences the affinity and therefore the Michaelis constant. The fold of the enzyme sets the framework on which these parameters depend.

Students should note that the rate parameters depend on conditions. Temperature, pH and the presence of inhibitors all change the rate, and enzymes are usually measured under controlled conditions so that comparisons are meaningful. Understanding the conditions is essential for interpreting measurements of enzyme activity.

A useful picture is a team of workers at a set of stations. When few items arrive, each worker is busy only part of the time, and the output rises as more items arrive. When all the workers are busy, adding more items does not speed up the line, and the output reaches its limit. The enzyme works like that team, with saturation as the point where every station is occupied.

A simple experiment shows the idea. A scientist measures the speed of a reaction at several substrate concentrations, plots the results and sees a curve that bends toward a flat plateau. The shape of the curve reveals whether the enzyme is limited by the substrate, by the number of enzyme molecules or by the speed of its own chemistry. Reading these curves is one of the basic skills of enzyme research.

The maximum rate also depends on how many enzyme molecules are present. Doubling the amount of enzyme doubles the maximum rate, while the Michaelis constant stays the same, because it reflects the binding of one substrate to one active site.

To summarize this chapter, the rate of an enzyme rises with substrate concentration and levels off at saturation, with the maximum rate and the Michaelis constant describing the behavior. These parameters reflect binding and chemistry, and they help cells regulate their metabolism. The next chapter introduces inhibition, which slows enzymes by blocking their active sites or changing their shape.
