A catalyst is a substance that speeds up a chemical reaction without being consumed by it. An enzyme is a biological catalyst, usually a protein, and it works by providing a different path for the reaction. The path has a lower energy barrier, so more molecules have enough energy to react at any given moment. The enzyme itself returns to its original state after each reaction and can be used again.

The effect of an enzyme can be very large. Some enzymes increase the rate of a reaction by many millions of times compared with the same reaction in water alone. This speed is what allows cells to carry out their chemistry at the pace of life. Without enzymes, the reactions of metabolism would be too slow to keep up with the needs of an organism.

Enzymes do not change the outcome of a reaction. They do not make a reaction happen if it would not happen otherwise, and they do not change the balance between starting materials and products. They only change how fast the reaction reaches its balance. This is an important point, because it means that enzymes can be used in both directions, depending on the conditions.

Pauling et al. (1951) described the regular folds of proteins, which create the cavities where enzymes perform their chemistry. The folded protein provides a shaped environment, and the arrangement of residues inside it forms the workplace of the enzyme. Without a stable fold, the enzyme would lose the shape it needs.

Koshland (1958) later showed that an enzyme does more than hold a reactant: its shape changes as the reactant arrives, so the catalytic groups are placed correctly. Benkovic and Hammes-Schiffer (2003) explained that this helps the enzyme speed up the reaction in a controlled way, rather than simply increasing the number of collisions between molecules.

Students often ask how an enzyme can be used again and again. The answer is that the enzyme binds the reactant, carries out the change and then releases the product. The enzyme is not changed by the chemistry, so it is ready for the next molecule. A single enzyme molecule can process many thousands of substrate molecules each second, depending on the enzyme.

Enzymes are also highly specific. Each enzyme usually acts on one substrate or a small family of substrates. This specificity keeps the many reactions of the cell separate, so that one enzyme does not interfere with another. The specificity comes from the shape of the active site, which is the subject of the next chapter.

A helpful picture is a skilled craftsperson with a special tool. The tool does not make the object itself, but it lets the craftsperson shape the material quickly and repeatedly. The enzyme is like that tool, bringing reactants together in the right way and then being ready for the next job.

A common mistake is to think that an enzyme is used up in the process, like a fuel. It is not. The enzyme may be shaped and reshaped many times, but at the end of each cycle it is the same molecule it was at the start. This is why a small amount of enzyme can change a large amount of material over time, and why cells need only modest quantities of most enzymes.

To summarize this chapter, an enzyme is a biological catalyst that speeds a reaction by lowering its energy barrier. It is not consumed, it does not change the balance of the reaction, and it is usually specific for one kind of substrate. The next chapter describes the active site, where this work takes place.
