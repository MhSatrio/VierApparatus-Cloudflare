Enzymes are highly specific. Each enzyme usually acts on one substrate or on a small family of closely related substrates, and it ignores the many other molecules in the cell. This specificity keeps the reactions of the cell orderly and prevents unwanted chemistry from occurring. Specificity is determined by the shape and chemistry of the active site, and by the way the substrate fits.

The simplest model of specificity is the lock and key. The substrate, the key, fits the active site, the lock, and only a matching key can open the lock. This model captures the essential idea that shape matters, but it is too rigid, because enzymes can change their shape to accommodate a substrate. The induced fit model adds this flexibility.

Koshland (1958) developed the induced fit model, which explained how enzymes can be both specific and adaptable. The active site changes shape to fit the substrate, and this change helps to exclude molecules that would not fit well. The enzyme therefore recognizes its substrate by a combination of shape, chemistry and dynamic adjustment.

Benkovic and Hammes-Schiffer (2003) pointed out that specificity is also a matter of chemistry. The side chains of the active site must hold the substrate with the correct groups and must avoid binding molecules that do not react. The balance of these interactions determines which molecules are accepted and which are rejected.

Chothia (1992) noted that enzymes in the same family often share a common fold but have different substrate specificities. Small changes in the residues of the active site can alter which substrate fits best, allowing the family to evolve new reactions while keeping a stable framework. This flexibility is one of the reasons that enzymes are so diverse.

Specificity is not absolute. Some enzymes act on more than one substrate, and some react with molecules that are similar to their normal targets. Such promiscuity can be useful in some cases, but it can also cause problems if the wrong reaction occurs. Cells control the activity of enzymes in ways that limit unwanted reactions.

Pauling et al. (1951) described the regular structures that position side chains in space, and this positioning helps to determine which substrates can be bound. The geometry of the fold sets the framework on which specificity is built.

Students should understand that specificity has a practical side. Drugs that target enzymes must be designed to fit their active sites, and many medicines work by blocking one specific enzyme. The principle of specificity therefore underlies both the normal function of the cell and the design of treatments.

A useful picture is a set of keys cut for different locks. Each key has a unique shape, and it opens only the lock it was made for. Enzymes are like locks with keys cut to fit, and the fit is refined by the enzyme's ability to adjust.

Specificity can also be seen in the stereochemistry of substrates. Many molecules exist in two mirror-image forms, and enzymes usually act on only one of them. The active site is shaped in three dimensions, so it can tell left from right. This is why cells make and use one form of a sugar or an amino acid, even though the other form has the same chemical formula.

Specificity also protects the cell from waste. If an enzyme acted on every molecule that resembled its target, it would generate unwanted products and consume resources. The narrow fit of the active site is a form of quality control, ensuring that each reaction has the right partners.

To summarize this chapter, enzymes are specific because their active sites match the shape and chemistry of particular substrates. The induced fit model explains how the enzyme can adjust to bind its target, and specificity is essential for the orderly chemistry of the cell.
