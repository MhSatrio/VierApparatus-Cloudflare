Every chemical reaction has an energy barrier, a hump that the reacting molecules must climb before they can form products. The height of this barrier determines how fast the reaction goes. Enzymes lower the barrier, which means that more molecules have enough energy to cross it at any given moment, and the reaction speeds up. Lowering the barrier is the central way that enzymes accelerate reactions.

Enzymes lower the barrier in several ways. They can hold reactants close together and in the correct orientation, which makes the reaction more likely. They can stabilize the transition state, the unstable arrangement of atoms at the top of the barrier, by providing groups that interact with it. They can donate or accept protons, which often speeds the reaction. Many enzymes use more than one of these strategies at once.

Benkovic and Hammes-Schiffer (2003) reviewed these strategies and explained that enzymes use the forces of their active sites to lower the energy of the transition state. The active site is not simply a place where reactants meet; it is a carefully designed environment that makes the difficult step of the reaction easier.

Pauling et al. (1951) showed that the geometry of the protein determines where the reactive groups can be placed. The folded chain positions its side chains in precise directions, and the enzyme uses this precision to align the groups that take part in the reaction.

Koshland (1958) suggested that the induced fit of the enzyme helps it lower the barrier, because the shape change can bring catalytic groups into the exact arrangement required. The enzyme does not simply hold a fixed shape; it adjusts to the substrate in a way that favors the reaction.

Enzymes do not lower the barrier by changing the energy of the starting materials and products. They change the energy of the transition state, which is the step that controls the speed. This is why an enzyme can accelerate a reaction without altering its overall balance, and it is why enzymes can work in both directions.

Students should keep in mind that the barrier depends on the conditions. Temperature, pH and the presence of other molecules all influence how easily the barrier is crossed. Enzymes are adapted to the conditions of their cells, and they work best within a narrow range. Outside that range, their shape and their chemistry may change, and their speed falls.

A helpful picture is a mountain pass. Without a pass, a traveler must climb over the mountain, which takes great effort. A pass through the mountain lets the traveler cross with much less effort. Enzymes make a pass through the energy landscape of a reaction, so that the crossing becomes much easier.

An everyday comparison helps. A reaction can be imagined as a ball that must roll over a ridge before it reaches a valley on the other side. The enzyme does not change the height of the valley where the ball starts or ends. It cuts a lower gap in the ridge, so that many more balls cross each second, and the total traffic over the ridge rises sharply.

Energy barriers also explain why heat alone is not the answer for cells. Raising the temperature would speed many reactions, but it would also damage proteins and membranes. Enzymes allow reactions to proceed quickly at the mild temperatures that living cells can tolerate.

To summarize this chapter, enzymes lower the energy barrier of reactions by holding reactants in position, stabilizing the transition state and providing groups that help the reaction proceed. The shape of the active site and the induced fit of the enzyme both contribute. The next chapter describes how enzymes distinguish their substrates.
