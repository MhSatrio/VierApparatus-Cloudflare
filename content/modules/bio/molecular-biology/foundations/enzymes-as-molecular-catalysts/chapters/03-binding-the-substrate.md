Before an enzyme can act, the substrate must bind to the active site. Binding is an interaction between the shapes and chemistries of the two molecules. The substrate is held by a combination of hydrogen bonds, electrostatic attractions, hydrophobic contacts and sometimes temporary covalent links. Together these interactions hold the substrate long enough for the reaction to take place.

The strength of binding is an important feature of an enzyme. An enzyme that binds its substrate too weakly will rarely catch it, while one that binds too strongly may hold the product too long and block the next reaction. Enzymes are therefore designed to bind their substrates with a balance of strength and release. The balance is often adjusted by the surrounding conditions.

Pauling et al. (1951) described the regular structures of the protein backbone, which place side chains in predictable positions. The side chains of an active site are arranged in these positions, so that they can reach the substrate from the correct directions. The regularity of the fold gives the enzyme a reliable framework for binding.

Benkovic and Hammes-Schiffer (2003) described the importance of the binding step in catalysis. The enzyme must bring the substrate into a position where the reactive groups can meet, and the binding energy helps to achieve this. Some of the energy released when the substrate binds is used to lower the barrier to reaction, which is one of the ways enzymes accelerate reactions.

Koshland (1958) emphasized that binding is accompanied by a change in the enzyme's shape. When the substrate fits, the active site closes around it, bringing the catalytic groups into the correct arrangement. This closing motion is part of the catalytic cycle, and it helps to exclude water from the active site at the moment of reaction.

Substrates can also be held in several ways at once. A substrate may form hydrogen bonds with polar side chains, be held by salt bridges with charged residues and be nestled against hydrophobic residues in the pocket. The combination of these interactions gives the enzyme both specificity and strength.

Students should note that the binding of a substrate is reversible. Once the reaction has taken place, the product is released and the enzyme is free to bind another substrate. The reversibility allows the enzyme to turn over repeatedly, and it is one of the reasons that enzymes can process large numbers of molecules.

A useful picture is a glove that fits a hand. The glove shape matches the hand, and when the hand is inserted, the glove adjusts slightly to hold it firmly. Binding works in a similar way, with the active site shaped to hold its substrate while the reaction takes place.

Binding can also be studied in the laboratory by measuring how tightly a substrate or inhibitor attaches to an enzyme. Scientists add known amounts of the molecule and measure how much of it becomes bound, which gives a number for the strength of the interaction. Such measurements help explain why one substrate is processed faster than another, even when both fit the active site.

Some enzymes bind their substrates in an ordered sequence. One substrate attaches first, followed by the second, and the reaction does not start until both are in place. This ordering protects the enzyme from unwanted reactions, and it ensures that the right partners meet at the right time.

To summarize this chapter, substrates bind to the active site through several kinds of interaction, and the binding can change the shape of the enzyme to bring catalytic groups into position. Binding is reversible, which allows the enzyme to process many molecules. The next chapter explains how enzymes lower the energy needed for reactions.
