The active site is the part of an enzyme where the reaction takes place. It is a pocket or groove on the surface of the folded protein, lined with amino acid side chains that can bind the substrate and take part in the chemistry. The active site is usually a small fraction of the whole enzyme, but it contains everything that is needed for catalysis.

The shape of the active site is determined by the fold of the protein. Residues that are far apart in the sequence can come together in space to form the pocket. The pocket must be shaped so that the substrate fits well, and the side chains must be arranged so that they can hold the substrate in the right orientation for the reaction.

Koshland (1958) proposed that the active site is not a rigid lock but a flexible one, which changes shape slightly when the substrate binds. This idea, called induced fit, explained how the enzyme can wrap around the substrate and position its reactive groups. The shape of the active site is therefore dynamic as well as fixed.

Benkovic and Hammes-Schiffer (2003) discussed how the active site uses the properties of its side chains. Some side chains provide acidic or basic groups that can donate or accept protons. Others provide hydrophobic surfaces that hold nonpolar parts of the substrate. The combination of these groups creates a chemical environment suited to the reaction.

Chothia (1992) noted that active sites are often conserved within families of proteins, even when the rest of the sequence varies. The conserved residues are the ones that make the active site work, and their preservation shows that the chemistry of catalysis is a stable feature of the family.

The active site also excludes water in many cases. Water can compete with the substrate for binding and can interfere with the reaction, so many enzymes form a pocket that is shielded from the surrounding solvent. The pocket is therefore a special microenvironment, different from the bulk fluid of the cell.

Students should understand that the active site is a region of the folded protein, not a separate object. Its location is set by the fold, and changes in the fold can move or distort the active site. A mutation that changes the shape of the fold can therefore destroy the enzyme's activity, even if the mutated residue is not itself in the active site.

A useful picture is a workbench designed for a particular task. The bench has a surface, a set of clamps and tools arranged around the work area, and the arrangement is chosen so that the job can be done efficiently. The active site is the workbench of the enzyme, with its clamps and tools positioned for the reaction.

Active sites differ in size. A small pocket may bind a single small molecule, while a large groove may bind a long chain of a polymer and act on one end at a time. The size and depth of the pocket decide which molecules can enter and how far they are held, which is one of the main ways that enzymes with related folds differ from one another.

Many active sites contain a metal ion or a cofactor, a small non-protein helper that takes part in the chemistry. The cofactor is held in place by the side chains of the pocket, and it may carry electrons or groups that the substrate needs. The presence of a cofactor shows that the active site is a cooperative arrangement of protein and helper molecules.

To summarize this chapter, the active site is a pocket on the enzyme where the substrate binds and the reaction occurs. Its shape is set by the fold, it can change slightly during binding, and its side chains provide the chemistry. The next chapter describes how substrates bind to the active site.
