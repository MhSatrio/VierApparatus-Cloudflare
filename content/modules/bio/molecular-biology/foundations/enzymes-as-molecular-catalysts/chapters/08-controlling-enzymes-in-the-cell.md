Cells control their enzymes in many ways. They can change how much enzyme is made, how long it lasts, where it is located and how active it is at any moment. These controls allow the cell to respond to changing needs, to switch pathways on and off and to keep its chemistry in balance. The control of enzymes is one of the most important features of life.

The amount of an enzyme is set by the rate at which its gene is transcribed and its protein is made, and by the rate at which the protein is broken down. Cells can increase the amount of an enzyme when its product is needed and reduce it when the product is in excess. This kind of control is slow, taking hours or days, but it is important for long-term adjustments.

Activity can be changed much faster. Enzymes can be switched on or off by chemical modifications, such as the addition of phosphate groups, as well as by binding of activators and inhibitors. Such changes take seconds or minutes, and they allow the cell to respond quickly to signals.

Benkovic and Hammes-Schiffer (2003) emphasized that enzymes are dynamic molecules whose activity depends on their environment. The same enzyme may work at different rates in different parts of the cell, and its activity can be adjusted by changes in conditions. This dynamic character is part of what makes enzymes so useful as regulators.

Koshland (1958) showed that the shape of an enzyme can change in response to binding, and that such changes can be used to control activity. Allosteric regulation depends on this principle, with molecules binding to one site and changing the shape of another. The idea remains central to our understanding of enzyme control.

Chothia (1992) pointed out that many enzyme families include regulatory sites that are separate from the active site. These sites are often conserved, reflecting their importance in controlling the enzyme. The presence of regulatory sites within a family suggests that control has been a stable feature of enzyme evolution.

Location matters as well. An enzyme may be confined to a particular compartment, such as the mitochondria or the nucleus, where it meets its substrates. Moving an enzyme or its substrate can change the rate of a pathway without changing the enzyme itself.

Pauling et al. (1951) described the regular structures of proteins, and these structures provide the framework on which regulatory sites and active sites are built. The control of enzymes depends on the fold, which allows regulatory changes to be transmitted to the active site.

Students should understand that enzyme control is a layered system. Amount, activity and location can each be adjusted, and the combined effect determines the flow of material through a pathway. Understanding these layers helps explain how cells remain stable while responding to change.

A useful picture is a city with many traffic controls. Some are slow, such as building new roads, while others are fast, such as traffic lights. Cells use both kinds of control, adding enzymes slowly and switching their activity quickly, so that the flow of traffic follows the needs of the city.

Consider a pathway that makes an amino acid. When the amino acid accumulates, it can bind to an early enzyme in the pathway and slow it down, so that less intermediate is produced. When the amino acid is used up, the inhibition is released and the pathway speeds up again. This kind of feedback keeps the output close to what the cell needs, without wasting materials.

To summarize this chapter, cells control enzymes by changing their amount, their activity and their location, using both slow and fast mechanisms. Enzymes are dynamic molecules whose shape and environment can be adjusted. This completes the module on enzymes, and the next module describes proteins that work as molecular machines.
