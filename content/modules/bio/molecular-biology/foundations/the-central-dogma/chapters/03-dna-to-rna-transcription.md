Transcription is the first step of the central dogma. It is the process in which a gene is copied from DNA into RNA. The enzyme that carries it out is RNA polymerase, which reads one strand of the DNA and builds a complementary RNA strand, adding one ribonucleotide at a time. The RNA produced has the same sequence as the coding strand of the gene, with uracil in place of thymine. The copy is then used for the next step, which is translation into protein.

A natural question for beginners is why the cell makes a copy in RNA at all. Why not make a second DNA copy and use it directly? The answer lies in the properties of the two molecules, as described in earlier chapters. DNA is stable and carefully protected, and it is kept in the nucleus, where it serves as the master record. An RNA copy can be made without disturbing the master record, can be sent out of the nucleus and can be broken down when it is no longer needed. The short life of RNA is an advantage, because the cell can switch off a message quickly.

Transcription also keeps the master record safe. Because the cell only makes RNA copies of the genes it needs, the DNA itself is not altered each time a protein is made. This separation protects the genome from the wear that would come with constant copying, and it allows the cell to use the same DNA to produce many different kinds of RNA in different cell types. A liver cell and a nerve cell carry the same DNA, but they transcribe different sets of genes.

The enzyme that performs transcription is RNA polymerase. Eddy (2001) noted that many of the RNA molecules made by this enzyme have functions beyond providing a message for protein. The enzyme is therefore the gateway to a large family of RNAs, not only messengers. In eukaryotes there are several RNA polymerases, each making a different class of RNA, while bacteria use a single polymerase for all their RNA.

The process begins when the enzyme binds to a promoter, a region of DNA that signals where transcription should start. The enzyme then opens a short stretch of the double helix, reads the template strand and begins adding ribonucleotides that pair with the template bases. The chain grows in the 5' to 3' direction, and the enzyme moves along the gene until it reaches a stop signal. The result is a single-stranded RNA molecule that matches the gene.

In eukaryotes, the RNA that is first made is often processed before it leaves the nucleus. The processing can add a cap, a poly-A tail and remove introns, as described in the earlier RNA module. Mattick and Makunin (2006) emphasized that this processing is part of the information flow, because the final message depends on which pieces are kept. The central dogma's first arrow therefore includes a series of steps that shape the message.

It is useful to compare transcription with copying a document by hand. The scribe reads the original, writes a new version line by line, and checks each line against the original. The new version is not the original, and it is written in a different medium, but it carries the same content. In the same way, the RNA produced by transcription carries the same sequence as the gene, written in the RNA alphabet.

Transcription is also a point of regulation. The cell decides which genes to transcribe, and how often. Many proteins bind to DNA near a gene and either help or block the polymerase. Because this control happens at the start of the flow, it affects every later step. A gene that is not transcribed cannot produce a protein, no matter how the later steps are arranged.

Errors can occur during transcription, though they are rarer than errors during DNA copying. The enzyme can add the wrong nucleotide, and it can sometimes stop or slip. Proofreading mechanisms in the enzyme and associated factors reduce these errors, and the RNA is also short-lived, so a rare mistake usually has limited consequences. This is one more reason that transcription is a good way to store temporary information, rather than a way to change the master record.

Transcription is therefore a central part of the central dogma. It converts a stable, protected record into a flexible, short-lived copy that can be used, controlled and removed. The next chapter describes the second step, translation, in which the RNA copy is read by the ribosome to build a protein.

The ENCODE Project Consortium (2012) showed how widely transcription occurs. Their survey found that many more regions of the genome are transcribed than are needed to make the known proteins, which means that the step from DNA to RNA is far busier than the simple picture suggests. Some of the transcripts are short, some are long, and many are not translated at all. This broad activity gives the cell a large repertoire of RNA molecules to choose from, and it means that transcription is best understood as a general activity of the genome rather than as a rare event attached to a few genes.

Transcription also depends on the state of the chromatin around a gene. A gene packed tightly into nucleosomes is harder for the polymerase to reach, while a gene in an open region is more accessible. This means that the DNA sequence is only part of the story. The packaging of the genome, which was described in the DNA module, sets the stage on which transcription takes place, and the cell can change that packaging to switch genes on or off.

To summarize, transcription copies DNA into RNA using RNA polymerase, keeps the master record safe and allows the cell to control which genes are used. The RNA copy is then processed and sent on to the next step. The next chapter explains how the message is translated into the amino acid sequence of a protein.
