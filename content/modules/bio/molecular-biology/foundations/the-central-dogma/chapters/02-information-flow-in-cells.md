Think of a cell as a busy workshop. Some of its tasks are done by machines that make new parts, some are done by workers who assemble the parts into finished products, and some are done by the plans that tell everyone what to make. The central dogma describes how the plans reach the workers. In the simplest version, the plan is written in DNA, a copy of the relevant instructions is made in RNA, and the RNA is used to build a protein. This chapter follows that flow as a whole, before the later chapters examine each step in detail.

The three transfers of sequence information can be listed in order. The first is from DNA to RNA, a process called transcription. The second is from RNA to protein, called translation. The third, which is the one that Crick (1970) described as not occurring in normal cells, is from protein back to nucleic acid. Each transfer involves a different enzyme or machine, and each uses the chemistry of the previous chapters in a different way.

The phrase "sequence information" is important. It means the order of the units in a molecule, such as the order of bases in DNA or of amino acids in a protein. Sequence is what carries the detailed instructions. A protein's shape and function depend on the order of its amino acids, and that order is specified by the order of codons in a messenger RNA, which in turn is copied from the order of bases in a gene. The central dogma is therefore a statement about how one sequence determines another.

The direction of the flow is what makes the dogma special. In normal cells, information moves from nucleic acid to protein and from DNA to RNA, but not from protein back to DNA or RNA in a way that changes the sequence. A protein cannot be used as a template to write a new gene. This one-way rule has an important consequence. The cell's stored instructions are protected from the day-to-day activities of its proteins, which can be damaged, altered or destroyed without rewriting the DNA.

Mattick and Makunin (2006) describe how the flow of information in the cell is more complicated than the simple picture suggests. Many RNAs carry information that is not used to make proteins, and some of them act directly to regulate other genes. The flow therefore includes steps that do not end in a protein. The central dogma's basic direction still holds, but the number of routes has grown.

It is useful to picture the central dogma as a set of arrows: DNA points to RNA, and RNA points to protein. Each arrow represents a process that uses a template and a set of enzymes or machines. The arrows are one-way in the normal case, and each arrow can be controlled by the cell. Many diseases involve a break in one of the arrows, for example when a mutation changes the DNA sequence or when a protein fails to fold correctly.

Eddy (2001) emphasized that the RNA step is where much of the regulation happens. Because transcription and processing can be controlled, the cell can decide which RNAs to make and how long they last. The chapters that follow describe these controls in detail. For now, it is enough to see that the flow of information is not automatic. It is managed at each step.

The flow also has a practical meaning in the laboratory. When a scientist measures the amount of a protein, they are measuring the end of a long chain of events. The amount depends on how much RNA was made, how stable that RNA is, how efficiently it is translated and how long the protein itself survives. A change at any step can change the final result, so the central dogma gives researchers a checklist of places to look.

Beginners often wonder whether the flow ever runs backward. In the strict sense, the answer for normal protein synthesis is no, because the cell does not use protein sequences to write DNA. However, some viruses have RNA genomes and use enzymes to copy RNA into DNA, and this reverse flow is an important exception. The fifth chapter of this module describes it. The exception does not overturn the central dogma. It shows the importance of the direction that the dogma describes.

The flow also has a time dimension. Transcription can happen in minutes, translation can happen in seconds per protein, and a protein can persist for hours or days. The stability of each molecule along the route therefore determines how much information is used and when. A cell that needs a rapid response can make and break messages quickly, while a cell that needs stable traits can keep its proteins for a long time.

The flow of information also differs between cell types and between stages of life. A developing embryo uses different sets of messages at different times, and a mature cell may keep a stable pattern of gene activity for years. Each pattern is a version of the same flow, running through different genes and producing different proteins. This variety is one reason that the dogma is useful for comparing cells: the same map can be used to describe a nerve cell and a skin cell, while showing which parts of the map are active in each.

Information also flows between cells, although the central dogma describes only the flow within one cell. Signaling molecules can carry messages from one cell to another, and those messages can change the pattern of gene activity in the receiving cell. The dogma does not describe these signals directly, but it explains how they are interpreted. A signal changes which genes are transcribed, and the rest of the flow then follows the usual route. The layers of communication are therefore nested, with the central dogma at the heart of each cell's response.

To summarize the flow, information moves from DNA to RNA by transcription, and from RNA to protein by translation, while the reverse transfer from protein to nucleic acid does not occur in the normal case. Each step is a point of control. The next chapter examines transcription in more detail, and explains why the copy it makes is an RNA rather than a second DNA.
