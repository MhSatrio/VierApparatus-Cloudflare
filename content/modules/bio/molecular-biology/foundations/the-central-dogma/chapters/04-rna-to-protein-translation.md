Translation is the second step of the central dogma. It is the process in which the ribosome reads a messenger RNA and assembles a chain of amino acids, which then folds into a protein. The message is written in an alphabet of four bases, while the protein is written in an alphabet of twenty amino acids. Translation is the conversion between these two alphabets, and it is carried out by a large machine made of ribosomal RNA and many proteins, aided by transfer RNAs that act as adaptors.

The message is read in groups of three bases, called codons. Because there are four bases and three positions in each codon, there are sixty-four possible codons. Sixty-one of them specify an amino acid, and three act as stop signals that end the chain. Several different codons can specify the same amino acid, which is called the redundancy of the genetic code. This means that the code is robust to some changes in the message. A change in the third base of a codon often leaves the amino acid unchanged.

The reading frame matters. The ribosome must start reading at the correct position, or every codon after the error will be read in the wrong groups. The start of a protein-coding message is marked by a start codon, usually AUG, which also codes for methionine. From that point, the ribosome reads the message three bases at a time until it meets a stop codon. A change that shifts the reading frame can therefore alter the whole protein that follows.

The ribosome has three sites for transfer RNAs, usually named A, P and E. A transfer RNA carrying the next amino acid enters the A site, where its anticodon is checked against the codon of the message. When the match is correct, the amino acid is transferred to the growing chain, and the ribosome moves forward by one codon. The empty transfer RNA then leaves through the E site. This cycle is repeated for each amino acid until the stop codon is reached.

Eddy (2001) pointed out that the ribosome is one of the clearest examples of an RNA-based machine. The ribosomal RNA forms the active site where the peptide bond is made, and the proteins of the ribosome support and stabilize that core. Translation therefore shows that the second step of the central dogma depends on RNA as much as on protein. The message is read by an RNA adaptor and processed by an RNA catalyst.

Mattick and Makunin (2006) noted that the translation machinery is itself made from RNA molecules that are encoded in the genome. This means that the cell uses the first two steps of the central dogma to build the machinery needed for the second step. The loop is a reminder that the flow of information is not a straight line in every sense, since the products of one step are needed to carry out the next.

It is useful to compare translation with reading a recipe. The recipe card is the messenger RNA, each line is a codon, and the cook is the ribosome. The cook reads the next instruction, fetches the ingredient named in that instruction, adds it to the dish and moves on to the next line. The ingredients arrive in the kitchen carried by helpers, which are the transfer RNAs. The finished dish is the protein, which must then be shaped and finished before it can be used.

Translation is an energy-consuming process. Each amino acid added to the chain requires the hydrolysis of energy-rich molecules, and the process as a whole can use a large share of the cell's energy when it is running at full speed. The cell therefore coordinates the rate of translation with its needs and with its supply of energy and building blocks. A cell that is starved of amino acids will slow down translation quickly, which is one of the ways the cell responds to stress.

Many translation errors are prevented by checks in the system. The synthetases that attach amino acids to transfer RNAs must choose the correct pair, and the ribosome checks the codon-anticodon match before adding each amino acid. These checks do not make the process perfect, but they keep errors rare enough that most proteins are built correctly. When mistakes do happen, the cell may degrade the faulty protein.

The result of translation is a chain of amino acids, which then folds into a three-dimensional shape. The chain must fold correctly to function, and many other proteins help this folding happen. The next chapters describe what can go wrong in the second step, and how the central dogma accounts for some exceptions to the usual flow.

The ENCODE Project Consortium (2012) also highlighted that the machinery of translation is among the most heavily transcribed parts of the genome, reflecting how much protein a growing cell must make. The genes for ribosomal RNA and for many ribosomal and transfer RNA components are used at very high levels in rapidly dividing cells. The cell's investment in translation is therefore visible in its transcription, and the two steps of the central dogma are linked by the economics of the cell.

Translation also needs to end cleanly. When the ribosome reaches a stop codon, release factors recognize the signal, the finished protein is set free and the ribosome subunits are separated so that they can start again. Without this release step, the ribosome would remain attached to the message, and the cell would run out of free ribosomes. The end of translation is thus as important as its beginning, and it completes the second step of the central dogma.

To summarize, translation reads an RNA message in groups of three bases, brings the correct amino acid for each codon with a transfer RNA, links the amino acids into a chain and stops at a stop codon. The ribosome is an RNA-based machine, and the process depends on accuracy at several steps. The next chapter describes a famous exception to the flow of information from RNA back to DNA.
