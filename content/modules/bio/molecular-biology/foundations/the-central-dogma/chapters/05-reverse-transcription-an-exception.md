The central dogma says that information normally flows from DNA to RNA to protein, and not back. One of the most important exceptions to this pattern is reverse transcription, in which an RNA molecule is copied into DNA. The name describes the reversal: the usual transcription runs from DNA to RNA, and reverse transcription runs in the opposite direction. This process is essential to the life cycles of many viruses, and it is a central tool in laboratories around the world.

Reverse transcription is carried out by an enzyme called reverse transcriptase. This enzyme reads an RNA template and builds a complementary DNA strand, adding deoxyribonucleotides in the usual 5' to 3' direction. Because the enzyme uses an RNA template, the DNA it makes is complementary to the RNA. Often the enzyme then makes a second DNA strand, creating a double-stranded DNA copy that can be inserted into a host genome.

The best known natural examples come from retroviruses, a family that includes the virus that causes HIV. Retroviruses carry their genetic material as RNA. When such a virus infects a cell, it uses its reverse transcriptase to make a DNA copy of its RNA. The DNA copy can then enter the nucleus and become part of the host's chromosomes, where it can be transcribed and used to make new viral particles. This strategy allows the virus to hide its genome inside the host's own DNA.

Morange (2009) pointed out that the discovery of reverse transcription was an important test of the central dogma. When it was first described in the 1970s, some scientists were surprised, because the dogma had been understood to rule out copying RNA into DNA. The discovery did not overturn the idea. Instead, it showed that the dogma describes the normal flow in cells, while allowing for specific exceptions that carry their own enzymes.

Cells also contain their own elements that use reverse transcription. Many of the mobile genetic elements in eukaryotic genomes, called retrotransposons, move around the genome by making an RNA copy of themselves and then reverse transcribing that copy into DNA at a new site. The ENCODE Project Consortium (2012) reported that a large fraction of the human genome is made up of such elements. Much of our DNA is therefore the remnant of past reverse transcription events.

Reverse transcription also plays a role in the protection of chromosome ends. The enzyme telomerase carries its own RNA template and uses it to extend the ends of chromosomes, adding repeated DNA sequences. Telomerase is a special kind of reverse transcriptase, and its activity helps to maintain the length of chromosomes in some cells. This is one of the few normal, non-viral uses of the reverse flow in human biology.

In the laboratory, reverse transcription is a standard method. Scientists often want to study RNA, but RNA is less stable and harder to handle than DNA. By converting RNA into complementary DNA with reverse transcriptase, researchers can make stable copies that can be amplified and measured. This method is the first step in many experiments that measure gene activity, and it is used in medical tests to detect viral RNA.

The reverse transcription method has an important limitation that beginners should understand. The DNA copy is made from the RNA sequence, so it reflects the transcribed sequence, not the original gene. Introns that have been removed from the messenger RNA are absent from the copy. This is useful for studying messages, but it means that reverse transcription does not directly recover the full genomic sequence of a gene.

Mattick and Makunin (2006) discussed how RNA-based genetic elements can influence the genome in many ways, and reverse transcription is one of the mechanisms behind this influence. Their work points out that the boundaries between RNA and DNA are not fixed, and that the cell's genome has been shaped by many episodes of RNA copying over evolutionary time.

Students should keep the scale of the exception in mind. Reverse transcription is important, but it is not the normal route of information in most cells. The vast majority of genes in a human cell are transcribed from DNA to RNA and then translated to protein, without any reverse step. The exceptions teach us about viruses, about the history of genomes and about useful laboratory techniques, but they do not replace the central pattern.

The existence of reverse transcription also raised practical questions for medicine. Antiviral drugs that block reverse transcriptase are now an important class of treatment for retroviral infections. Because the enzyme is essential for the virus to make its DNA copy, a drug that blocks it can stop the infection from establishing itself in new cells. The design of such drugs depends on knowing how the enzyme reads its RNA template and how it differs from the cell's own DNA polymerases. This is a clear case where a basic discovery about the flow of information has led to a therapy.

Mattick and Makunin (2006) also noted that RNA copying can shape genomes over long periods. Repeated copying events have added many copies of mobile sequences to the genome, and the cell has evolved mechanisms to suppress or control these elements. The reverse flow of information is therefore not only a feature of viruses but also a force that has helped to build the genomes of many organisms.

Reverse transcription also shows the value of studying exceptions. Each exception has forced scientists to examine the assumptions behind the rule more carefully. In the case of reverse transcription, the discovery encouraged researchers to ask what else the cell might copy in reverse, and it led to the identification of the retrotransposons and telomerase described above. The exception has therefore enriched the picture of the genome, rather than weakening it.

To summarize, reverse transcription copies RNA into DNA using reverse transcriptase. It is the central step in the life cycles of retroviruses, it has shaped many mobile elements in genomes, it helps maintain chromosome ends through telomerase and it is a standard laboratory method.
