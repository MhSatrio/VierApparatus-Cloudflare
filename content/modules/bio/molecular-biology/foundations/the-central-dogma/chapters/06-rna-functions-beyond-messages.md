When the central dogma was first formulated, RNA was thought of mainly as a messenger, a short-lived copy of a gene that carried its instruction to the ribosome. That picture was useful, and it still describes one important role of RNA. But the messenger is only one of many RNA molecules in a cell, and many of the others do not carry any protein instructions at all. This chapter describes some of the main non-messenger roles and shows how they fit into the framework of the central dogma.

Transfer RNA is the most familiar example after messenger RNA. It does not carry a message to be read in the usual sense. Instead, it acts as an adaptor, pairing its anticodon with the codons of the message and carrying the correct amino acid. Transfer RNA is therefore a bridge between the RNA alphabet and the protein alphabet, and it is needed for the second step of the central dogma to work at all.

Ribosomal RNA is a second example. It forms the framework and the catalytic core of the ribosome, the machine that reads messages and builds proteins. The ribosome's RNA does not carry a message for protein. It carries out the chemistry of protein synthesis, and it does so with a structure that is more than half of the ribosome's mass. A central dogma that focused only on messages would miss this essential role.

Small regulatory RNAs form a third group. Molecules such as microRNAs guide protein complexes to messenger RNAs that carry matching sequences, and they can reduce the amount of protein made from those messages. These small RNAs do not themselves become proteins, but they control the flow of information from messages to protein. They therefore act as regulators of the central dogma's middle step, rather than as part of its main chain.

Mattick and Makunin (2006) argued that the number and variety of noncoding RNAs in the genome had been underestimated. Their review emphasized that many RNAs have structural or regulatory roles, and that these roles are important for the development and function of complex organisms. The central dogma remains a useful guide, but it must be read alongside the growing list of RNAs that work without becoming proteins.

Eddy (2001) described the wider view of RNA as a molecule with many functions, including catalysis and regulation, not only information transfer. He suggested that RNA may have played an even larger role in early life, before proteins took over many tasks. The modern cell retains traces of that history in its ribosome and in its many small RNAs, which carry out functions that were once central.

Long noncoding RNAs form a fourth group. These molecules, which are longer than two hundred bases, can influence chromatin, bind proteins and interact with other RNAs. Their functions are still being discovered, and many of them may act at the level of gene control rather than protein production. They illustrate that the flow of information in cells includes steps that do not end in a protein.

The existence of these RNAs raises a question about the central dogma. Does the dogma still describe the main flow of information? The answer is yes for the basic route, in which genes give rise to proteins through messengers. But the dogma does not describe the many RNAs that regulate or support that route. A fuller picture includes RNA as an active participant at many points, not only as a passive copy.

It may help to imagine a theatre production. The messenger RNA is the script that is read out to the audience, which corresponds to the protein. The transfer RNA and ribosome are the actors and stage crew who bring the script to life. The small regulatory RNAs are the directors who decide which scenes are performed. The long noncoding RNAs are the set designers, who shape the environment in which the performance happens. The production depends on all of them, not only on the script.

Students who meet RNA for the first time sometimes assume that anything called RNA must be a messenger. This is a common and understandable error, and it is worth correcting early. Many RNA molecules are structural, catalytic or regulatory, and they are as important as messages in the cell. Keeping this wider view in mind makes the central dogma easier to use, because it reminds us that the middle step of the flow is a busy place.

Crick (1970) included RNA in his description of the flow of information, but the way RNA is used in cells has become much richer than the early picture. The adaptor RNAs of translation, the catalytic RNA of the ribosome and the regulatory RNAs that control messages all show that RNA can be a machine, a template, a guide and a switch. The central dogma does not forbid any of these roles. It simply did not describe them in detail when it was first proposed.

Researchers today use this wider view to ask new questions. Does a particular RNA regulate a message, or does it simply reflect the activity of a nearby gene? Does a noncoding RNA stabilize a protein complex, or does it act as a decoy? Answering these questions requires careful experiments, and each answer adds to the picture of RNA as an active participant in the flow of information.

The practical consequence of this wider view is that a researcher cannot assume that an RNA is a message without checking. Many transcripts are measured in large studies, and their names alone do not reveal their roles. Careful work is needed to show whether an RNA is translated, whether it binds proteins, and whether its removal changes the activity of other genes. The central dogma provides the framework for these questions, but the answers come from experiments that test each RNA individually.

To summarize, RNA does far more than carry messages. Transfer RNA adapts codons to amino acids, ribosomal RNA forms and catalyzes the ribosome, small regulatory RNAs control messages, and long noncoding RNAs shape chromatin and other processes. These roles fit into a broader picture of the central dogma, in which RNA is an active participant rather than a simple copy. The next chapter shows how the dogma is used in modern research.
