Not all chromatin looks the same. Under the microscope, some regions of the nucleus appear dark and dense, while others appear pale and open. Scientists named these two states heterochromatin and euchromatin. The names come from Greek roots meaning "other colored" and "true colored", reflecting the way the two states took up stain. The distinction is one of the most important ideas in chromosome biology, because it describes how the cell decides which genes to use.

Heterochromatin is the tightly packed state. Its nucleosomes are closely spaced, and the chromatin fiber is folded into a compact form that is difficult for proteins to reach. Genes in heterochromatin tend to be silent, and many of these regions contain repeated sequences that are not used to make proteins. Heterochromatin is common around the centromere, near the telomeres and in regions that the cell must keep quiet.

Euchromatin is the open state. Its nucleosomes are more widely spaced, and the fiber is looser, allowing proteins to reach the DNA. Euchromatin contains most of the active genes, and it is the state in which transcription typically takes place. The regions that are being read and copied are therefore usually euchromatic, while the silent regions are heterochromatic.

Kornberg and Lorch (1999) explained that the packing state is set by the nucleosomes and by the chemical marks on their histone tails. Open chromatin often carries marks that loosen the packing, while closed chromatin carries marks that tighten it and recruit proteins that compact the fiber further. The difference is not a fixed property of the DNA sequence. It is a property of the protein environment built on that sequence.

Luger and colleagues (1997) provided the structure that explains why packing matters. The nucleosome holds the DNA in a defined position, and its surface determines which proteins can bind. In closed chromatin, the nucleosomes are arranged so that they block access to the DNA, while in open chromatin the same nucleosomes can be displaced or modified to make the DNA accessible.

Blackburn (2001) described one important example of heterochromatin, the chromosome ends. Telomeric chromatin has a specialized packaging that keeps the ends protected and prevents them from being treated as breaks. This heterochromatic state is an essential part of chromosome stability, and it shows that heterochromatin serves a protective function as well as a silencing one.

The two states are not permanent. A region can change from heterochromatin to euchromatin when the cell needs its genes, and it can change back when they are no longer needed. Such changes happen during development, when different cell types switch on different sets of genes, and they also happen in response to signals from the environment. The cell can therefore adjust its genome to its circumstances.

The switch between states involves several kinds of proteins. Some enzymes add or remove chemical marks on histones, others move nucleosomes along the DNA and still others bind to specific marks and recruit additional proteins. Together these form a system that can establish, maintain and erase the packing state of a region. The system is robust enough to keep a silent region silent through many cell divisions, but flexible enough to open it when required.

It helps to picture heterochromatin and euchromatin as a library. The heterochromatin is the set of archives kept in locked storage, and the euchromatin is the reading room where the books are used every day. A librarian decides which books move from the archive to the reading room, and the books can be returned when they are no longer needed. The genome is organized in a similar way, with the cell's machinery deciding which sections are open for use.

Students should be careful not to think of heterochromatin as entirely useless. Some heterochromatin contains important genes that are used only at particular times, and some regions are essential for the structure of the chromosome itself. The centromere, for example, is rich in heterochromatin, and it is needed for the accurate division of chromosomes. Heterochromatin has a structural role as well as a silencing role.

The distinction between the two states is also important in disease. Abnormal changes in heterochromatin can silence genes that should be active or activate genes that should be quiet. Such changes are found in several kinds of cancer and in some inherited disorders. Understanding the packing state of a region is therefore part of understanding how a disease arises.

The spreading of heterochromatin is another striking feature. A silent state can extend along a chromosome from a starting point, covering neighboring genes and silencing them. Scientists have observed this spreading in model organisms, where a silent region can invade an adjacent active region unless boundary elements block it. The boundaries are special sequences and proteins that act as walls, and they help keep the silent and active states separate along the chromosome.

The two states also interact with the cell's history. Some silent states are inherited through cell division, so that the daughter cells remember the silence of their parent. This memory is one of the reasons that differentiated cells keep their identity over many generations, even though all of them carry the same DNA. The packing state of the chromatin is therefore part of how a cell records what it has become.

A simple way to test your understanding is to ask, for any gene, whether it sits in open or closed chromatin, what marks would be expected there and what would happen if the region were opened or closed. Working through such questions makes the abstract states concrete.

Finally, remember that the two states are a useful simplification. In practice, chromatin lies along a range of packing levels, and many regions are neither clearly open nor clearly closed. The division into two states is a helpful starting point for thinking about the genome, and it is refined by the detailed measurements described in the final chapter.

To summarize, heterochromatin is the tightly packed state of chromatin, usually silent and structurally important, while euchromatin is the open state, where most active genes are found. The state is set by nucleosomes and their marks, and it can change as the cell's needs change. The next and final chapter of this module describes some of the ways scientists study chromosomes.
