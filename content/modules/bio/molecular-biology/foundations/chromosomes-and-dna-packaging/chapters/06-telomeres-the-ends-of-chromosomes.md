A chromosome has two ends, and those ends are special. Each end is capped by a structure called a telomere, a region of DNA made of short repeated sequences that protects the chromosome from being mistaken for a broken piece of DNA. Without telomeres, the cell's repair machinery might join chromosome ends together, scrambling the genome. This chapter introduces telomeres, the problem they solve and the enzyme that maintains them.

The telomeric DNA in humans consists of a short sequence repeated many times, with the same motif of six bases appearing thousands of times at each end. The repeats are rich in guanine and are arranged so that the strand which forms the end is slightly longer than its partner. This arrangement gives the end a single-stranded overhang, which is a distinct shape that proteins can recognize. The telomere is therefore both a sequence and a structure.

Protective proteins bind to the telomere and form a cap. The cap tells the cell that the end is intentional and not damaged. Blackburn (2001) described how the telomere is kept distinct from other DNA, and how the proteins bound to it help to suppress the repair responses that would otherwise recognize the end as a break. The cap therefore protects the chromosome from being joined to others and from being degraded.

A serious problem arises during DNA copying. The enzymes that copy DNA need a short starting primer at the 3' end of each strand, and when the primer at the very end of a chromosome is removed, a small piece of DNA at the end is not copied. Each time a chromosome is copied, its end becomes slightly shorter. This is called the end-replication problem, and it means that chromosomes would shorten with every division if nothing were done about it.

The enzyme telomerase solves this problem. Telomerase carries its own RNA template, which contains the sequence needed to build the telomeric repeat, and it uses this template to add new repeats to the end of the chromosome. Blackburn, Greider and Szostak (2006) reviewed the discovery and the consequences of this enzyme, including its link to aging and cancer. Telomerase allows the chromosome ends to be extended, counteracting the shortening.

The balance between shortening and extension is important. In most normal adult cells, telomerase is not very active, so telomeres gradually shorten over time. When they become too short, the cell can recognize the end as damaged and stop dividing. This limit on division is thought to protect organisms from cancer and to contribute to aging. In many cancer cells, however, telomerase is reactivated, allowing the cells to divide without limit.

Kornberg and Lorch (1999) placed telomeres in the wider context of chromosome organization. Telomeric chromatin is packaged into nucleosomes and other proteins, and its structure differs from that of the bulk genome. The ends form a special region with its own proteins and marks. This specialized packaging is part of how the cell keeps the ends separate from the rest of the chromosome.

It helps to picture a telomere as the plastic tip on the end of a shoelace. The tip stops the lace from fraying and makes it easier to thread. Each time the lace is used, the tip wears a little, and eventually the tip must be replaced. Telomerase is the tool that replaces the tip, and the cap proteins are the structure that holds the tip in place.

Telomere length has become an important topic in medical research. Scientists measure telomere length as a rough indicator of cell history, and they study how telomeres change with age and disease. The relation between telomere length and health is complex, and many factors influence it. Telomere measurements are therefore used as one piece of evidence, rather than as a simple test.

Students should note that telomeres are not the same in every organism. Some species have telomeres with different repeat sequences, and some lack telomerase entirely and use other mechanisms to maintain their ends. The basic problem of the end-replication issue is shared across many species, however, and the solutions reflect a common challenge of copying linear chromosomes.

The telomere shows how a simple feature of chromosome organization can have wide consequences. The end must be protected, copied and sometimes extended, and each of these functions depends on specific proteins and enzymes. The telomere links the packaging of the chromosome to the life history of the cell and of the organism.

The telomere also shows how a cell distinguishes a healthy end from a broken one. A broken end is recognized by repair proteins that try to join it to another broken end. A telomere, by contrast, is coated with proteins that prevent this joining. The difference lies in the protein cap, and losing the cap can make a telomere look like damage. Experiments in many organisms have shown that when telomere capping fails, chromosomes fuse together, which can produce severe instability in the genome.

Telomere studies have also taught researchers about the relation between genes and enzymes. The telomerase enzyme carries an RNA that serves as the template for the repeats, which is an unusual arrangement. This discovery connected the study of RNA with the study of chromosomes, and it showed that the two fields are closely linked in the maintenance of the genome.

Readers who want to follow this topic further should note that telomere research has moved from a small specialist area into a broad field, linking chromosome biology to aging and to cancer treatment. The basic facts described in this chapter are the foundation for that wider work.

The story of telomeres also reminds us that scientific knowledge often arrives in pieces. The structure of the telomere, the protective cap, the enzyme that extends it and the link to disease were discovered over many years by different groups, and each finding changed the picture of the others. Understanding the whole requires putting these pieces together with care.

To summarize, telomeres are repeated DNA sequences with protective proteins that cap the ends of chromosomes. Copying shortens the ends each time, and telomerase counteracts this shortening with its own RNA template. The balance between these forces influences cell division, aging and cancer.
