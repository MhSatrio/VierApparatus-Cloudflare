The nucleosome is the smallest repeating unit of chromosome packaging. It is a compact structure in which a short length of DNA is wound around a core of proteins. The DNA wraps around the core in a left-handed superhelix, making about one and two-thirds turns, and the whole particle is roughly eleven nanometers across. Nucleosomes are strung along the DNA like beads along a thread, separated by short stretches of linker DNA. This chapter describes the structure of the nucleosome and what it does for the cell.

The core of the nucleosome is made of eight histone proteins, two copies each of four kinds called H2A, H2B, H3 and H4. These proteins come together to form a disk-shaped octamer. The DNA wraps around the outside of this disk, held in place by many contacts between the protein surface and the DNA backbone. Each contact is weak, but there are enough of them that the DNA stays firmly wrapped in the normal conditions of the cell.

Olins and Olins (1974) first saw the regularity that led to this unit. Their electron microscope images showed rounded bodies of roughly the right size, arranged along chromatin like beads. They called these bodies nu bodies, and their description became the foundation for later work on the nucleosome. They could not see the internal structure, but they established that chromatin has a repeating organization.

The detailed picture came from the crystal structure determined by Luger and colleagues (1997). Their work showed that the DNA wraps around the histone octamer at a high degree of regularity. The structure revealed the positions of the individual histones, the path of the DNA and the places where the histones grip the DNA. It also showed that the DNA is bent at specific points, which is important for how the nucleosome is positioned and for how it can be opened.

Why is the nucleosome such a good packaging unit? The answer lies in the compaction it achieves. Each nucleosome holds about one hundred and forty-seven base pairs of DNA in a compact form. Because the DNA is wrapped, the length of a chromosome is reduced several times over, and the first step of the reduction is accomplished by these units. The nucleosome therefore solves the first half of the packaging problem: it makes the DNA small enough to fit into the nucleus.

The nucleosome also helps solve the second half, which is access. Because the DNA is wrapped on the outside of the core, its sequence remains accessible to proteins that can reach it through the gaps between the wraps. The wrapping is not so tight that proteins cannot bind, and cells have machinery that can slide, remove or reposition nucleosomes when needed. The packaging is thus compact but not closed.

Kornberg and Lorch (1999) reviewed how the nucleosome changed the understanding of chromosomes. They pointed out that the nucleosome is not merely a packing device. It is a platform for regulation, because the histones can carry chemical marks, and the arrangement of nucleosomes affects which genes are active. The nucleosome therefore links the physical problem of packaging to the biological problem of control.

Consider the nucleosome as a spool of thread. The thread is wound around the spool in a regular way, and the spool is small enough to handle easily. Many spools are strung along a single thread, each separated by a short length of bare thread. The spool is the histone core, the wound thread is the DNA, and the bare thread between spools is the linker. The arrangement is orderly, but the thread can still be pulled off the spool when it is needed.

It is important to note that the nucleosome is a dynamic structure. It is not a fixed lock that holds the DNA forever. Nucleosomes can be moved along the DNA, partly removed or replaced by variant histones with different properties. Each of these changes can alter how easily a region of DNA can be read. Such dynamics are one reason why the nucleosome is central to the study of gene regulation.

The nucleosome also explains why the DNA sequence alone does not determine whether a gene is active. Two identical sequences can sit in different chromatin environments, one wrapped tightly and the other open. The environment is set by the nucleosomes and the chemical marks on them, which means that the same DNA can behave differently in different cells. This is a key reason that molecular biology has had to look beyond the DNA sequence itself.

Students should remember that the nucleosome is the first level of organization, not the last. The beads on the string are themselves folded into higher structures, which the next chapter describes. The nucleosome is nonetheless the foundation on which all higher levels of chromosome organization are built.

The shape of the wrapped DNA also helps explain how the cell can compact its genome without tangling it. The DNA enters and leaves the octamer at a particular angle, and the wraps are stacked so that each one lies next to the previous one. This regular stacking keeps the linker DNA between nucleosomes in a predictable orientation, which is important when the chain is folded into higher structures. The nucleosome is therefore not simply a bead that happens to hold DNA; it is a geometrically defined unit whose shape constrains the way the chain can fold.

Scientists have also learned that the sequence of the DNA influences how easily it wraps. Some sequences bend more readily than others, and they tend to sit more comfortably around the histone core. This means that the packaging is not entirely uniform across the genome. Certain positions are favored for nucleosomes, and these positions can be important for controlling where other proteins bind. The nucleosome therefore responds to the DNA sequence as well as shaping it.

To summarize, the nucleosome is a compact unit in which about one hundred and forty-seven base pairs of DNA are wrapped around an octamer of histone proteins. It packages the DNA, leaves it accessible and provides a platform for gene regulation. The next chapter describes the histones themselves, including their flexible tails.
