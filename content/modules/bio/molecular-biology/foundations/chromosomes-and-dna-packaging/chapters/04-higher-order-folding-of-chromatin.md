Nucleosomes shorten a chromosome by several times, but a chromosome must be shortened far more than that to fit into a dividing cell. The next step is higher-order folding, in which the chain of nucleosomes is coiled, looped and compacted into progressively denser structures. This chapter describes the levels of folding and why scientists still debate some of their details.

The first level is the chain itself. When nucleosomes are spread along the DNA with short linkers between them, the result looks like beads on a string. Electron microscopy of chromatin from low-salt conditions shows exactly this pattern, a fiber about eleven nanometers wide with nucleosomes spaced along it. Olins and Olins (1974) observed the beads that formed the basis for this picture, and their work remains the starting point for understanding the chain.

The second level is the folding of the chain into a thicker fiber. In the presence of salt and some other proteins, the beads of the chain can pack closer together, forming a fiber roughly thirty nanometers in diameter. The exact structure of this fiber has been debated for many years. Several models have been proposed, in which the nucleosomes are arranged in a helix or in a zigzag pattern, and the question is not fully settled. What is clear is that the chain becomes more compact when the conditions allow.

Kornberg and Lorch (1999) pointed out that these higher levels of folding are dynamic and depend on the state of the cell. Chromatin is not always folded into the thick fiber. In regions that are active, the chain tends to be looser, and in silent regions it tends to be more compact. The degree of folding therefore reflects the activity of the genes in that region, and it changes as the cell's needs change.

The third level is the organization of the fiber into loops. Large loops of chromatin are anchored to a scaffold of nonhistone proteins, and these loops are arranged along the length of each chromosome. The loops bring distant parts of the DNA together, and they help organize the genome into domains, regions that share similar activity. The loops are an important part of how a long chromosome is held in a manageable form.

The final level of folding is the chromosome itself, which becomes visible under a light microscope during cell division. At this stage, the chromatin is condensed to its most compact form, and the chromosome appears as a distinct body. The condensation involves many proteins beyond the histones, and it allows the chromosome to be moved and separated accurately during division.

Luger and colleagues (1997) described the nucleosome core, which is the foundation of all these levels. Their structure shows how the DNA is wrapped at the first level of packing, and it provides the basis for understanding how neighboring nucleosomes might interact in the higher fibers. The detailed structure of the higher levels is more difficult to determine, but the nucleosome core remains the reference point.

It helps to picture the folding as a set of nested coils. The beads of nucleosomes form a chain, the chain coils into a thick rope, the rope forms loops that are anchored to a frame, and the frame is compacted for division. Each level adds compactness, and each can be loosened or tightened by the cell. The result is a structure that holds a great deal of DNA in a small space and that can still be read when necessary.

The degree of folding also affects how the genome is organized in the nucleus. Chromosomes occupy distinct regions, called chromosome territories, and the loops of chromatin shape how those territories interact. Genes in one territory may be close to genes in another, and such proximity can influence how they are regulated. The three dimensional arrangement of the genome is thus part of its function.

Students should note that the details of the higher levels are still under investigation. Some models are supported by many experiments, while others are still debated. Scientists use a range of methods, including microscopy, crosslinking and sequencing, to examine how the chromatin is folded in living cells. Each method reveals a different aspect of the structure, and the picture is refined as new data arrive.

The main lesson of this chapter is that chromatin folding is hierarchical and flexible. Each level depends on the one below, and each can be adjusted by the cell. The beads of nucleosomes are the foundation, and the higher levels are built on them. The next chapter describes the way chromosomes are organized during cell division, when the folding reaches its most compact form.

Higher-order folding also affects how repair happens. When a break occurs in the DNA, the cell must find the broken site among a huge amount of chromatin and make the repair without damaging nearby genes. The folding of the chromatin determines how easily the repair proteins can reach the break. Some regions are open and easy to access, while others are compact and need special handling before repair can take place. The cell therefore coordinates its repair machinery with the state of the chromatin around each break.

The folding also changes during the cell cycle. As a cell prepares to divide, its chromatin is reorganized, and the loops and domains are rebuilt. This reorganization must be precise, because it affects which genes are active in the daughter cells. Cells that are about to divide reset their chromatin so that the pattern of gene activity can be re-established after division. The cycle of folding and unfolding is therefore part of the inheritance of gene activity as well as of the DNA itself.

To summarize, chromatin is folded from a chain of nucleosomes into thicker fibers, into loops and finally into the condensed chromosomes seen during division. The degree of folding reflects the activity of the genes in each region, and the details of the higher levels are still being studied. The next chapter describes the chromosomes during cell division.
