Most of the time, a cell's chromosomes are spread out in the nucleus, so thin and diffuse that they cannot be seen with an ordinary light microscope. They become visible only when the cell prepares to divide, because at that moment they condense into compact bodies. Each chromosome shortens and thickens, and the familiar X-shape appears. This chapter explains why the condensation happens and what it accomplishes.

The reason for condensation is accurate separation. When a cell divides, each daughter cell must receive exactly one copy of each chromosome. The process is carried out by a structure called the spindle, which pulls the chromosomes apart toward opposite ends of the cell. A tangled, spread-out mass of DNA could not be divided accurately, because the spindle would not be able to grip the individual chromosomes. Condensed chromosomes are compact enough to be moved as units.

Before condensation, each chromosome is copied. The copy is held to its partner by a protein complex, and the two identical halves are called sister chromatids. The chromatids are joined at a region called the centromere, which is the site where the spindle attaches. During division, the chromatids are separated and pulled to opposite poles. The condensation of each chromatid makes this separation possible, and it helps prevent the chromatids from becoming entangled with one another.

Kornberg and Lorch (1999) noted that the condensation of chromatin involves the same basic packaging described in earlier chapters, but at a much higher level of compaction. The nucleosomes are folded more tightly, and the loops of chromatin are arranged into a dense frame. The result is a chromosome that is many times shorter than its extended form, while still carrying all of its genes in the correct order.

Condensation is driven by a group of proteins that bind to the chromosome and organize it. These proteins help to form loops and to fold the chromatin into a compact body. They are not part of the nucleosome itself, but they work alongside it to build the final structure. The activity of these proteins is regulated during the cell cycle, so that condensation happens at the right time and is reversed after division.

Olins and Olins (1974) described the fundamental organization of chromatin that all of this folding builds upon. Their observations of the repeating units along chromatin show the starting point of the process, which ends in the highly compacted chromosome. The same basic unit is present in extended and condensed chromatin, and the cell changes only the degree of packing.

Blackburn (2001) emphasized that the ends of chromosomes also matter during division. The chromosome ends, or telomeres, must be protected from being treated as broken DNA, and they must remain intact through each round of copying. The condensation process must therefore respect these ends, and the cell has special machinery to keep them safe. The next module describes telomeres in more detail.

It helps to compare division with packing a suitcase for a long trip. The clothes must be folded so they fit, and each item must be kept in its place so that it can be unpacked correctly at the other end. The condensed chromosome is the folded suitcase, and the spindle is the hands that place each item into the right bag. A careless packing would lead to a mess, and a careless division would lead to cells with missing or extra chromosomes.

Errors in chromosome separation can have serious consequences. A cell that receives too many or too few chromosomes may fail to develop properly or may die. Such errors are called aneuploidy, and they are an important feature of many cancers. The accurate condensation and separation of chromosomes is therefore essential for the health of an organism.

The condensed chromosome also allows scientists to study the genome. Chromosomes can be stained, photographed and counted during division, and this is the basis of the karyotype, an image of all the chromosomes of an individual arranged in pairs. Karyotypes are widely used in medicine to detect large changes in chromosome number or structure, and they depend on the compaction that happens during division.

Students should remember that condensation is reversed after division. In the daughter cells, the chromosomes decondense and return to the spread-out form, where the genes can be read again. The cycle of condensation and decondensation is one of the most striking features of chromosome biology, and it shows how the packaging system can be adjusted to suit the needs of the cell.

The timing of condensation is carefully controlled. The cell waits until DNA copying is complete before it fully condenses its chromosomes, and it checks that the chromosomes are attached to the spindle correctly before it allows them to separate. These checks protect the cell from errors. If a chromosome is not properly attached, the cell delays the separation until the problem is fixed, which reduces the chance that the daughter cells will inherit the wrong number of chromosomes.

Condensation also has a visible structure that scientists use as a guide. The two sister chromatids are held together along their length by a protein complex that links them after copying, and this linkage is released in stages during division. The release is needed for the chromatids to separate cleanly, and it shows that the physical state of the chromosome is controlled at each step of the division process.

It is also worth remembering that the process is not the same in every organism. Some species keep their nuclear membrane through division, and others break it down completely. In all cases the chromosomes must be condensed and sorted accurately, and the specific arrangement varies with the biology of the species.

Students often find it helpful to compare the stages of division with a sequence of tasks in a busy kitchen. First the ingredients are prepared, then they are measured and laid out, then they are carefully divided into equal portions, and finally each portion is put in its own container. The order matters, and an error at any step affects the final result. Chromosomes behave in the same way, and each stage of their condensation and separation has a role in making two correct cells.

To summarize, chromosomes condense into compact bodies during cell division so that the spindle can separate the sister chromatids accurately. The condensation uses the same packaging principles as interphase chromatin but at a higher level of compaction, and it is reversed after division.
