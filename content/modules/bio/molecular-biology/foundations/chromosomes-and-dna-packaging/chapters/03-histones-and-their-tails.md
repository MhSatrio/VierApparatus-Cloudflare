Histones are the proteins that give the nucleosome its structure. They are small, positively charged proteins, rich in the amino acids lysine and arginine, which carry positive charges at the pH of the cell. Because DNA is negatively charged, the histones attract the DNA strongly, and this attraction is a large part of what holds the nucleosome together. This chapter introduces the four core histones and the flexible tails that stick out from their cores.

The four core histones are called H2A, H2B, H3 and H4. Each is a small protein of about one hundred to one hundred and forty amino acids. They share a common fold, called the histone fold, in which three helical segments are linked by short loops. Two histones of different kinds can pair through this fold, forming a dimer. Two dimers of H3 and H4 form a tetramer, and two dimers of H2A and H2B attach to the tetramer on either side, making the complete octamer.

Luger and colleagues (1997) showed that the histone fold is the key to how the octamer is assembled. The pairing of the histones is highly regular, and it creates a surface onto which the DNA is wrapped. The positively charged parts of the histones lie close to the negative backbone of the DNA, and this closeness is what allows the DNA to wrap so tightly without repelling itself. The octamer is therefore a precisely shaped scaffold for the DNA.

Each histone also has a tail, a flexible segment that extends beyond the globular core. The tails are not part of the fold, and in the crystal structure they often appear disordered, meaning they do not settle into a fixed shape. This flexibility is important. The tails reach out from the core and can interact with other proteins and with neighboring nucleosomes, and they are the places where most chemical marks are placed.

Kornberg and Lorch (1999) explained that the histone tails are central to the regulation of chromatin. Because the tails are flexible and exposed, they can be modified by enzymes that add or remove chemical groups. Common modifications include acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation and ubiquitination. Each modification can change the local environment of the nucleosome, altering how tightly the DNA is held and which proteins are attracted to it.

The modifications matter because they change the charge and shape of the tail. For example, acetylation removes a positive charge from a lysine, weakening the attraction between the tail and the DNA. This loosens the packaging locally and makes the DNA easier to read. Methylation has more varied effects, depending on which residue is modified and how many methyl groups are added. Some methyl marks are linked with active genes, and others with silent ones.

Olins and Olins (1974) saw the nucleosome core as a body of uniform size, but the later work on histone tails revealed a much richer picture. The basic architecture they observed is the same in active and inactive regions, but the marks on the tails can make a dramatic difference to how the chromatin behaves. The nucleosome is therefore a constant framework with variable decorations.

It is useful to picture the histone core as a spool with a set of flags. The spool is the octamer, the thread is the DNA, and the flags are the tails. Each flag can carry a different colored sign, and the signs tell other proteins what to do. Some signs say "open this region", while others say "close it". Reading and writing these signs is one of the main ways a cell controls its genes without changing the DNA sequence.

Histone variants also exist. Cells contain versions of H2A, H3 and other histones that differ slightly in sequence. These variants can be placed in particular regions of the genome, and they can change the stability of the nucleosome. A nucleosome containing a variant may be easier or harder to remove, and it may carry different marks. The use of variants adds another layer of control to chromatin.

For a beginner, the key idea is that the histone core provides structure and the tails provide flexibility and signaling. The core keeps the DNA compact, and the tails allow the packaging to be adjusted. This division of roles is one of the elegant features of the nucleosome, and it explains why the study of histone tails has become so important in modern biology.

It is worth noting that the same protein family is found across eukaryotes. The histones are among the most conserved proteins known, and their sequences are very similar in organisms as different as yeast and humans. This conservation reflects the central importance of the nucleosome, and it has allowed researchers to study histones in many model organisms.

The discovery that histones are modified was an important turning point. Researchers initially thought of histones as passive scaffolds, but experiments showed that their tails are chemically changed in patterns that vary between cell types and between active and silent regions. This finding suggested that the packaging of DNA carries a second layer of information, sometimes described as an epigenetic layer, which can be passed on through cell division. The layer does not change the DNA sequence, but it influences which genes are read in each cell.

The number of possible marks is large, and each mark can have different effects depending on where it occurs. Some marks are added at one residue and others at a neighboring residue, and the combination can be read by proteins that bind to specific patterns. Scientists now study these combinations as a kind of language, and they try to understand how the patterns are written, read and erased. This research is still developing, but it has already changed how biologists think about the regulation of genes.

To summarize, the four core histones form an octamer with a shared fold, and their flexible tails extend from the core. The tails carry chemical marks that change the packaging and the activity of the nearby genes. The next chapter describes how nucleosomes are folded into higher levels of chromatin.
