Chromosomes are too small to be studied by eye, and their packaging is too complex to be understood from a single picture. Scientists therefore use a range of methods to study them, each of which reveals a different aspect of their structure and behavior. This final chapter introduces the main approaches, from the simple staining of condensed chromosomes to the modern measurements of chromatin packing. The methods show how the ideas of the earlier chapters were discovered and tested.

The oldest and most widely used approach is staining. When cells are treated with certain dyes, their chromosomes take up the stain in characteristic patterns of light and dark bands. The patterns are reproducible for each chromosome, which means that a stained chromosome can be recognized and assigned to its number. Staining works best on condensed chromosomes during division, which is why cell division is often used as a stage for chromosome analysis.

A karyotype is an organized picture of all the chromosomes of an individual. The chromosomes are photographed, cut out and arranged in pairs, usually in order of size. The karyotype allows a laboratory to count the chromosomes and check for large changes, such as the loss or gain of a whole chromosome or the rearrangement of a large piece. Karyotypes are a standard tool in medical genetics, and they depend on the compaction of chromosomes described in the previous chapters.

Olins and Olins (1974) showed that the packaging of chromatin could be seen directly in electron microscope images, long before the detailed structure was known. Their images of repeating units were an early form of imaging, and they established the principle that the organization of chromatin could be studied visually. Modern imaging methods build on this principle, although they use fluorescent labels and sophisticated analysis.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization is one of the most powerful modern methods. A short DNA probe, labeled with a fluorescent dye, is designed to match a particular region of a chromosome. When the probe is added to the cells, it binds to its target, and the target lights up under a fluorescence microscope. Using probes of different colors, researchers can show the positions of several regions at once, and they can detect small deletions or rearrangements that a karyotype would miss.

The structure of chromatin can also be studied at high resolution. X-ray crystallography, as used by Luger and colleagues (1997), gives the atomic details of the nucleosome core. Other methods, such as cryo-electron microscopy, reveal the shapes of larger assemblies. These approaches tell us how the DNA is wrapped, how the histones are arranged and how neighboring nucleosomes might interact.

Measuring the packing of chromatin in living cells is more challenging. Some methods use enzymes that cut DNA only where it is open, so the pattern of cuts shows which regions are accessible. Others use chemical marks that are found only in active chromatin, so that the marks can be mapped across the genome. These methods reveal the difference between euchromatin and heterochromatin in great detail, and they allow researchers to see how the state of a region changes.

Kornberg and Lorch (1999) reviewed how such methods have built on the nucleosome concept. Each new technique tests the model of chromatin organization, and each result either supports the model or suggests a refinement. The study of chromosomes is therefore a continuing dialogue between ideas and measurements, and the field has grown through this process.

Blackburn and colleagues (2006) showed how the study of chromosome ends led to new discoveries about telomerase and about the protection of the genome. Those discoveries were made by combining genetics, biochemistry and imaging, which illustrates how the strongest results often come from several methods working together.

It helps to remember that each method answers a different question. Staining answers how many chromosomes there are and whether large parts have changed. Fluorescence answers where a specific sequence is located. Crystallography answers how a molecular complex is built. Accessibility mapping answers which regions are open. A complete picture of the genome comes from putting these answers side by side.

Students should be aware that the methods have limits. Staining cannot reveal small changes, fluorescent probes can only detect the sequences they are designed for, and structural methods work on isolated molecules that may not be identical to those in a living cell. Good scientists are careful about these limits, and they check their conclusions with more than one method before accepting them.

The study of chromosomes is also important in medicine. Karyotypes help diagnose genetic disorders, and fluorescent probes are used to detect changes in cancer cells. The packing of chromatin is increasingly linked to disease, and methods for measuring it are being developed for clinical use. The basic knowledge of chromosome structure therefore has practical value far beyond the laboratory.

A further example is the study of how chromatin changes in disease. Researchers compare the packing and marks of healthy and diseased cells, and they look for patterns that distinguish them. Such comparisons require careful controls, because the patterns can be affected by the sample preparation and by the age or type of the cells. Good studies therefore use several samples, check their results against known standards and report their methods in enough detail for others to repeat them.

Students who learn these methods gain a valuable habit of mind. Each technique is a tool with a purpose and a limit, and the most reliable knowledge comes from putting several tools together. This habit is the same one that scientists use across all of biology, and it is part of what makes the study of chromosomes a model for careful research.

To summarize, chromosomes are studied by staining and karyotyping, by fluorescence methods that locate specific sequences, by structural methods that reveal molecular detail and by measurements that map the openness of chromatin. Each method answers a different question, and together they build the picture of the genome presented in this module.
