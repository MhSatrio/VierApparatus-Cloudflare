Open a textbook of biology and you will find pictures of chromosomes as X-shaped bodies. These pictures are familiar, but they show something that is rarely seen in a living cell. Most of the time, the chromosomes are spread out, far too thin to see in an ordinary microscope. The X-shape appears only during cell division, when the DNA has been condensed into a compact form. This chapter introduces what a chromosome is and why cells organize their DNA in this way.

A chromosome is a single long molecule of DNA together with the proteins that help package and organize it. Each chromosome carries many genes, arranged along its length in a fixed order. Humans normally have forty-six chromosomes arranged in twenty-three pairs, with one set inherited from each parent. Other species have different numbers. The number of chromosomes is not the same as the amount of genetic information, but it is a useful way of describing the organization of a genome.

The central problem is one of scale. A chromosome in a human cell can be several centimeters long when fully stretched, and the DNA of all the chromosomes together reaches about two meters. The nucleus, where most of that DNA is kept, has a diameter of only a few micrometers. Fitting the DNA into the nucleus requires a reduction in length of several thousandfold. Without some system of folding, the molecules would tangle into an unusable mass.

Packaging must also allow access. A cell must be able to read its genes, copy its DNA and repair damage. The DNA cannot be packed so tightly that these processes are blocked. The cell therefore needs a system that compacts the DNA but leaves parts of it open for use. This is the second competing need. A good packaging system is neither too loose nor too tight, and it can change as the cell's needs change.

Olins and Olins (1974) observed the first clear evidence of the regular organization that makes such a system possible. Using electron microscopy, they saw small rounded bodies strung along the chromatin of cells, a material made of DNA and proteins. The regularity of these bodies suggested that chromatin is not a random tangle, but a structure built from repeating units. This discovery opened the way to the modern understanding of how DNA is folded.

Kornberg and Lorch (1999) later reviewed how the nucleosome, the unit suggested by those early observations, became the basic building block of chromosomes. Their review traced the development of the idea over a quarter of a century and showed how the nucleosome explained both the compaction of DNA and the regulation of access to it. The nucleosome is now recognized as the first and most important level of chromosome organization.

Luger and colleagues (1997) provided the detailed structure of this unit. Their work showed how DNA is wrapped around a core of histone proteins, and it gave a physical basis for the idea that the nucleosome compacts the DNA while leaving it accessible. Understanding that structure is the first step toward understanding the whole of chromosome biology.

It helps to think of a chromosome as a rope that has been coiled and bundled for storage. The rope must be kept tidy enough to fit in a box, but the coils must be loose enough that a sailor can reach any part of it when needed. The cell solves the same puzzle, using proteins to organize the coils and chemical marks to adjust how tight they are. The result is a system that holds a great deal of information in a small space.

Chromosomes also play a role in cell division. When a cell divides, its chromosomes must be copied and then separated into two sets, one for each new cell. The condensation of the chromosomes into compact forms makes this separation possible. A tangled and spread-out mass of DNA could not be divided accurately, but compact chromosomes can be pulled apart by the machinery of the dividing cell.

Students sometimes ask whether all cells have the same chromosomes. In most organisms, the chromosomes are the same in every cell of the body, but they are used differently. A liver cell and a nerve cell have the same DNA packaged into the same chromosomes, yet they use different genes. The chromosome is therefore a container and an organizing system, and its content can be read in different ways by different cells.

Bacteria also organize their DNA, although their system is different from ours. A bacterial chromosome is usually a single circular molecule that is folded into a compact region of the cell, without a nucleus. The principles of packing and access still apply, and the bacterial system shows that the problem of organizing a long molecule is shared across life, even though the solutions differ.

The idea of a chromosome as an organized unit also has a long history. Early cytologists, who studied cells with light microscopes and stains, noticed that the threadlike bodies in the nucleus behaved in regular ways during division. They saw that the threads duplicated, that they lined up in the middle of the cell and that they were pulled apart to opposite ends. These observations were made long before anyone knew that DNA was the carrier of heredity, and they laid the groundwork for the link between chromosomes and genes that was established in the twentieth century.

The organization of a genome also has consequences for how it can be inherited and repaired. Because genes sit at particular places along a chromosome, their neighbors matter. Genes that lie close together tend to be passed on together during reproduction, and certain repair processes depend on having an intact partner chromosome to copy from. Packaging and organization therefore affect not only the reading of genes but also the way the genome is maintained from one generation to the next.

To summarize, a chromosome is a long DNA molecule with its associated proteins, organized so that a huge length of DNA fits into a small nucleus while remaining accessible for reading, copying and repair. The basic unit of this organization is the nucleosome, which the following chapters describe in detail.
