Most amino acids fit into the groups described in the previous chapters, but three stand out for their special behavior. Glycine has the smallest side chain of all, which is just a hydrogen atom. Proline has a side chain that loops back and joins the backbone, forming a rigid ring. Cysteine has a side chain containing sulfur, which can link to another cysteine. These three residues shape proteins in ways that the other amino acids cannot.

Glycine is the most flexible amino acid. Because its side chain is so small, it fits into tight spaces and allows the backbone to bend sharply. Proteins often place glycine at turns, where the chain must change direction, and at places where two strands pass very close to each other. Glycine's flexibility is useful, but too much of it can make a region floppy, so it must be used with care.

Proline is the most rigid. Its side chain forms a ring that connects back to the nitrogen of the backbone, which limits how the chain can bend at that point. Proline therefore often appears at the start of helices or in places where a fixed kink is needed. Pauling, Corey and Branson (1951) described the regular helical structures of the backbone, and proline's ring is one of the features that influences which helices can form.

Cysteine is special because of its sulfur atom. Two cysteine side chains can join through a covalent bond called a disulfide bridge, which holds parts of a protein together more strongly than most other interactions. Disulfide bridges are common in proteins that are secreted from cells, where the surrounding fluid does not reduce them. Inside the cell, the environment is more reducing, so disulfide bridges are less common in cytoplasmic proteins.

Chothia (1992) noted that the placement of glycine and proline is often conserved in protein families, because these residues control the shape of key turns and loops. A change at such positions can distort the fold, so the residues tend to be kept even when other parts of the protein vary. Their special properties make them important for the architecture of the fold.

Kyte and Doolittle (1982) grouped these residues with others on their hydropathy scale, but their true behavior depends on shape rather than on water preference alone. Glycine is often placed on the surface because it is small and does not need much protection, while proline's ring makes it useful in both surface and interior locations. The scale helps in comparison, but it does not capture everything about these residues.

Students should know that the special amino acids are not rare. Glycine and proline are common in many proteins, especially in structural regions and in loops. Their unusual properties are a practical tool that the cell uses to shape proteins, and understanding them makes the overall picture of amino acid behavior more complete.

One way to remember the three is to picture the ways each changes a chain. Glycine lets the chain bend freely, proline makes it stiff at a fixed point, and cysteine ties two parts of the chain together. Each of these effects can be decisive for the shape of a protein, even when the rest of the sequence is similar.

The special residues also show that the alphabet of proteins is not uniform. A useful way to think about it is that most amino acids are interchangeable at many positions, while glycine and proline are often fixed because their shapes are needed. Mutations that change a glycine or a proline in a critical turn can therefore be more damaging than changes at other positions, even when the chemistry of the replacement seems similar.

To summarize, glycine is small and flexible, proline is rigid because its side chain forms a ring with the backbone, and cysteine can form disulfide bridges that hold proteins together. These residues shape turns, helices and outside-facing structures in ways that the common amino acids cannot. The next chapter explains how the properties of amino acids are measured and compared.
