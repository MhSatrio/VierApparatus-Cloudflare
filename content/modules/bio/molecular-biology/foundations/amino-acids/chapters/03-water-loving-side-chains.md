Not every amino acid avoids water. Many have side chains that contain oxygen or nitrogen atoms, which share electrons unevenly and attract water molecules through hydrogen bonds. Such side chains are called "polar", and they are often described as hydrophilic, meaning water-loving. Serine, threonine, asparagine, glutamine, tyrosine and cysteine are common polar amino acids. They tend to be found on the outside of proteins, where they can meet the surrounding water.

The polar side chains are useful because they can form hydrogen bonds. A hydrogen bond between a side chain and a water molecule keeps the protein soluble, and a hydrogen bond between two side chains can help hold a protein in shape. Pauling, Corey and Branson (1951) showed that hydrogen bonds are central to the regular structures of proteins, and polar side chains take part in these bonds at the surface and within the chain.

Kyte and Doolittle (1982) placed the polar amino acids at the hydrophilic end of their scale. Their scores show that these residues are much less likely to be buried in the core of a protein. A sequence that contains many polar residues in a row is therefore likely to lie on the surface, or to be part of a flexible region that interacts with water.

Polar side chains also contribute to the sites where proteins bind other molecules. A pocket that binds a sugar, for example, often lines its walls with polar residues that form hydrogen bonds with the sugar's hydroxyl groups. The fit between the pocket and its target depends on the arrangement of these polar groups, and changing one of them can weaken or abolish binding.

Chothia (1992) emphasized that proteins from different families share many of these surface features. Surface residues tend to be more variable than core residues, because they can be exchanged without destroying the fold. The surface of a protein is therefore a place of change, while the core is the place of stability, and both are shaped by the polar and hydrophobic character of their residues.

Cysteine deserves a special mention. Its side chain contains sulfur, and two cysteines can form a covalent bond called a disulfide bridge. Disulfide bridges help hold some proteins together, especially proteins that are secreted from cells into the surrounding fluid. Cysteine is therefore polar in some respects but has a unique chemistry of its own.

Students often confuse polar with charged. Polar side chains have uneven sharing of electrons, but they carry no net charge at the normal pH of cells. Charged side chains, which are the subject of the next chapter, carry a full positive or negative charge. The distinction matters, because charged groups interact more strongly with water and with one another.

A useful picture is a surface of hooks. The polar side chains are the hooks that catch water and other polar molecules, holding the protein in the aqueous environment of the cell. The hydrophobic side chains are kept inside, out of the water, and the protein's shape reflects the balance between the two.

Polar side chains also help a protein respond to its surroundings. Their hydrogen bonds with water can shift when the protein moves, and small changes in the pattern of these bonds can change how easily the protein opens or closes. Proteins that change shape in this way often have clusters of polar residues at their hinges, where the flexibility is needed.

To summarize, polar amino acids such as serine, asparagine and tyrosine have side chains that attract water through hydrogen bonds. They usually lie on the protein surface, they help in binding and they make proteins soluble. The next chapter introduces the amino acids with charged side chains.
