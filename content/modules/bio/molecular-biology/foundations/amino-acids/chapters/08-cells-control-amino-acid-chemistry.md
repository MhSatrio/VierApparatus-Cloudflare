The properties of an amino acid are not fixed once it is part of a protein. Cells can change the chemistry of amino acids after the chain is made, by attaching small chemical groups to their side chains. These changes are called post-translational modifications, and they are one of the fastest ways that a cell can switch a protein on or off, change where it goes or alter what it binds. This final chapter of the module shows how amino acid chemistry is adjusted during the life of a protein.

One common modification is phosphorylation, in which a phosphate group is attached to a serine, threonine or tyrosine. The phosphate carries negative charges, so it changes a neutral polar residue into a strongly charged one. A phosphorylated residue can form salt bridges, attract positive residues nearby and change the shape of a loop. Many signals in cells work by adding or removing phosphates in this way.

Another modification is acetylation, in which an acetyl group is attached to lysine. This removes the positive charge of lysine, which weakens its attraction to negative partners. Acetylation is used to regulate proteins in several places in the cell, including the proteins that package DNA. Because acetylation can be added and removed, it works as a reversible switch.

Pauling, Corey and Branson (1951) described the regular backbone structures in which these side chains sit. A change in the side chain alters the way it packs against the helix or the sheet, and a charged group added to the surface can change how the protein interacts with its neighbors. The regular structures give a framework in which the changes have predictable effects.

Chothia (1992) noted that protein families often contain sites that are chemically modifiable, and that these sites are frequently conserved. A residue that is repeatedly used for regulation is less likely to be replaced, because its modification is important to the protein's function. Conservation in these positions therefore points to regulatory roles.

Kyte and Doolittle (1982) measured the properties of unmodified amino acids, and modification can change those properties. A hydrophobic patch can become more polar after a modification, and a polar surface can become charged. The scales of the previous chapter describe the starting chemistry, and modification shows how that chemistry can be adjusted.

Modifications have an important advantage over making a new protein. A cell that wants a rapid response does not need to synthesize new molecules. Instead, it can change existing ones, adding or removing a small group in seconds or minutes. This speed explains why modifications are common in signaling pathways that respond to changing conditions.

Students should be aware that modifications can be complex. A single protein may carry several marks, and the combination can have effects that differ from any single mark. Scientists study these combinations to understand how proteins integrate many signals at once.

A useful picture is a light switch with a dimmer. Phosphorylation is like a switch that can turn a protein on or off, and acetylation is like a dimmer that adjusts the level. Cells use many such controls together, and their combined pattern decides how the protein behaves.

Students should also know that modification is removed as well as added. Enzymes called phosphatases remove phosphate groups, and enzymes called deacetylases remove acetyl groups. The balance between the adding and removing enzymes sets the level of modification, and a shift in that balance can change the activity of many proteins at once.

To summarize, cells change the chemistry of amino acids after a protein is made, through modifications such as phosphorylation and acetylation. These changes alter charge, shape and interactions, and they give cells a fast and reversible way to control protein function.
