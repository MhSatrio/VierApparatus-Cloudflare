Biologists often want to compare amino acids on a single scale. Is one more hydrophobic than another? Which is larger? Which carries a charge? Such questions are answered with property scales, which assign a number to each amino acid. The numbers make it possible to scan a protein sequence, to compare families and to predict which regions are likely to be buried or exposed. Scales are simplifications, but they are very useful.

The most famous hydrophobicity scale was published by Kyte and Doolittle (1982). They assigned each amino acid a value that reflects how much it prefers a water-free environment, with high positive values for the most hydrophobic residues and negative values for the most hydrophilic. They then averaged the values over a window of residues along a protein sequence, producing a profile. Regions with high average values are likely to be buried or to cross a membrane.

The profile method is simple, and it works best when the window is wide enough to smooth out small fluctuations. A narrow window shows fine detail, but it is noisy. A wide window reveals broad hydrophobic regions, such as those that span a membrane. Choosing the window size is one of the practical decisions in using the method.

Chothia (1992) emphasized that the properties of residues are linked to the structures of protein families. Scales that describe hydrophobicity or size help to explain why certain positions in a family are conserved and others are variable. Comparisons across families can reveal which features of the amino acid alphabet are most important for the shape of a protein.

Pauling, Corey and Branson (1951) showed that the geometry of the backbone also limits which amino acids fit into particular structures. A single scale of hydrophobicity cannot capture this geometry, so good analysis combines several kinds of information. Size, shape, charge and the ability to form hydrogen bonds all matter, and a scale that captures only one of them gives a partial picture.

The limits of scales should be kept in mind. A residue's properties depend on its surroundings. A hydrophobic residue on the surface of a protein may behave differently from one in the core, and a charged residue may be neutralized by a nearby partner. Scales ignore these context effects, and they can mislead if they are used without thought.

Students should also know that different scales exist for different purposes. Some measure hydrophobicity from experiments on how amino acids partition between water and oil. Others measure the frequency of each amino acid in known structures, or the typical shape of the helices and sheets that each favors. Choosing the right scale depends on the question being asked.

A useful habit is to treat a property scale as a map rather than as the territory. The map is helpful for finding the way, but it leaves out much of the landscape. The best use of a scale is to suggest where to look more closely, not to give a final answer.

Scales can also be combined with structural information. When the shape of a protein is known, researchers can check whether the residues predicted to be buried by a scale are in fact inside the folded structure. Agreement between the two strengthens the conclusion, while disagreement points to a region that needs closer study, such as a surface patch that binds a partner.

To summarize, property scales assign numbers to amino acids so that they can be compared and their sequences analyzed. The Kyte and Doolittle hydropathy scale is widely used to find buried and membrane-crossing regions. Scales are helpful but simplified, and they must be used with awareness of context.
