Every amino acid has the same basic design. At its center is a carbon atom, called the alpha carbon, to which four groups are attached. One group is an amino group, which carries nitrogen, and another is a carboxyl group, which carries a carbon atom with two oxygens. A hydrogen atom is attached to the third position, and the fourth position holds a "side chain", the part that varies from one amino acid to the next. This shared design is the key to how proteins are assembled.

The amino group and the carboxyl group are the parts that link amino acids together. When two amino acids meet, the carboxyl group of one reacts with the amino group of the other, releasing a molecule of water and forming a bond between them. Because every amino acid has both groups, the reaction can repeat, and a chain grows one unit at a time. The backbone of a protein is made of these repeating links, and the side chains project from the backbone like branches from a stem.

The side chain is what makes each amino acid distinct. It can be a single hydrogen atom, as in glycine, or a larger group with rings, charges or sulfur atoms. Because the side chains differ so widely, they give proteins their varied properties. Two proteins with the same backbone but different side chains can behave in completely different ways.

Pauling, Corey and Branson (1951) studied the structure of the polypeptide chain and showed that the backbone can fold into regular helical shapes held together by hydrogen bonds. Their work established that the backbone has a repeating pattern of atoms, while the side chains are free to point in different directions. This division between a fixed backbone and variable side chains is central to protein structure.

The amino acids are the same in nearly all living things. Chothia (1992) pointed out that the families of proteins share recurring building blocks, and that the same small set of amino acids is used across the diversity of life. This conservation reflects the shared chemistry of cells and the efficiency of a common alphabet.

Amino acids differ from one another in size, shape and chemistry, but they all share the features described here. This is one reason why a protein chain can be predicted to some degree from its sequence, even though the full shape depends on many interactions. Learning the shared structure first makes it easier to understand the differences.

Students sometimes confuse the amino acid with the peptide bond. The amino acid is the free unit, with its intact amino and carboxyl groups. Once two amino acids are linked, the resulting unit is part of a peptide, and the groups are changed by the bond. The next modules describe the bond in more detail.

A useful way to remember the design is to picture a central carbon with four arms. One arm carries nitrogen, one carries an acid group, one carries a hydrogen and the last holds the variable side chain. The first three arms are the same in every amino acid, and the fourth decides its personality.

The side chain also determines how an amino acid is classified in studies of protein chemistry. Kyte and Doolittle (1982) used the side chain as the basis of their hydropathy scale, which is why the scale rests on the chemistry of the fourth position rather than on the shared core. Two amino acids with very different side chains can therefore be expected to behave very differently in the same protein, even though they link into chains in exactly the same way.

The shared core also explains why amino acids are read in a single direction along a chain. Each residue contributes an amino end and a carboxyl end, so a protein has a defined beginning and end. The direction matters for the way the chain is made by the ribosome, and it is the reason protein sequences are always written from the amino end to the carboxyl end.

To summarize, each amino acid has a central alpha carbon bearing an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen atom and a variable side chain. The shared core lets amino acids link into chains, and the side chains give proteins their diversity. The next chapter introduces the amino acids with side chains that avoid water.
