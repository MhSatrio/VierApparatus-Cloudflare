Five amino acids carry an electrical charge in their side chains at the pH of the cell. Lysine and arginine carry a positive charge, and histidine can carry a positive charge under some conditions. Aspartate and glutamate carry a negative charge. These charged side chains are among the most important features of a protein, because they attract water strongly and they can attract one another across the protein.

A charged side chain is at home in water. Its charge pulls water molecules around it, forming a shell that makes the residue very soluble. For this reason, charged residues are almost always found on the surface of a protein, where they can interact with the surrounding fluid. Burying a charge in the hydrophobic core would be costly, so the protein usually places charges where they can be solvated.

Charged residues can attract one another. A positive side chain and a negative side chain can form a salt bridge, a weak attraction between opposite charges. Salt bridges help hold parts of a protein together, and they can stabilize a fold or help two proteins bind. Because they depend on the presence of charges, their strength changes with the salt concentration and the pH of the solution.

Kyte and Doolittle (1982) placed charged residues at the most hydrophilic end of their scale, which reflects their strong preference for water. A stretch of charged residues in a sequence is therefore a strong signal of a region that is exposed to the solvent, or that is flexible and does not pack into a core.

Chothia (1992) noted that charged residues on the surface of proteins are often more variable across families than the core residues. The surface can tolerate substitutions of one charged residue for another as long as the overall charge pattern is preserved. This flexibility allows proteins to change their binding properties during evolution without losing their fold.

The charges of these side chains also make them useful in binding. Many enzymes use charged residues to hold a negatively charged substrate, such as a phosphate, in place. The positive side chains of an active site are often arranged to hold the substrate while the reaction proceeds. The chemistry of the binding site therefore depends on the careful placement of charges.

Pauling, Corey and Branson (1951) described the regular helical structures of the polypeptide backbone, which place side chains in predictable directions. The side chains of charged residues project outward from the helix, where they can interact with solvent or with partner residues. Their placement is one of the structural features that the helical models explained.

Students should note that the charge of a side chain depends on pH. Histidine is a good example. At the pH of most cells it carries little charge, but its side chain can gain or lose a proton within the normal range, changing its charge. This makes histidine a common participant in enzymatic reactions that need a switchable charge.

A simple picture for a charged side chain is a magnet with an electric field around it. The field attracts water and any opposite charge in its neighborhood. Salt bridges are like two magnets of opposite polarity that stick together when they meet, while the water around them plays the role of a crowd that they must push through.

To summarize, lysine, arginine and histidine carry positive charges, while aspartate and glutamate carry negative charges, at the pH of cells. Charged side chains are usually on the surface, they can form salt bridges and they are important in binding and catalysis.
