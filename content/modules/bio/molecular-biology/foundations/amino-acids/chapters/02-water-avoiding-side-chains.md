Some amino acids dislike water. Their side chains are built mostly from carbon and hydrogen, which share electrons evenly and do not form strong attractions with the water molecules around them. Such side chains are called "hydrophobic", meaning water-fearing. Leucine, isoleucine, valine, phenylalanine, methionine and tryptophan are among the best known hydrophobic amino acids, and they make up a large part of the cores of many proteins.

Why do water-avoiding side chains matter? Water surrounds a protein inside the cell, and water molecules form a network of hydrogen bonds with one another. A hydrophobic side chain disrupts that network, which is energetically unfavorable. The protein can reduce the disruption by folding so that its hydrophobic side chains are buried in the interior, away from water. This tendency, often called the hydrophobic effect, is one of the main forces that shapes proteins.

Pauling, Corey and Branson (1951) showed that the polypeptide backbone can form regular helices held together by hydrogen bonds within the chain. These helices leave their side chains free to point outward or inward, so the arrangement of hydrophobic side chains depends on how the helices pack. A protein that buries its hydrophobic side chains in a compact core is more stable than one that exposes them.

Kyte and Doolittle (1982) developed a way to measure how hydrophobic each amino acid is. They assigned each amino acid a score that reflects its water-avoiding character, with high positive values for the most hydrophobic and negative values for the most hydrophilic. The scores allow a sequence to be scanned for stretches that are likely to be buried. A long run of hydrophobic amino acids often signals a region that crosses a membrane or packs into a core.

Chothia (1992) noted that the families of proteins are shaped by the pattern of hydrophobic and hydrophilic residues. The interior of a protein is usually well conserved in its hydrophobic character, even when the exact amino acids vary, because the important feature is that the core stays water-free. This explains why evolution can swap one hydrophobic residue for another without losing function.

Hydrophobic side chains are not all alike. They differ in size and shape, and the difference matters for how well they fit inside a core. A large side chain such as phenylalanine or tryptophan fills more space than a small one such as alanine. Packing is a matter of fit, and protein cores are arranged to hold their side chains tightly without gaps or clashes.

Students should be careful with the word hydrophobic. It does not mean that the amino acid is repelled by everything, or that it cannot dissolve. It means that its side chain prefers a water-free environment. In a membrane, which is also water-free in its core, hydrophobic side chains are very comfortable, and many membrane proteins are rich in them.

A simple way to picture this is oil and water. Oil droplets gather together in water, not because they attract one another, but because water pushes them together. Hydrophobic side chains behave in a similar way: they cluster together, and the cluster is held in place by the surrounding water.

The hydrophobic effect also helps explain why proteins can be stable inside a crowded cell. Because the core is water-free, the surface of the folded protein is the only part that meets the surrounding fluid, and this reduces the total area of contact with water. The protein's shape is therefore a compromise between exposing as little hydrophobic surface as possible and keeping the chain compact enough to pack its core efficiently.

Some proteins hide a hydrophobic patch until they need it. A protein that binds a membrane, for example, may expose a hydrophobic stretch only when it approaches the membrane surface. This controlled exposure is a useful way for the cell to direct proteins to the correct place without a constant risk of sticking to the wrong partner.

To summarize, hydrophobic amino acids such as leucine, valine and phenylalanine have side chains that avoid water and tend to cluster in the interior of proteins. Their tendency is measured with hydropathy scales, and their packing is a major force in protein folding. The next chapter introduces the amino acids whose side chains are charged or attract water.
