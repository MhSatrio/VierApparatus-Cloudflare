A protein's sequence is a pattern of hydrophobic, polar and charged amino acids. The pattern is not random. Its arrangement tells the chain where to fold, which parts will face water, which will bury themselves in the core and which will form a binding site. This chapter shows how the properties of amino acids combine to shape a protein.

The simplest pattern is the division between core and surface. Hydrophobic residues tend to be buried, while polar and charged residues tend to be on the outside. A protein sequence therefore often shows alternating stretches, with hydrophobic residues in one face of a helix or strand and polar residues in the other. Such patterns can be seen by eye in a sequence and are used by predictive methods to estimate the fold.

Kyte and Doolittle (1982) developed their profile to make these patterns visible. Stretches of high hydrophobicity mark regions likely to be buried, while stretches of low hydrophobicity mark regions likely to be exposed. Their method showed that the sequence contains information about the shape, even before the structure is known.

Pauling, Corey and Branson (1951) explained how the backbone's regular geometry gives rise to helices and sheets. In a helix, side chains point out at a regular angle, so a repeating pattern of hydrophobic and polar residues can put all the hydrophobic residues on one face. The helix then packs against other helices with its hydrophobic face inward, a common architecture in proteins.

Chothia (1992) described the families of proteins as sharing characteristic patterns of residues that give them their folds. The same kind of fold can be built from different sequences, as long as the pattern of hydrophobic and polar residues fits the architecture. This is why distant relatives can have similar shapes despite low sequence similarity.

The properties of amino acids also determine what a protein binds. A binding site is a surface pocket lined with residues whose side chains fit a partner molecule. Hydrophobic residues in the pocket can welcome a nonpolar partner, while polar and charged residues can hold a partner through hydrogen bonds and salt bridges. The chemistry of the pocket is set by the amino acids that form it.

A protein may also behave differently in different environments. A change in pH can alter the charge of histidine, a change in salt can weaken salt bridges, and a change in temperature can disturb the hydrophobic core. Such changes can switch a protein between active and inactive forms, so the cell can control the function of a protein by changing its surroundings.

Students should appreciate that the sequence is the starting point, not the whole story. The final shape depends on the interactions among all the residues, on the environment and on the help of other proteins. The amino acid properties describe the tendencies, and the protein's final form emerges from the balance of many tendencies together.

A useful picture is a team of people with different skills. Each amino acid brings its own property, and the team's success depends on how the members are arranged and how they work together. The hydrophobic members form the inner core, the polar and charged members face the public, and a few special members shape the turns and the binding sites.

To summarize, the pattern of hydrophobic, polar and charged residues in a sequence shapes where a protein folds, which parts face water and where a partner binds. Patterns can be read from sequences, and the same properties explain why proteins behave differently under different conditions. The next chapter shows how cells control these properties through the chemistry of their amino acids.
