Most of the variation described so far comes from splicing, which chooses which parts of a message to keep. "RNA editing" is a different kind of change. It alters the letters of the message itself, so that the sequence read by the ribosome differs from the sequence written in the gene. Because the DNA is unchanged, editing adds diversity without any change to the genome, and it can be switched on or off by the cell.

Editing is carried out by specific enzymes that recognize a target site in the RNA and change one nucleotide into another. In one well-known class, an enzyme converts adenosine into inosine, which the cell reads as guanosine during translation. In another, an enzyme removes a single cytidine and changes it into uridine. Each enzyme has a defined target, and the change it makes is precise, which means that editing is a controlled process rather than a random error.

Gott and Emeson (2000) reviewed the functions and mechanisms of these reactions and described how they are used across organisms. The review showed that editing is not a rare curiosity but a widespread means of altering the protein that a message encodes. Some edits change a single amino acid, while others change a codon into a stop signal that shortens the protein.

The best-known example in mammals involves a gene for a protein that carries fats in the blood. In the liver, the message is read in full and produces a long form of the protein. In the intestine, an enzyme edits one cytidine in the same message, creating a stop signal that cuts the protein short. The two tissues therefore make different proteins from the same gene, and the difference is produced entirely by editing.

Kornblihtt et al. (2013) placed editing in the wider context of message processing. Like splicing, editing acts on the precursor message and is part of a sequence of steps that shape the final product. The processes are not separate, and their combined effects can determine which protein is made in a particular tissue.

For a beginner, editing can be pictured as a typist correcting a document after it has been printed. The original text remains in the file, but a few letters are changed on the copy that is read. The reader then sees a different sentence, even though the underlying document has not been rewritten. This comparison also explains why editing is easier to control than changing the source itself.

Editing matters for brain function too. Some editing enzymes act on the messages of receptors that respond to chemical signals in the nervous system, and small changes in these receptors can alter how strongly the neurons respond. Because the brain relies on precise signalling, even a small shift in editing can have effects that are measurable in behavior.

Editing has also been linked to disease. When editing enzymes are missing or overactive, the messages they should change may be left unedited or edited too often. Such imbalances can alter the function of proteins that control growth, immunity or the response to injury. Researchers are therefore interested in editing as both a source of normal variation and a possible contributor to illness.

Editing is generally less common than splicing, but its effects can be precise. Gott and Emeson (2000) emphasized that each edit is directed by a specific target site, which is why the changes can be so exact. A single changed letter can alter one amino acid, which may be enough to change how a protein folds, binds a partner or responds to a signal. This makes editing a useful tool for fine-tuning protein function without altering the gene.

It is useful to compare editing with the other sources of diversity. Splicing decides which parts of the message are kept. Promoters and end points decide where the message starts and stops. Editing changes the letters within the message. Each mechanism works at a different point, and together they give the cell several independent ways to vary the proteins it makes.

To summarize this chapter, RNA editing changes individual nucleotides in a message after transcription, altering the protein without changing the gene. Enzymes such as those that edit adenosine and cytidine act at defined sites, and the best-known example produces two forms of a blood-lipid protein in different tissues. The next chapter describes how proteins are modified after they are made.
