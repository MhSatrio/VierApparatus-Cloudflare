Alternative splicing changes the middle of a message, but a gene can also vary at its two ends. The start of the message is decided by the "promoter", the region of DNA where transcription begins, and the end is decided by the signal where the message is cut and a tail of adenine nucleotides is added. A gene may have more than one promoter or more than one end point, and each choice can produce a different message from the same gene.

A promoter is the docking site for RNA polymerase and the general transcription machinery. Many genes have a single promoter, but some have several, located at different places along the DNA. Each promoter starts transcription at a different point, so the first exon of the resulting message can differ. If the first exon is part of the protein's start region, a different promoter can change where the protein begins, and this in turn can change where the protein goes in the cell.

Kornblihtt et al. (2013) described how the start of transcription and the processing of the message are linked. The choice of promoter influences which exons are available for splicing, and the processing machinery can respond to the way that transcription is organized. Promoter choice and splicing are therefore not separate decisions, but parts of one coordinated program that shapes the message.

The other end of the message is also variable. After transcription, the message must be cut at a signal known as the polyadenylation site, and a tail of adenine nucleotides is added. Many genes have more than one such site. A message that is cut at an early site is shorter than one cut at a later site, and the two versions can differ in their stability, their location in the cell and the proteins they can make.

The difference at the end of the message can also matter because of the sequence that remains. A longer tail region may contain signals that bind regulatory molecules, while a shorter one may escape them. The cell can therefore influence how much of a protein is made by choosing one end point over another, even when the coding sequence is the same.

Nilsen and Graveley (2010) pointed out that alternative choices of this kind, together with splicing, expand the range of proteins that a genome can supply. Each choice adds another layer of variation, and the combination of choices at the start, middle and end of a message can produce many distinct versions from a single gene.

For a student, the message can be pictured as a ribbon with a beginning, a middle and an end. The beginning is where the ribbon is cut from a roll, the middle is where pieces may be cut out, and the end is where the ribbon is finished and sealed. A craftsperson can choose where to start the cut, which pieces to remove and where to finish, and each decision produces a different ribbon from the same roll.

These choices are regulated. As with splicing, the decisions depend on signals in the RNA that bind regulatory proteins, a pattern described by Black (2003). A cell does not select promoters and end points at random, but responds to its needs. Some promoters are active only in certain tissues or during certain stages of development, and polyadenylation sites can be used more or less often depending on the state of the cell. The regulation of these choices is therefore an important part of how a cell adapts.

Changes at the start and end of a message can have clear effects on protein structure. A promoter that begins transcription inside the coding region can remove the first part of a protein, which may remove a signal sequence that directs the protein to a membrane. Similarly, a message with an early end point may produce a shortened protein that lacks a domain needed for binding a partner.

Researchers study these effects by measuring where transcription begins and where messages end. Techniques that map transcription start sites and polyadenylation sites reveal which versions are actually made, rather than which are possible. This information is important for understanding gene regulation and for interpreting changes in gene expression observed in disease.

To summarize this chapter, alternative promoters and polyadenylation sites change the start and end of a message, adding to the diversity produced by a single gene. These choices are linked to splicing and are regulated by the state of the cell. The next chapter describes RNA editing, which can change the message after it has been made.
