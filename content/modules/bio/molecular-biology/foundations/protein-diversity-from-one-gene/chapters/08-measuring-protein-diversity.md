Describing protein diversity requires measurement at two levels: the messages that a cell makes and the proteins that those messages produce. Each level can reveal different information, and the two must be compared with care. A message that is abundant does not guarantee a large amount of protein, and a protein that is present may be in a modified or cut form that the message alone does not show.

The most common way to study messages is "RNA sequencing". The messages in a sample are copied into DNA, the DNA is read by a sequencing machine, and the sequences are matched to the gene map. Reads that cross an exon boundary reveal which exons were joined, and the pattern of reads across a gene shows how much of each isoform is present. This approach transformed the study of splicing, because it allowed thousands of messages to be read in a single experiment.

Pan et al. (2008) showed how powerful this method is. By sequencing the messages of human cells in depth, they could see the many ways that exons were joined and estimate how common each pattern was. Their work turned alternative splicing from an occasional curiosity into a feature that could be measured across the whole transcriptome.

Measurements also differ between tissues. Wang et al. (2008) compared messages from several human tissues and found that the relative amounts of isoforms varied considerably from one organ to another. A measurement made in one tissue therefore cannot be assumed to apply to another, and researchers must choose their samples with the question in mind.

Proteins are harder to measure than messages, because they are chemically varied and often present in very different amounts. The main tool for studying them is mass spectrometry. A protein sample is cut into peptides, the peptides are ionized and their masses are measured. By matching the measured masses to a database of predicted peptides, researchers can identify the proteins present and detect many of their modifications.

Mann and Jensen (2003) explained how mass spectrometry can be used to find modifications on specific sites. A small chemical addition changes a peptide's mass in a way that can be detected, and the position of the addition can be found by breaking the peptide into smaller pieces. This allows researchers to map modifications at high resolution, although the work is technically demanding.

For a beginner, measuring a protein sample can be compared with reading a list of ingredients from a finished meal. The list tells you which ingredients were used, but it does not tell you how each one was cooked, sliced or seasoned. Mass spectrometry identifies the ingredients and some of the preparation, but a full picture requires several kinds of evidence.

Antibodies offer another route. An antibody binds to one part of a protein, and the binding can be detected with a fluorescent or enzymatic label. Because an antibody can be designed to recognize one isoform or one modified form, it can show whether a particular version is present in a tissue or cell. The method is useful for checking results obtained by other methods, but antibodies can also bind to the wrong target, so their specificity must be tested.

Each method has limits. Sequencing can show which messages are present but may not reveal whether they are translated. Mass spectrometry can identify proteins but may miss those that are rare or hard to ionize. Antibody methods depend on the quality of the reagent. Combining the methods gives a stronger picture than any one alone.

Interpreting the data requires caution. A change in the proportion of isoforms may reflect a difference in the cell's state, a difference in the sample's handling or a difference in how the messages were read. Researchers therefore use controls, repeat experiments and compare results across labs, so that the conclusions rest on evidence that holds up under testing.

The field is moving toward measuring diversity in single cells and in living tissue. These methods reveal which combinations of messages and proteins occur together, and they show how diversity varies from cell to cell. This detail is important, because the average of a tissue can hide large differences between the cells that make it up.

To summarize this chapter, protein diversity is measured by sequencing messages, by mass spectrometry of proteins and by antibody methods, and each has strengths and limits. Combining them gives the most reliable picture of the isoforms and modified forms a cell makes.
