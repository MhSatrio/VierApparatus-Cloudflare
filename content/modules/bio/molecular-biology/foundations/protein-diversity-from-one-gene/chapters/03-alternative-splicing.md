"Alternative splicing" is the process by which one precursor messenger RNA is joined in more than one way, so that a single gene produces several mature messages. Each version, called an "isoform", can contain a different set of exons, and each isoform can be translated into a protein with a different sequence. The same gene therefore becomes a family of related proteins rather than a single product.

The most common patterns are simple to describe. In "exon skipping", an exon is removed along with the introns around it, so that the mature message lacks that stretch. In "mutually exclusive exons", two exons are alternatives, and the cell includes one or the other but not both. In "alternative splice sites", the boundary of an exon is shifted, so that a few extra or fewer nucleotides are kept. In "intron retention", an intron is left in the message instead of being removed. Each pattern changes the protein in a different way.

Black (2003) explained that these choices are made by the same spliceosome that handles ordinary splicing. The difference lies in the signals. Strong and weak splice sites compete with one another, and proteins that bind enhancer or silencer sequences in the pre-mRNA can tip the balance toward one exon or another. The result is a regulated decision, not a random one.

The scale of this process became clear with sequencing. Pan et al. (2008) used high-throughput sequencing to read the messages of human cells in great detail. They found that most genes with several exons produce more than one version of the message, and that many of these versions differ in ways that could change a protein's function. This finding showed that alternative splicing is a central feature of human gene expression.

Alternative splicing also depends on where the cell is. Wang et al. (2008) compared messages from many human tissues and found that the same gene often produces different isoforms in different organs. A gene that makes one version of a protein in the brain may make another version in the liver. The tissue, not only the gene, decides which protein is made.

For a student, a helpful picture is a set of building blocks. Each exon is a block, and the cell can choose which blocks to snap together. The same box of blocks can make a tower, a bridge or a house, depending on which pieces are used and in what order. Alternative splicing gives the cell a box of blocks that it can assemble in many forms.

The effects on proteins can be large. Removing an exon may delete a region that binds another molecule, and the protein may no longer work with its partner. Adding a new exon may insert a stretch that changes where the protein is placed in the cell. In some cases, two isoforms have opposite effects, one promoting a process and the other blocking it, so the balance between them controls the outcome.

Alternative splicing also affects the stability of the message. Some isoforms contain a premature stop signal that marks them for rapid destruction. The cell can therefore control how much of a protein is made by changing the proportion of isoforms that survive. This is a form of regulation that works even before the protein is built.

Errors in this process are linked to disease. A mutation that changes a splice site can cause an exon to be skipped or an intron to be kept, and the resulting protein may be unable to function. Many inherited conditions have been traced to such errors, and researchers now look for splicing changes when a gene looks normal but a disease is present.

The key lesson is that the number of proteins a cell can make is set by its genes and also by its choices about how those genes are edited. The same DNA can produce a range of messages, and the particular set made in each cell is shaped by the signals that the cell reads and responds to.

To summarize this chapter, alternative splicing joins the same precursor in different ways to produce several isoforms from one gene. Its main patterns are exon skipping, mutually exclusive exons, shifted splice sites and intron retention. The next chapter describes how choices at the start and end of a message add further diversity.
