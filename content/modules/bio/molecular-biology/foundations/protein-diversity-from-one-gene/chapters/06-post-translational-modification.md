Once a protein has been built from its amino acids, it is often changed further. A chemical group may be added to one of its side chains, a sugar may be attached, or a small piece of the chain may be removed. These changes are called "post-translational modifications", because they happen after translation, the step in which the ribosome builds the chain. They add another layer of diversity, since the same protein sequence can exist in many modified forms.

Walsh et al. (2005) described the chemistry of these modifications in detail. They include the addition of phosphate groups, sugars, acetyl groups and lipids, as well as the linking of small proteins such as ubiquitin. Each modification uses a specific enzyme, and each enzyme recognizes a particular site on the protein. The result is a set of chemical marks that can be placed, read and removed in a controlled way.

Phosphorylation is the most widely studied example. An enzyme called a kinase attaches a phosphate group to a serine, threonine or tyrosine residue, and the added charge can change the shape of the protein. The change can switch an enzyme on or off, or it can create a docking site for another protein. Because phosphate groups can be removed by other enzymes, phosphorylation acts as a switch that can be turned back and forth.

Glycosylation is another common change. Sugar chains are attached to some proteins while they are being made or after they are finished, and these chains can affect how the protein folds, how stable it is and where it goes in the cell. Many proteins that sit on the outside of cells carry sugar chains, and these sugars help cells recognize one another.

Mann and Jensen (2003) explained how these changes are detected. Mass spectrometry measures the mass of protein fragments with great precision, and a small addition such as a phosphate group alters the mass in a measurable way. By comparing masses before and after treatment, researchers can identify which sites carry a modification and estimate how much of the protein is modified.

Hunter (2007) showed that modifications often work together. Phosphorylation and ubiquitination, for example, can influence one another, so that the addition of one mark makes another more or less likely. This "crosstalk" means that a protein's state is set by a combination of marks rather than by a single change, which gives the cell a rich vocabulary for control.

For a student, a helpful picture is a label on a jar. The jar and its contents stay the same, but the label can be added, changed or removed to tell people how the jar should be used. A protein with several modifications is like a jar with several labels, each one changing how the contents are handled.

These changes influence a protein's life in many ways. A modification may determine whether a protein binds to DNA, whether it stays in the nucleus or moves to the membrane, or how long it survives before being broken down. Because these effects are so varied, a single gene can produce proteins with very different behaviors depending on how they are modified.

Modifications are also tied to disease. Many cancers involve changes in the enzymes that add or remove phosphate groups, and some inherited conditions affect the addition of sugar chains. Drugs that target kinases are now an important part of medicine, and they work by changing the phosphorylation state of specific proteins.

Modifications are not always permanent. Some are added and removed many times in a short period, while others are present only at certain stages of a cell's life. This dynamic behavior allows the cell to respond quickly to new conditions, and it is one reason why measuring the state of a protein at a single moment can give an incomplete picture.

To summarize this chapter, post-translational modifications add chemical groups or remove pieces of proteins after they are made, and they can switch activity, change location and alter stability. Phosphorylation and glycosylation are common examples, and modifications can work together. The next chapter describes how proteins are cut and trimmed after translation.
