A human genome holds roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes, yet the body contains far more distinct proteins than that. Muscle, blood, brain and skin each rely on thousands of different proteins, and many of them are closely related but not identical. If every gene made exactly one protein, the number of proteins would be about the same as the number of genes, and the complexity of living things would be much harder to explain.

The answer is that a single gene can give rise to several different proteins. A gene is first copied into a precursor message, and that message can be cut and joined in more than one way before it is read. Other steps can change the protein after it has been made, by adding chemical groups, cutting it into pieces or editing the message itself. Together these steps multiply the diversity that a fixed set of genes can produce.

Large-scale sequencing has shown that this multiplication is not a rare exception. Nilsen and Graveley (2010) argued that alternative splicing greatly expanded the proteome of complex organisms, so that a similar set of genes could make a much larger set of proteins. Their argument suggests that the expansion is one of the main reasons complex animals can have so many protein types, even when their gene counts are not very different from those of simpler organisms.

Early evidence for this came from comparing messages with their genes. Pan et al. (2008) used high-throughput sequencing to survey the splicing patterns of human cells and found that most genes with several exons produce more than one version of the message. This changed how biologists thought about genes. A gene came to be seen as a template with options rather than a single fixed instruction.

Diversity also depends on where the gene is working. Wang et al. (2008) examined many human tissues and showed that the same gene can produce different versions in different organs. The brain, liver and muscle may read a shared gene in different ways, so the protein diversity of the body is organized by tissue as well as by gene.

For a beginner, it helps to picture a recipe book. Each gene is like a recipe, but the cook can choose which steps to follow, which ingredients to add and whether to garnish the dish afterwards. The same recipe can produce several dishes, and the final meal depends on the choices made during cooking as well as on the written instructions.

Diversity matters for evolution too. A new protein variant produced by a change in splicing can appear without any change to the DNA sequence of the gene itself. This allows cells to try new versions of a protein, keep the ones that work and adjust the balance between versions as conditions change. Evolution can therefore act on the way genes are read, not only on the genes themselves.

Protein diversity is not random, however. Cells control which versions are made, and when this control fails, disease can follow. A splicing error can remove a part of a protein that is needed for its function, and some inherited conditions are caused by mistakes in how a gene is read rather than by a broken gene. Understanding diversity is therefore part of understanding health.

Scientists also have to be careful about what they count. A message that is detected in a sample does not always lead to a stable protein, and some versions are rapidly destroyed by the cell. The number of possible proteins is therefore larger than the number actually made in any one cell at any one time, and measuring the real output requires more than reading the genes.

This module follows the path from gene to diverse protein, step by step. Chapter 2 describes how a gene is copied into messenger RNA. Chapter 3 covers alternative splicing in detail. Chapters 4 and 5 describe changes at the start and inside the message. Chapters 6 and 7 cover changes to proteins after they are made, and Chapter 8 explains how scientists measure protein diversity.

To summarize this chapter, one gene can give rise to many proteins because its message can be processed in different ways and its protein can be changed after it is made. This is why the number of proteins far exceeds the number of genes. The next chapter describes how a gene is copied into messenger RNA.
