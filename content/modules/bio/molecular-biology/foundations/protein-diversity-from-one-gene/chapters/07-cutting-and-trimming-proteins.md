Some proteins are made in a longer form than they are used. The chain is produced first as a precursor, and a specific piece is then cut away by an enzyme called a protease. This cutting is called "proteolytic processing", and it can turn an inactive precursor into an active protein, produce several different proteins from one chain or remove a signal that is no longer needed. Like splicing and modification, it is a way for one gene to yield more than one product.

A classic example is the digestive enzyme trypsin. Trypsin is made as an inactive precursor called trypsinogen, which is stored in the pancreas. When trypsinogen reaches the small intestine, a protease removes a short piece from its end, and the molecule changes shape into the active enzyme. The inactive form protects the pancreas from digesting its own tissue before the enzyme is needed.

Insulin follows a similar path. The first form made from its gene is a long precursor, which is trimmed to a shorter form and then cut again. The final hormone is made of two chains joined by bonds, and a connecting piece is removed along the way. The cut-away piece has no hormone activity, but it is released alongside the hormone and can be used as a marker of how much insulin the body has made.

Walsh et al. (2005) grouped proteolytic processing with the other chemical changes that create proteome diversity. Cutting a chain is one of the most drastic changes a protein can undergo, because it removes a region permanently. The change cannot be reversed, which means that the timing and location of the cut are especially important for the cell.

Mann and Jensen (2003) noted that cut proteins can be detected with the same mass measurements used for modifications. When a protein is cut, the fragments have masses that depend on where the cut was made. By identifying the fragments, researchers can work out where the processing occurs and which forms of a protein are present in a sample.

For a beginner, proteolytic processing can be pictured as trimming a sheet of paper before it is pinned to a board. The paper is cut to fit the space, and the trimmed piece is the one that does the job. The offcuts are discarded, and the final shape depends on the place that the cut is made. A small change in where the knife falls can change the result.

Cutting is also used to control the destruction of proteins. A protein may be marked with a small tag called ubiquitin, and the tagged protein is then recognized and broken down by a large cellular machine. Hunter (2007) explained that ubiquitin marking and phosphorylation can work together, so that a phosphate mark can decide whether a protein is tagged for destruction or left to function.

Processing can also change where a protein goes. Some precursors carry a signal sequence at one end that directs them to a membrane or to a compartment in the cell. Once they arrive, the signal is cut away, and the mature protein stays in its new location. Without this cut, the protein might remain attached to the membrane or be sent to the wrong place.

Errors in processing matter for health. If a precursor is cut too early or too late, the resulting protein may be inactive, too active or unable to reach its target. Some diseases involve the uncontrolled release of active proteases, which can damage tissues, and others involve the failure to remove a signal that should be discarded.

Proteolytic processing is therefore an important source of diversity and control. It allows a cell to keep a protein ready in an inactive form, to activate it when needed and to remove it when its job is done. The cut is a one-time decision, but it sets the destiny of the protein from that point onward.

To summarize this chapter, proteolytic processing cuts precursor proteins to produce active forms, separate products or proteins in the correct location. Trypsin and insulin are classic examples, and the cut is permanent. The next chapter describes how scientists measure protein diversity in the laboratory.
