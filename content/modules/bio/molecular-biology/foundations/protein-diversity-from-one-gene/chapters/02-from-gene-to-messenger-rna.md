The first step in reading a gene is transcription. An enzyme called RNA polymerase reads one strand of the DNA and builds a matching molecule of RNA, which is a chain of nucleotides related to DNA but single stranded and usually shorter lived. In a eukaryotic cell, the first RNA copy made from a protein-coding gene is called the precursor messenger RNA, or pre-mRNA, and it is not yet ready to be read by the protein-making machinery.

A typical gene is made of "exons", which are the parts that will eventually appear in the mature message, and "introns", which are the stretches that are removed. The pre-mRNA therefore contains both kinds of sequence. Before it can be used, the introns must be cut out and the exons joined together. This removal and joining is called splicing, and it is one of the most important processing steps in the life of a gene.

Black (2003) described how the cell recognizes the boundaries of each intron. Short signals at the start and end of an intron, together with a branch point sequence inside it, are read by a large complex called the spliceosome. The spliceosome is built from small nuclear ribonucleoproteins, which are RNA and protein assemblies that pair with the pre-mRNA. The complex then cuts the message at the correct sites and joins the neighboring exons.

Splicing is not the only processing. The end of the pre-mRNA receives a chain of adenine nucleotides in a step called polyadenylation, which protects the message and helps it leave the nucleus. The start of the message receives a chemical cap, which helps the ribosome recognize it later. These steps are carried out while the pre-mRNA is still being made, so that the processing of a message begins before the RNA polymerase has finished reading the gene.

This timing is important. Kornblihtt et al. (2013) emphasized that transcription and splicing are linked, so that the speed at which RNA polymerase moves along a gene can influence which exons are included. The processing machinery is therefore not a separate stage that follows transcription, but part of the same continuous process. Black (2003) also described the signals that the spliceosome reads while the message is still being made, which fits this view.

Once processing is complete, the mature messenger RNA is exported from the nucleus to the cytoplasm. There the ribosome reads its sequence in groups of three nucleotides, each group specifying one amino acid. The order of the amino acids is then determined by the mature message, which is why the choices made during splicing and processing matter so much for the final protein.

For a beginner, it helps to picture a film being edited before it is shown. The raw footage contains many scenes, some of which will be cut, and the editor decides which scenes to keep and in what order. The film that reaches the audience depends on those editing decisions, and two editors working on the same footage can produce two different films.

The editing of messages is not a random process. Each intron and exon carries signals that the spliceosome reads, and additional signals bind proteins that either encourage or discourage the use of a given site. These regulatory sequences are often called enhancers and silencers, and they act like notes in the margin of the film, telling the editor which scenes to keep.

Mistakes in processing have real consequences. If the wrong site is used, the message may gain or lose a stretch of code, which can shift the reading frame or insert a stop signal. A cell has checks that catch many errors, but some faulty messages are still translated, and the resulting proteins may be shorter, longer or unable to fold.

It is also important to remember that the pre-mRNA is a precursor, not the final product. A researcher who reads only the gene sequence learns the full set of possibilities but not the message that was actually made. The processing steps decide which possibilities are realized in a particular cell, at a particular time, in a particular tissue.

To summarize this chapter, a gene is copied into a precursor messenger RNA, which is then cut, joined, capped and tailed before it can be read. The spliceosome performs the cutting and joining, and transcription and processing are closely linked. The next chapter describes alternative splicing, in which the same precursor is edited in more than one way.
