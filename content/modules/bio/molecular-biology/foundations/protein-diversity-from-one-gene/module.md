## Overview

A human genome holds roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes, yet the body contains far more distinct proteins than that. This module explains how a single gene can give rise to several different proteins. The first step is "alternative splicing", in which the message copied from a gene is assembled in different ways. Editing can change the message further, and proteins can be modified or trimmed after they are made. Together these steps multiply the diversity that a fixed set of genes can produce.

Each important term appears in quotation marks the first time it is used, with a plain explanation. The module builds on the Protein Folding Basics module, because a changed protein must still fold correctly to do its work.

Readers do not need prior knowledge of splicing or modification. Each chapter builds on the one before it, and each ends with a short summary that points to the next topic. Examples are drawn from familiar proteins such as digestive enzymes and insulin, and the references at the front of this module lead to the original studies for readers who want more detail.

The eight chapters follow the route from gene to protein. The first sets out the puzzle. The second describes how a gene is copied into messenger RNA. The third covers alternative splicing, and the fourth covers choices at the start and end of the message. The fifth describes RNA editing. The sixth and seventh cover changes to proteins after they are made, and the eighth explains how scientists measure protein diversity.
