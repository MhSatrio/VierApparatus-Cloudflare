Genomes come in very different sizes. Some bacteria have genomes of a few million base pairs, while some plants and amphibians have genomes many times larger than the human genome. Within the animals and plants themselves, the variation is just as wide. This chapter describes the range of genome sizes and explains one of the most surprising findings in genome biology: the size of a genome is not a reliable guide to the number of genes it contains.

The smallest genomes belong to bacteria and to some viruses. A typical free-living bacterium carries a genome of a few million base pairs, containing several thousand genes. Many of its genes are essential for life, and the genome is compact, with little repeated material between them. Small genomes of this kind are efficient, because the cell can copy them quickly, and the genes are tightly packed.

Larger genomes are found in eukaryotes. Yeast has a genome of about twelve million base pairs, while the human genome contains about three billion. The International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium (2001) provided the first detailed picture of the human genome, and it showed that the increase in size over yeast does not reflect a proportional increase in genes. The human genome has a few times more genes than yeast, but it is hundreds of times larger.

Some plants and amphibians have genomes that are far larger than the human genome. Certain flowering plants carry genomes many times the size of ours, and some lungfish have genomes that are larger still. These enormous genomes are not explained by having more genes. Instead, most of the extra DNA consists of repeated sequences, transposable elements and other noncoding material that has accumulated over evolutionary time.

The mismatch between genome size and gene number is one of the most important lessons of comparative genomics. It shows that the genome contains much more than protein-coding information. The extra DNA may include regulatory regions, structural elements, functional RNA genes and sequences whose role is still unclear. The genome is not a simple list of genes, and its size reflects the history of how it has grown and been reshaped.

Eddy (2001) argued that the existence of many noncoding RNA genes contributes to the complexity of genomes that is not captured by a gene count. Functional RNAs, including transfer RNAs, ribosomal RNAs and the many small RNAs described in the RNA module, add to the functional content of a genome. Some of this content is small in size but large in importance, which means that the size of a genome is only a partial measure of its function.

The ENCODE Project Consortium (2012) found that much of the human genome is biochemically active, even though much of it does not encode proteins. Their analysis suggested that the noncoding part of the genome is involved in regulating genes in many ways. This finding helps to explain why the human genome is so large, while the protein-coding fraction remains modest.

Why do genomes vary so much in size? One explanation is that the amount of repeated DNA has grown in some lineages, through the copying of transposable elements and through duplications of segments of DNA. Another explanation is that the loss of DNA has been more active in some lineages than in others. The balance between gain and loss over long periods determines the size of the genome, and this balance differs between species.

It is useful to picture genome size as the size of a storage facility. A large facility may contain many useful items, or it may contain a lot of material that is rarely used. Size alone does not tell you how much is useful, and the same is true of the genome. A large genome may be full of valuable instructions, or it may carry a great deal of baggage from its history.

Genome size has practical implications for research. Large genomes are more expensive and time-consuming to sequence, and their analysis requires more computing. The size of a genome also affects the design of experiments, because a large genome has more regions that might be mistaken for genes. Researchers must take these differences into account when they compare species or plan new studies.

Students should note that genome size does not measure complexity in a simple way. Some organisms with very large genomes are not more complex in their body plan than organisms with small genomes. Complexity depends on the way the genome is used, including the regulation of genes, the organization of chromatin and the networks of interactions among proteins and RNAs. The genome is a foundation, not a complete measure of biological organization.

Comparing genome sizes can also be misleading if the measurements are made in different ways. Early estimates were based on the amount of DNA in a cell, measured by staining and by physical methods, and these estimates were sometimes revised when sequencing became possible. The most reliable comparisons now use complete sequences, but for many species only partial sequences exist. Researchers must keep these limits in mind when they interpret differences in size.

Genome size is also linked to the biology of the organism. Some species with large genomes reproduce slowly, and some with small genomes have fast life cycles. These patterns suggest that the cost of carrying extra DNA, and the time needed to copy it, may influence how genomes evolve. The relation between genome size and life history is an active subject of study, and it shows how genome structure can be connected to the way an organism lives. Each explanation must be tested against many species before it can be accepted.

To summarize, genome sizes range from a few million base pairs in bacteria to many billions in some plants and amphibians, and the number of genes does not scale with size. The difference reflects the accumulation of repeated and noncoding DNA over evolutionary time, and the functional content of the genome cannot be read from its size alone.
