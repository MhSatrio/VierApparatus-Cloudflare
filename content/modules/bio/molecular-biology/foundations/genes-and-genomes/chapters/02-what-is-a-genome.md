If a gene is a region of DNA that produces a product, the genome is the whole collection of DNA in an organism. It includes every chromosome in the nucleus, and in some organisms it also includes DNA in other compartments such as mitochondria or chloroplasts. The genome is the complete record that the organism carries, and it is the record from which all of its genes are read. This chapter explains what the genome is and what it contains.

The word genome combines the words gene and chromosome, and it was coined to name the complete set of genetic material of an organism. A genome is a fixed set of DNA molecules in a given cell type, although different cell types read different parts of it. A human liver cell and a human nerve cell have essentially the same genome, but they use different genes. The genome is therefore a library, and each cell reads a different selection of its books.

The International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium (2001) produced the first published draft of the human genome, a sequence of roughly three billion base pairs. The draft revealed the broad outline of the genome. It showed how many genes there are, how they are spread over the chromosomes and how much of the genome is made of repeated sequences. The project was a landmark, because it gave researchers a complete reference against which to study variation and disease.

A large part of the genome is not in genes at all. The sequencing showed that protein-coding exons make up only a small fraction of the sequence. The rest includes introns, regulatory regions, repeated elements, pseudogenes and regions whose function is not yet clear. The distinction between these parts is one of the central questions of modern genome biology, and it is discussed in later chapters of this module.

The ENCODE Project Consortium (2012) mapped many functional features of the genome in a wide range of cell types. Their work showed that much of the genome is transcribed or bound by regulatory proteins, which means that the genome is much more active than the protein-coding fraction suggests. The genome is therefore not a list of genes separated by silent spaces, but a densely organized system in which many regions have some function.

Eddy (2001) pointed out that the genome also contains genes for functional RNAs, which are part of the genome's record even though they do not produce proteins. Transfer RNA genes, ribosomal RNA genes and small RNA genes all contribute to the list of functional elements. The genome's content is therefore broader than its protein-coding catalogue.

The genome is also organized into chromosomes, and the chromosomes have their own structure. Each chromosome carries a centromere, which is needed for division, and telomeres at its ends, which protect the chromosome. Between these landmarks, the genes and other sequences are arranged along the chromosome in a particular order. The position of a gene matters, because neighboring genes and regulatory regions can influence each other.

Genome size is one of the most striking features of genomes. The number of base pairs varies enormously between species, and the number of genes does not always follow the size. Some organisms with very large genomes have relatively few genes, while others with smaller genomes have many. This mismatch tells us that the genome is not simply a collection of protein-coding genes, and that other kinds of DNA contribute to its size.

It helps to think of the genome as an archive. The archive contains the books that the organism uses, the notes that explain how to use them, the reference lists that link them together and the old drafts that were never removed. A reader of the archive must learn which parts are essential, which are supporting material and which are remnants of the past. Learning to read a genome is largely the process of sorting these parts.

For research, the genome provides a common reference. Scientists compare the genomes of different people to find variants linked to disease, compare the genomes of different species to trace evolution and compare the genomes of cells to see how they differ. Each comparison depends on the genome being a stable, well described object, even though its contents are read in different ways.

A final point is that the genome is a physical molecule as well as an information store. Its DNA is folded into chromosomes, wrapped in nucleosomes and modified by chemical marks. The physical state of the genome affects how it is read, so the genome cannot be fully described by its sequence alone. This is why genome biology draws on the ideas of the earlier modules on packaging and chromatin.

A useful way to start thinking about a genome is to ask what would be lost if it were damaged in a particular place. Some regions, such as a gene for an essential enzyme, are needed by every cell and cannot be lost without severe effects. Other regions, such as some repeated sequences, may be tolerated in many copies or may be absent in some individuals with little apparent effect. These differences show that the genome is not uniform in importance, and that its organization reflects the history of which parts have been kept and which have been allowed to drift.

The genome also differs between the cells of one individual in small ways. Some changes arise during development or later in life, and they can make one tissue differ slightly from another. Most cells carry the same genome, but the reading of that genome differs. Keeping the distinction between the fixed record and its changing use is one of the most useful habits in genome biology.

To summarize, the genome is the complete DNA of an organism, organized into chromosomes and containing genes, regulatory regions, repeated sequences and other elements. Most of it is not protein-coding, and its size varies widely across life. The next chapter looks at how genome size differs between species and what those differences mean.
