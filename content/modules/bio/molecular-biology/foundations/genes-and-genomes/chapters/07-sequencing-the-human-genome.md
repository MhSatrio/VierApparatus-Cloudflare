Reading the sequence of the human genome was one of the largest scientific projects of its time. The genome contains roughly three billion base pairs, and it had to be read accurately and then assembled into complete chromosomes. The effort involved laboratories around the world, new technologies and new computational methods. This chapter explains the general strategy that was used and what the project achieved.

Sequencing a genome requires breaking it into pieces that are small enough to read. The DNA is cut into fragments of a few hundred to a few thousand bases, each of which is copied and read with a sequencing method. The reads are then assembled by computer, which looks for overlaps between them and builds up longer and longer stretches of the sequence. The process is like reconstructing a book from a set of torn pages, where the overlaps between the pieces show how they fit together.

The International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium (2001) reported the first published draft of the human genome. The draft was produced by a large international effort that used two main approaches. One approach mapped the chromosomes first and then sequenced the mapped pieces, while the other sequenced random fragments and assembled them by computer. The two approaches were combined to produce a draft that covered most of the genome.

The draft revealed many features of the genome. It showed that the genome contains about twenty thousand to twenty-five thousand protein-coding genes, a number that surprised many researchers who had expected more. It showed that a large share of the genome is repetitive and that much of it is made of transposon-derived sequence. It also showed how genes are distributed unevenly along the chromosomes, with some regions dense in genes and others nearly empty.

The draft was not perfect. Some regions were difficult to read, especially those with long repeats, and some gaps remained in the assembly. Over the following years, researchers filled many of these gaps and corrected errors, producing more complete reference sequences. The draft therefore should be understood as a first step, rather than a finished product, and the reference genome has continued to improve as new technologies have appeared.

The ENCODE Project Consortium (2012) extended the work of sequencing by mapping the functional elements of the genome. Their project used the sequence as a reference and added information about where genes are transcribed, where regulatory proteins bind and where chromatin is open. Together, the sequence and the functional maps give a much richer picture of the genome than the sequence alone could provide.

Eddy (2001) noted that the sequence also revealed many noncoding RNA genes, which had been difficult to find without a complete genome. The sequencing project therefore changed the list of functional elements, adding RNAs that had been overlooked and making clear that the RNA world extends across the whole genome. The sequence became a catalogue not only of proteins but of the many RNAs that support the cell.

A reference genome has several uses. It gives a standard against which the genomes of individuals can be compared, so that variation can be identified and linked to traits or disease. It allows researchers to find the locations of genes and regulatory sequences, and to design experiments that target them. It also provides a basis for comparing the genomes of humans with those of other species, which helps to reveal the history of evolution.

In medicine, the reference genome has become essential. Doctors and researchers use it to interpret genetic tests, to identify the causes of rare diseases and to choose treatments that depend on the genetic profile of a patient. The cost of sequencing has dropped greatly since the first draft, and sequencing is now a practical tool in many clinical settings. The project's legacy is therefore present in everyday medical practice.

It helps to think of the reference genome as a map of a large country. The first version of the map showed the main roads and cities, and it had many blank areas and some errors. Later maps added details, corrected mistakes and included information about the terrain and the land use. The reference genome is a map of the same kind, and it is continually improved as researchers explore the territory.

The project also raised questions about data sharing and ethics. The sequence was made publicly available, which allowed researchers around the world to use it freely. Questions about the privacy of genetic information and about the rights of individuals to their own data have grown since then, and they remain important in genome research today.

Students should remember that the draft sequence was not a sequence of one person. It was assembled from several individuals, and it represents a composite reference rather than a single genome. Individual genomes differ, and the study of those differences is a major area of research. The reference genome is a starting point for understanding variation, not a complete description of every human.

Sequencing also showed the value of comparing genomes between species. Once the human sequence was known, it could be lined up with the genomes of mice, fish and other organisms, revealing blocks of sequence that are shared and blocks that are unique. This comparison helped to identify regulatory sequences and to understand how genes have changed over evolutionary time. The reference genome thus served as a standard for many species, and it turned individual sequencing projects into a connected body of knowledge.

The project also taught scientists how to organize large collaborations. Hundreds of researchers in many countries shared data, agreed on standards and checked each other's results. These habits of openness and coordination have since become common in genomics and in other large fields of biology.

To summarize, the human genome was sequenced by breaking the DNA into fragments, reading them and assembling the reads by computer. The first draft revealed the number and distribution of genes, the large share of repetitive DNA and the many features that later projects would map in detail. The reference genome now supports research and medicine. The final chapter of this module describes how genomes are used in practice.
