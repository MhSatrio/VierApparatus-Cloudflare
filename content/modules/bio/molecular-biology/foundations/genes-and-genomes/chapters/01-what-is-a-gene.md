The word gene is one of the most used and most debated terms in biology. In everyday speech it describes the reason a trait runs in a family. In the laboratory it names a specific place on a chromosome. Over the last century the scientific meaning has shifted several times, as new discoveries revealed how DNA is read and used. This chapter explains the modern meaning of a gene and why the older definitions are no longer enough.

The earliest scientific idea of a gene came from breeding experiments. Researchers saw that traits pass from parents to offspring in predictable ways, and they proposed that units of inheritance carry these traits. The gene was defined by its effect: it was whatever made a trait appear, skip a generation or segregate in a predictable ratio. The physical nature of the gene was unknown at the time, and no one could point to a stretch of DNA as the gene.

Once DNA was recognized as the carrier of heredity, the gene could be described in chemical terms. A gene became a sequence of DNA that is transcribed into an RNA molecule. For protein-coding genes, the RNA is then translated into a chain of amino acids. This definition captured the central idea of the central dogma, and it remains the starting point for most teaching today.

The modern definition has to be broader, however. The International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium (2001) reported the sequence of the human genome and found that only a small fraction of it encodes proteins. The rest was not obviously useful, at least for protein production, which raised the question of how to define a gene in a genome that contains so much noncoding DNA. Many scientists now define a gene as a region of DNA that produces a functional product, which may be a protein or an RNA.

The ENCODE Project Consortium (2012) extended this picture by showing that a large part of the genome is transcribed, including many regions that do not code for proteins. Some of these transcripts are regulatory, some are structural and some have unknown roles. The result is that the boundaries of a gene are no longer simple. A single region of DNA may give rise to several RNAs with different functions, depending on how it is processed.

Eddy (2001) described how noncoding RNA genes have been recognized as a major class. A noncoding RNA gene encodes a functional RNA molecule that is not translated into protein. Examples include transfer RNAs and ribosomal RNAs, which were known for decades, and many small and long RNAs discovered more recently. These genes are as real as protein-coding genes, even though they do not produce proteins.

A key modern insight is that a gene is often not a single continuous stretch of DNA. In eukaryotes, the coding part of a gene may be split into exons, with intervening introns that are removed from the RNA before it is used. The exons may be joined in different combinations, producing several versions of the product from one region. The gene is therefore a set of possible products, not one fixed message.

Consider how the definition applies to a typical gene. It has a promoter, a signal that tells RNA polymerase where to start. It has a coding region, made of exons, that specifies the product. It has regulatory regions, which may lie far away and which control how much product is made. It has an end, marked by signals that stop transcription. A useful definition of the gene includes all of these parts, not only the sequence that is eventually translated.

Students sometimes find it helpful to think of a gene as a recipe that can be used in several ways. The recipe is the DNA region, the cook is the transcription machinery, and the dishes are the products. The same recipe can yield different dishes depending on which parts are used, when they are used and how they are prepared. The recipe itself does not change, but its use does.

The modern definition also depends on the question being asked. A geneticist interested in inheritance may think of a gene as the unit that a mutation affects. A molecular biologist may think of it as the DNA region that is transcribed. A clinician may think of it as the source of a disease. Each view captures part of the truth, and a complete understanding brings them together.

The modern definition also has practical value for researchers who search for genes. Finding a gene in a genome means finding the signals that surround it, such as promoters, splice sites and stop signals, and checking whether a stretch of sequence is transcribed and translated. Computer programs do much of this work, but their results must be checked against experiments, because the boundaries of a gene are sometimes uncertain. A definition that is clear about function gives the researcher a way to decide which features belong to a gene and which do not.

A simple test can help a student apply the definition. Ask whether a region of DNA leads to a product that the cell uses, and whether that product has an effect when the region is changed. If both answers are yes, the region is part of a gene in the modern sense. If the region is only copied into RNA and then discarded with no effect, the answer is less clear, and the researcher must look for further evidence before calling it a gene.

To summarize the chapter, a gene began as a unit of inheritance defined by its effects, became a DNA sequence once the chemistry of heredity was known and now is understood as a region of DNA that produces a functional product, which may be a protein or an RNA. The gene may be split into exons, it may be regulated from a distance and it may give rise to several products.
