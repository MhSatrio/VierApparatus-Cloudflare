When the human genome was first sequenced, one of the most striking results was how little of it encodes proteins. The protein-coding exons amount to only about one or two percent of the sequence. The rest was called noncoding DNA, a term that was often used in a dismissive way, as if it were the leftover material of evolution with no purpose. Modern research has changed this view considerably. This chapter explains the difference between the coding and noncoding parts of the genome and why the noncoding part matters.

Protein-coding DNA is the part of the genome that specifies the amino acid sequence of proteins. In eukaryotes it is usually made of exons, which are the pieces of a gene that remain in the mature messenger RNA. The coding region of a gene is read in groups of three bases, and it ends at a stop codon. The coding DNA is the most intensively studied part of the genome, because it contains the blueprints for the proteins that do most of the work of cells.

The International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium (2001) estimated the number of protein-coding genes and found that the genome contains far fewer of them than many researchers had expected. The result emphasized that the regulation of genes and the diversity of their products must be explained by more than the number of genes alone. It also drew attention to the vast stretches of the genome that do not code for proteins.

Noncoding DNA is everything else. It includes introns, which lie within genes but are removed from the RNA; regulatory sequences, which control gene activity; repetitive elements, which are copies of mobile sequences; and regions that produce noncoding RNAs. Some noncoding DNA has been known to be functional for decades, while other parts were recognized only through large-scale sequencing and analysis.

The ENCODE Project Consortium (2012) showed that much of the noncoding genome is biochemically active. Regions that do not code for proteins are transcribed, bound by regulatory proteins or marked by chemical changes in chromatin. Their work suggested that a large fraction of the genome has some function, although the exact function of many regions is still uncertain. This finding has changed the view of noncoding DNA from a passive background to an active part of the genome.

Introns are an important example. They are sequences within genes that are copied into RNA but removed before the message is translated. Introns can be very long, sometimes making up most of a gene. Their removal, called splicing, is essential for producing a correct message, and some introns contain sequences that regulate how the splicing happens. Introns therefore have a functional role in the production of proteins, even though they do not themselves encode any amino acids.

Eddy (2001) described the noncoding RNA genes that form another important part of the noncoding genome. These genes produce functional RNAs, such as transfer RNAs and ribosomal RNAs, that are essential for translation. They also include the many small and long regulatory RNAs described in the RNA module. These genes are part of the functional content of the genome, even though their products are never translated.

Regulatory sequences are a third class. These include promoters, enhancers and silencers, which bind proteins that control transcription. Many regulatory sequences lie far from the genes they control, sometimes thousands of bases away or on another chromosome. Their presence in the noncoding genome shows that a large part of the genome's logic is about when and where genes are used, rather than what proteins they make.

The distinction between coding and noncoding DNA is not always sharp. Some regions overlap, and some noncoding RNAs are later found to encode small proteins. Some regions that were thought to be noncoding turn out to contain short open reading frames that can be translated under special conditions. The boundary is therefore a matter of ongoing investigation, and the simple division into coding and noncoding is a useful starting point rather than a final classification.

It helps to think of the coding DNA as the list of ingredients in a cookbook, and the noncoding DNA as the instructions for when and how to use them, the notes on the margins, the cross references between recipes and the unused pages that were kept for reasons no one remembers. Without the instructions and the notes, the list of ingredients would be of limited use. The coding part is necessary but not sufficient.

The practical consequences are large. Studies of genetic disease increasingly find that changes in noncoding DNA can cause illness, by altering regulatory sequences, splicing or the production of RNAs. Such changes are harder to interpret than changes in protein-coding genes, because the function of the noncoding region is often less clear. Researchers must therefore work out the roles of these regions before they can understand their effects.

Students should keep in mind that the word noncoding does not mean nonfunctional. It means that the DNA does not encode a protein. A noncoding region may still have a critical role in controlling genes, in building the structure of chromosomes or in producing a functional RNA. The term describes what the DNA does not do, not what it is good for.

The noncoding genome also shows how evolution works through change rather than design. Much of the noncoding sequence is shared between related species, which suggests that it has been kept by natural selection. Other parts differ greatly between species and may reflect changes that were tolerated without strong harm. Comparing these patterns helps researchers identify the regions that are most important, because functional sequences tend to be conserved while nonfunctional ones drift.

To summarize, protein-coding DNA is the part of the genome that specifies proteins, while noncoding DNA includes introns, regulatory sequences, repetitive elements and genes for functional RNAs. The noncoding part is now known to be highly active, and it is essential for the regulation of genes.
