A genome is useful only when it is read, compared and interpreted. Once the sequence of a genome is known, researchers can ask questions about how the organism works, how it evolved and how its genes contribute to disease. The genome has become a central tool in biology and medicine, and it is used in ways that its first sequencers could not have imagined. This final chapter of the module describes some of these uses and the issues they raise.

In research, genomes are compared across species to understand evolution. By lining up the sequences of related organisms, scientists can identify regions that have been conserved over millions of years, which are often functional, and regions that have changed rapidly, which may reflect adaptation. Comparative genomics has shown that many features of the human genome are shared with other mammals, and that some are unique to humans.

Genomes are also compared across individuals within a species. Each person carries small differences from the reference sequence, and these differences can be used to trace ancestry, to study population history and to identify variants linked to traits. The study of genetic variation has become one of the largest areas of genome research, and it depends on the reference genome as the common coordinate system.

The ENCODE Project Consortium (2012) showed how the functional map of a genome can be combined with variation data. By placing a variant on the map of regulatory elements, researchers can ask whether it lies in a region that is active in a particular cell type. This combined approach helps to interpret variants that lie outside protein-coding genes, which are the majority of variants found in a person.

In medicine, genome information is used to diagnose disease. A genetic test may look for known variants associated with a condition, or it may sequence a set of genes or the whole genome to search for changes that explain a patient's symptoms. The results are interpreted by comparing the patient's sequence with the reference genome and with databases of known variants. The reference genome is therefore essential to the accuracy of the test.

Rare diseases are a particular area where genome sequencing has made a difference. Many rare conditions have a genetic cause, but the cause may be a unique or very uncommon change that is hard to find without sequencing. Sequencing a patient and comparing the result with a reference can reveal the change, which may open the way to a diagnosis, to genetic counseling and in some cases to treatment.

Genome information also guides treatment choices. Some drugs work better, or cause fewer side effects, in people with particular genetic variants. Pharmacogenetic tests can help doctors choose the right drug and dose for a patient. The use of genome information in this way is a practical example of how basic research on the genome leads to changes in clinical care.

The International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium (2001) laid the groundwork for these applications, and its reference sequence remains the foundation for much of the medical use of genomes. Each new use depends on the accuracy and completeness of that reference, which is why ongoing improvements to the reference are so important.

Eddy (2001) reminded us that the genome contains many functional RNAs, and that some of the changes that matter for disease may lie in genes that do not produce proteins. Interpreting such changes requires knowledge of RNA function, as well as of the regulatory map. The medical use of genomes therefore draws on the whole range of genome biology described in this module.

The use of genome information raises ethical questions. Genetic information can reveal facts about a person's health, their ancestry and their relatives, and it may be used in ways that affect employment, insurance or family life. The privacy of genetic data must be protected, and people must understand what they agree to when their genome is tested or shared. Such questions are part of the broader discussion about how genomic medicine should be practiced.

Students should also appreciate the uncertainty in genome interpretation. A variant may be harmful, harmless or of unknown effect, and many variants are never fully classified. Interpreting genome information therefore requires caution, careful communication and a willingness to revise conclusions as new evidence arrives. The reference genome provides the starting point, but the judgment about what a variant means depends on much more evidence.

It helps to think of the genome as a language that people are only beginning to read fluently. The sequence is the text, the regulatory map is the grammar and the variation between people is the set of dialects. Learning to read this language is a long task, and each new reading reveals more about the text and about the people who speak it. The reference genome is the dictionary that makes the reading possible.

Genome information also supports the practice of prevention. Knowing that a person carries a variant linked to a disease can prompt earlier screening, lifestyle advice or monitoring for relatives who may share the variant. Such uses depend on clear communication, because a genetic result that is misunderstood can cause unnecessary worry or false reassurance. Trained counselors and clear reports help patients and families make sense of the information they receive.

Finally, the use of genomes in science depends on the quality of the data. Errors in sequencing, in assembly or in annotation can mislead researchers, and careful checking is essential. Responsible use of genome information therefore means knowing how the data were produced, what their limits are and how confident the conclusions can be.

To summarize, genomes are compared across species and individuals to study evolution, variation and disease. In medicine, genome information supports diagnosis, the treatment of rare conditions and the choice of drugs. The use of genome data depends on an accurate reference and raises important ethical questions about privacy and interpretation.
