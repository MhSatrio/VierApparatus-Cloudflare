A gene is not switched on or off by its own sequence alone. Its activity is controlled by regulatory sequences, short stretches of DNA that bind proteins which help or block transcription. These sequences are scattered through the genome, and many of them lie far from the genes they control. This chapter describes the main kinds of regulatory sequence, how they work and why they are so important for the way the genome functions.

The most familiar regulatory sequence is the promoter, which lies immediately upstream of a gene and marks the start of transcription. The promoter is recognized by RNA polymerase, often with the help of general transcription factors. Promoters vary in strength, and their sequence determines how easily the enzyme can start. A strong promoter starts transcription often, while a weak one starts it rarely.

Enhancers are regulatory sequences that increase the activity of a gene. They can lie many thousands of bases away from the promoter, sometimes upstream, sometimes downstream and sometimes within another gene. An enhancer binds activator proteins, and through looping of the DNA it brings those proteins into contact with the promoter. The looping allows a distant sequence to control a gene in a way that depends on the three-dimensional arrangement of the chromosome.

Silencers are the opposite. They bind repressor proteins that reduce transcription of a gene, sometimes by blocking the promoter and sometimes by recruiting proteins that compact the chromatin. Like enhancers, silencers can act at a distance, and they help define the set of cell types in which a gene is used. A gene may have both enhancers and silencers, and the balance between them sets its level of activity.

Insulators are a fourth class. These sequences bind proteins that act as boundaries, blocking the influence of enhancers on genes beyond the boundary and separating regions of different activity. Insulators help keep the genome organized into domains, so that a gene in one region is not switched on by an enhancer that belongs to a neighboring gene. They are part of the architecture that keeps the regulatory system orderly.

The ENCODE Project Consortium (2012) mapped many regulatory elements across the human genome and in a range of cell types. Their work showed that the number of such elements is very large, and that they are organized in complex patterns of activity. Different cell types use different sets of enhancers, which is one reason that cells with the same genome can behave so differently.

The International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium (2001) noted that the number of protein-coding genes is modest compared with the complexity of human biology. The regulatory sequences help to explain the gap. By combining a limited set of genes with a large and varied set of regulatory sequences, the genome can produce many different patterns of gene activity. The complexity lies largely in control, not in the number of genes.

Eddy (2001) discussed how the regulatory system extends to RNA as well as to DNA. Some regulatory RNAs bind to the promoters or enhancers of genes, and some act at the level of messages. The regulatory sequences of the genome therefore work together with RNA molecules to form a network, in which DNA elements and RNA molecules share the task of controlling gene activity.

It helps to think of the regulatory sequences as the stage lighting in a theatre. The genes are the actors, and the regulatory sequences decide which actors are lit, how brightly and at what time. Some lights are close to the stage, while others are placed high in the rigging and shine from a distance. The lighting does not perform the play, but it determines what the audience sees.

Mutations in regulatory sequences can have striking effects. A change in an enhancer may reduce the activity of a gene, even though the gene itself is perfectly normal. Such changes have been linked to many inherited conditions, and they are one reason that the noncoding genome is studied so intensively in medical research. Finding these changes is harder than finding changes in protein-coding regions, because the regulatory code is less easy to read.

Researchers identify regulatory sequences by several methods. They can look for sequence motifs that match known binding sites, they can test the activity of candidate sequences in reporter assays, and they can map proteins and chemical marks that indicate active regions. Each method gives a different kind of evidence, and combining them gives the most reliable picture of the regulatory map.

Students should note that the function of a regulatory sequence depends on context. The same enhancer may be active in one cell type and inactive in another, depending on which proteins are present. The sequence itself is only one part of the system, and its effect depends on the chromatin, the other regulatory elements and the state of the cell. This context-dependence is a key feature of the regulatory genome.

Regulatory sequences also help to explain how one gene can be used in many tissues. A single gene may have separate enhancers for different tissues, each responding to a different set of activator proteins. When one enhancer is damaged, the gene may still work elsewhere, but fail in a particular tissue. This modular design makes the regulation of genes flexible and allows evolution to change the use of a gene in one tissue without disturbing its use in others.

A useful practical point is that regulatory sequences are often more difficult to study than genes. Their effects may be small, their position may vary between individuals and their function may depend on the cell type. Researchers therefore combine computational predictions with laboratory tests before concluding that a sequence is an enhancer or silencer.

To summarize, promoters, enhancers, silencers and insulators are the main regulatory sequences of the genome. They control when and where genes are used, often from a distance and through the three-dimensional folding of chromosomes. Their combined activity gives the genome its ability to produce many patterns of gene use from a limited set of genes.
