If you could read a genome from end to end, you would find that many sequences recur again and again. Some repeats are short and occur in long runs, and others are long segments that appear thousands of times across the chromosomes. Together these repetitive sequences make up a large share of the human genome and an even larger share of some plant genomes. This chapter describes the main kinds of repetitive DNA and the mobile elements called transposons that have shaped the genome.

Repetitive DNA can be divided into two broad kinds. Tandem repeats are sequences that are repeated next to one another, such as a short motif copied many times in a row. Interspersed repeats are copies of a sequence scattered throughout the genome. Tandem repeats are often found near the centromeres and telomeres, and some of them are involved in disease when they expand. Interspersed repeats are mostly the product of transposons, which are the subject of much of this chapter.

Transposons are DNA sequences that can move from one place in the genome to another. They are often called jumping genes, although the name is a simplification. Some transposons move by a cut-and-paste mechanism, in which the element is excised from one site and inserted at another. Others move by a copy-and-paste mechanism, in which the element is first copied into RNA and then reverse transcribed into DNA at a new site, leaving the original in place.

The International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium (2001) found that a large share of the human genome consists of transposon-derived sequences. Many of these are ancient copies that have accumulated over millions of years and can no longer move. They are recognizable mainly through their shared sequences, which show that they came from the same source. The sequencing revealed that the genome is, in large part, a record of past transposon activity.

The copy-and-paste mechanism links transposons to reverse transcription, which was described in the central dogma module. The transposon is transcribed into RNA, and an enzyme reverse transcribes that RNA into DNA, which is then inserted into a new location. This mechanism is shared with retroviruses, and it is the reason that many scientists consider retrotransposons to be close relatives of viruses in the genome.

The ENCODE Project Consortium (2012) examined how transposon-derived sequences contribute to gene regulation. Some transposon fragments carry promoters or enhancers, and they can influence the activity of nearby genes. This means that transposons are not only passive passengers. They can supply new regulatory sequences, which the cell may then use for its own purposes.

Eddy (2001) noted that some transposon-derived sequences give rise to noncoding RNAs, and that others have been co-opted for functions in the cell. The discovery that transposons can contribute to the RNA world is one of the more surprising results of genome research. The genome's repetitive content is therefore part of its functional repertoire, even though much of it is ancient and degraded.

Repetitive DNA affects genome size dramatically. Each new copy of a transposon adds to the length of the genome, and because transposons can accumulate over long periods, they can enlarge a genome substantially. This is one of the main reasons that plant and amphibian genomes are so much larger than the human genome, even though they do not have correspondingly more genes.

Transposons also pose a risk to the cell. A transposon inserted into the middle of a gene can disrupt its function, and a transposon inserted near a gene can change its regulation. For this reason, cells have evolved mechanisms to silence transposons. Often the transposon DNA is packed into heterochromatin, and small RNAs help to direct the silencing. The balance between the benefits of transposition and the risks of disruption shapes the evolution of the genome.

Repeats also cause technical problems for genome research. Because the same sequence appears many times, it is hard to tell which copy a short read of DNA came from, and assembling a genome around repeats is difficult. Researchers have developed long-read sequencing and clever computational methods to address these problems, and the progress in handling repeats has been essential to producing complete genome sequences.

It helps to picture repetitive DNA as the wallpaper and carpet of a very old house. Some of it was laid down deliberately and still serves a purpose, while some was added by accident during renovations and has been trodden into the floor for centuries. A visitor who wants to understand the house must learn to distinguish the original design from the later additions. Transposons are the builders who keep adding new layers, some useful and some not.

Students should note that the word repetitive does not mean useless. Repetitive DNA includes structural elements of chromosomes, regulatory sequences, functional RNA genes and the raw material for evolutionary change. Its study has revealed how the genome grows, how new genes arise and how the cell protects itself against elements that could damage it.

The study of repeats has also revealed the ways that cells keep their genomes stable. Repeated sequences can recombine with one another, and when this happens between distant copies it can delete or duplicate large regions. Cells use careful repair and silencing to limit such events. Errors in these systems can cause rearrangements that are linked to disease, which shows why the control of repetitive DNA is important for the health of the organism as well as for the history of its genome.

Students who want a simple picture of repeats can imagine a notebook in which a single sentence has been copied many times, sometimes exactly and sometimes with small errors. Counting the copies and noting the errors tells us how old each copy is and how it spread. The genome records its own history in this way, and reading that record is part of the work of genome biology.

To summarize, repetitive DNA includes tandem repeats and interspersed repeats, and much of the interspersed material comes from transposons that move by cut-and-paste or copy-and-paste mechanisms. Transposons have shaped genome size and evolution, they can supply regulatory sequences and they pose a threat that cells control.
