Once the RNA polymerase has found a promoter, the work of starting transcription really begins. The DNA double helix is tightly paired, and the two strands must be separated before any nucleotide can be read. The enzyme must also place the first nucleotides of the RNA chain correctly, and then move away from the promoter without losing its grip on the template. This chapter describes these early steps, which are often called "initiation" to distinguish them from the longer process of elongation that follows.

The first stable contact between the enzyme and the promoter is called the "closed complex". In this state, the enzyme sits on the DNA with the two strands still paired. It has recognized the promoter sequence, but it has not yet opened the helix. The closed complex is a little like a key placed into a lock without yet turning it. The next step, which turns the key, is the opening of the DNA.

When the enzyme opens the DNA, the two strands are pulled apart over a short stretch of about a dozen base pairs. This opened region is called the "transcription bubble". It forms around the -10 element of the promoter, which is rich in A and T bases. These base pairs are held together more weakly than G and C pairs, so they are easier to separate. This is one reason why the A and T rich -10 element is useful: it helps the enzyme open the helix at the right place. The resulting structure, with the DNA already opened, is called the "open complex". Browning and Busby (2004) note that the open complex is an important point at which many bacterial genes are regulated.

After the DNA is open, the enzyme must start the RNA chain. The first nucleotide is usually a purine, either adenine or guanine, and it is placed on the template at the transcription start site. The enzyme then links the second nucleotide to the first, and the third to the second. Each addition requires the correct base to be paired with the template. Early in this process, the enzyme is not yet stable on the DNA, and it often makes short RNA pieces of two to about ten nucleotides before losing them and starting again. This repeated, unsuccessful start is called "abortive initiation". It may seem wasteful, but it is a normal part of the process, and it gives the enzyme a chance to test whether the start is correct.

The enzyme continues to make short RNAs until it reaches a point where it can leave the promoter behind. This step is called "promoter escape". To escape, the enzyme must break its contacts with the promoter, especially the contacts made by the sigma factor, and move forward onto the rest of the gene. In bacteria, the sigma factor is usually released at about this stage. Once the sigma factor is gone, the core enzyme is able to keep copying the gene for a long distance, without the help of the signals that were needed to find the start.

Eukaryotic initiation is more complicated, and it follows a different sequence of events. The eukaryotic RNA polymerase II cannot recognize the core promoter on its own. Instead, a set of helper proteins known as the "general transcription factors" must assemble on the promoter first. Roeder (1996) describes how these factors work together. The first to bind is often TFIID, which contains the TATA-binding protein. This protein recognizes the TATA box and bends the DNA. Other factors then join, including TFIIB, TFIIF, TFIIE and TFIIH, and finally the polymerase itself. The completed assembly is called the "preinitiation complex". Each factor has a specific job, from positioning the enzyme to helping open the DNA.

The eukaryotic system is therefore built step by step, with each factor waiting for the one before it. Smale and Kadonaga (2003) explain that different promoters need different combinations of these factors, depending on which core elements they contain. A promoter with a TATA box will recruit the factors in one way, while a promoter without one may recruit them in another. This variety is one reason why eukaryotic transcription is so flexible, and why a single set of factors can serve thousands of genes.

One important feature of eukaryotic initiation is that the polymerase is released from the promoter after a short time, leaving some of the factors behind. The leftover factors can then help the next round of transcription. This is one way the cell reuses its machinery. It is also a reason why the process of starting transcription can be repeated many times per minute on a busy gene.

Another point to note is that the enzyme does not usually start at a single fixed nucleotide. Real promoters allow some variation in the exact start position, so the transcription start site can be spread over a short region. The DNA sequence near the start, especially the initiator element, helps to set the exact point. This variation is part of why careful mapping of start sites, which you may meet in the research literature, requires precise methods.

It helps to summarize the steps in order. The enzyme first binds the promoter in the closed complex. It then opens the DNA to form the open complex. It makes the first nucleotides and often fails and tries again, which is abortive initiation. Finally it escapes the promoter, releasing the sigma factor in bacteria or leaving some general factors behind in eukaryotes. Each of these steps can be a point of control, and the cell can influence the rate of transcription at each one.

These early steps are sometimes described as the hardest part of transcription, because the enzyme has to do several things at once: recognize the promoter, open the helix, pick the right first nucleotides and then leave. Once it has escaped, the enzyme settles into a more stable pattern of copying. The current chapter has set out the steps in their order, so the later discussion can focus on the parts that differ between species and between genes.
