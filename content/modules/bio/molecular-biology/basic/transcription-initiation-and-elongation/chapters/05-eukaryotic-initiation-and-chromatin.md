Eukaryotic cells are more complicated than bacteria, and their transcription has more layers. The RNA polymerase II that makes messenger RNA cannot simply find a promoter and begin. It must be connected to the signals that switch a gene on, and it must work with a DNA that is packed around proteins. This chapter describes two of the most important extra layers: the Mediator complex, which links the switch to the enzyme, and chromatin, which controls how easily the enzyme can reach the DNA in the first place.

The RNA polymerase II enzyme is built from many subunits. Hahn (2004) describes its structure and the way it works with the general transcription factors. The enzyme has a core that is related in its general design to the bacterial enzyme, but it has many more subunits, and it has a long tail at one end called the "C-terminal domain", often shortened to CTD. The CTD is a chain of repeated amino acid sequences that can be chemically modified, and it acts as a platform for other proteins. Later chapters describe how the CTD is changed as transcription proceeds, but for now it is enough to know that it is a central hub of control.

Genes in eukaryotes are switched on by "activators". These are proteins that bind to specific sequences, often far from the promoter, and then send a signal to the machinery at the promoter. An activator cannot touch the polymerase directly in most cases. There is a gap between the two, and something must bridge it. That bridge is the "Mediator complex", a large assembly of around two dozen subunits that sits between the activator and the polymerase. Allen and Taatjes (2015) describe Mediator as a central integrator, because it receives signals from many activators and passes them to the enzyme in a coordinated way.

A helpful analogy is a relay. The activator is the person who gives the signal, and Mediator is the messenger who carries it to the team on the field, which is the polymerase and its general factors. Mediator does more than carry the message, though. It can help the polymerase assemble at the promoter, and it can influence how the enzyme moves away from the promoter. In this sense, Mediator is one of the places where the cell decides how strongly a gene is transcribed.

The eukaryotic genome is not naked DNA. It is wrapped around protein cores to form structures called "nucleosomes". Each nucleosome contains about 147 base pairs of DNA wrapped around a core of histone proteins, and the nucleosomes are spaced along the chromosome like beads on a string. Kornberg and Lorch (1999) reviewed how the nucleosome was discovered and why it matters. The nucleosome is a basic unit of packaging, and its existence means that the DNA at any gene is usually covered by protein.

This packaging creates a problem for transcription. A promoter buried inside a nucleosome is hard for the general factors and the polymerase to reach. The DNA is held tightly, and the proteins that want to bind the promoter may be blocked. For this reason, many active genes have promoters that are kept open, with the nucleosomes moved aside or replaced. The opening of chromatin is a key step in turning a gene on, and it is often the step that activators work on first.

Cells use several strategies to open chromatin. Some proteins called "chromatin remodeling complexes" use the energy of ATP to slide or eject nucleosomes from the DNA. Other enzymes add chemical marks to the histone tails, changing how tightly the DNA is held. Acetylation of certain histone residues is commonly linked with open, active chromatin, while other marks are linked with closed chromatin. These modifications are sometimes called the "histone code", although researchers now know that the relationship between a mark and the state of a gene is more complicated than a simple code.

It is worth noting that these chromatin changes are not always permanent. A gene can be opened at one stage of development and closed at another. The same gene may be open in one cell type and closed in another, because each cell uses a different combination of activators and chromatin proteins. This flexibility allows a single set of genes to produce many different cell types, and it is one of the main reasons that eukaryotic transcription is studied so widely.

The interaction between Mediator, activators and chromatin shows that transcription is a team effort. No single protein makes a gene active. Instead, a set of proteins work together, each adding part of the signal. A helpful way to picture this is a stage with many lights. Each light is a different activator, chromatin change or general factor, and the gene is bright only when enough lights are on at once.

Chromatin also has a memory. Activators recruit enzymes that add chemical marks to histone tails near the promoter, and proteins that read those marks can recruit more of the same enzymes. This creates a feedback loop. A gene that is active tends to stay active, and a gene that is silent tends to stay silent. Such loops help a cell keep its identity stable through many rounds of cell division, which is one reason a liver cell remains a liver cell over a long life.

There is an important lesson for beginners here. In bacteria, a sigma factor and the core enzyme are often enough to start transcription, once the promoter is accessible. In eukaryotes, the same basic steps are present, but they are surrounded by many more layers of control. The promoter alone does not determine whether a gene is active. Mediator, activators and chromatin all matter, and each adds a point at which the cell can change its behavior.

The chapter has covered the connections between the polymerase and its regulators, and the way packaging shapes access. The next chapter returns to the enzyme itself and follows transcription once it has left the promoter and begun to move along the gene. That stage, called elongation, is the longest part of transcription, and it contains its own set of controls, including pauses, that have an important role in determining how much RNA a cell makes.
