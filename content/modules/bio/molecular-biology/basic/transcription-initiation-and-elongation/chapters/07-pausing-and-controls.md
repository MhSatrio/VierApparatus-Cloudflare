In the last chapter, elongation was described as a steady process, with the enzyme adding one nucleotide after another. That description is true in general, but it hides an important detail. RNA polymerase does not move at a constant speed. It sometimes stops, sometimes for a short time and sometimes for much longer. These stops are called "pauses", and they are not simply accidents. Pausing is one of the most important places where a cell can control how much RNA a gene produces.

A pause is a period when the enzyme remains on the template without adding nucleotides, or adds them very slowly. Some pauses are caused by the DNA sequence. Certain sequences make the enzyme more likely to stop, for example when the RNA being made can form a hairpin, a small folded structure in which part of the RNA pairs with another part of itself. The hairpin can hold the enzyme in place. Landick (2006) explains that pausing is often a multistep process, in which the enzyme first enters a paused state, and then either escapes or backtracks, depending on the surrounding signals.

A pause is not always the same as a failure. In many cases the paused enzyme is waiting for a signal, and the cell can use this waiting period to decide whether to continue. Think of a runner at a starting line who has been told to wait for the signal before going on. The runner is ready but not yet moving, and the signal decides when the race begins. Paused polymerases can be thought of in the same way, as enzymes ready to continue once the right message arrives.

The best known form of regulated pausing in animal cells is called "promoter-proximal pausing". In this case, the RNA polymerase II enzyme initiates transcription and then pauses shortly after the start site, often within the first 50 or so nucleotides of the gene. The enzyme remains paused until a signal releases it into productive elongation. This pattern was once thought to be unusual, but it has turned out to be widespread.

Core et al. (2008) provided the key evidence for how common this is. They used a method that measured the newly made RNA at human promoters with high precision, and they found that many promoters carry a paused polymerase close to the start site. The pausing was not rare. It was a general feature of many genes, which suggested that the step after initiation is a major point of control. Their work also showed that transcription can begin in two directions at many promoters, which adds to the complexity of the start region.

Why would a cell pause the enzyme after it has already started? One reason is speed. A paused polymerase is ready to go, and it can be released very quickly when the cell needs the gene to be active. Another reason is coordination. The paused enzyme sits at a position where the RNA is being capped and prepared for processing, and waiting there may allow the cell to synchronize these steps. A third possibility is that pausing provides a checkpoint, so that the cell can decide whether a gene should be fully transcribed at all.

The release of paused polymerase requires specific factors. In animal cells, two proteins called NELF and DSIF help hold the enzyme in the paused state. A kinase called P-TEFb, which contains the CDK9 enzyme, adds phosphate groups to these factors and to the CTD of the polymerase. The modification changes the behavior of the enzyme, allowing it to leave the pause and enter productive elongation. This is a clear example of how chemical marks on proteins can switch a process on or off.

Hsin and Manley (2012) review the role of the CTD in this kind of control. The CTD is a repeated sequence that is modified at different points along the gene, and the pattern of modification changes as the enzyme moves. Early in the gene, the CTD carries marks that favor the recruitment of capping enzymes. Later, other marks favor the recruitment of splicing and processing factors. The enzyme therefore acts as a platform that changes its partners as it travels, and pausing is one of the times when these changes are made.

Pausing in bacteria works in a related but different way. The enzyme pauses at sites where the RNA can form a hairpin, and the pause may be followed by termination of transcription or by continued elongation. Some pauses are used by the cell to coordinate transcription with translation, because the ribosome can follow the RNA polymerase along the message. If the ribosome stalls, the polymerase may pause, and if the ribosome keeps up, the polymerase may continue more quickly. This is an example of two machines in the same cell coordinating their timing.

It is useful to see that pausing has both costs and benefits. A pause slows down the production of RNA, so it reduces the number of transcripts that a gene can make in a given time. This is a cost, but it can be a useful one, because the cell can use the slowdown to keep its response in check. The benefit is that the cell gains precise control, allowing it to decide quickly and reversibly how much of a gene product to make.

Pauses can also be used to protect against unwanted transcription. Some regions of the genome are transcribed weakly or not at all, and pausing can help block the continuation of transcription from places where it is not needed. In this sense, pausing acts as a gate. The enzyme can pass through the gate only when the right signal arrives, and the gate prevents the production of RNA from sequences that the cell does not want to express at that moment.

Researchers have learned much about pausing by studying the enzyme in controlled conditions. In the laboratory, the enzyme can be stopped at known positions, its structure can be examined, and the effects of different factors can be measured. This work has shown that pausing is not a simple on or off state, but a set of related states with different stabilities. Some states are easy to leave, others are much harder, and the balance between them depends on the sequence, the factors present and the modifications on the CTD.

The chapter has covered several ways in which the cell controls elongation. Pausing can be caused by the sequence, by hairpins in the RNA, or by the balance of factors. Promoter-proximal pausing is a widespread control point in animal cells, and its release depends on chemical marks.
