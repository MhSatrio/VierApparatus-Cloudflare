Once RNA polymerase has escaped the promoter, the transcription machinery settles into its longest and most regular phase. This phase is called "elongation". During elongation, the enzyme moves steadily along the DNA template, reads one base at a time, and adds a matching ribonucleotide to the growing RNA chain. A typical gene is many hundreds or thousands of nucleotides long, so elongation involves a very large number of identical steps. This chapter describes the steps and the mechanism that makes them reliable.

The basic unit of elongation is the "nucleotide addition cycle". At each cycle, the enzyme has a position on the DNA template and a growing RNA chain whose last nucleotide sits in the active site, which is the pocket where chemistry happens. The next template base is read, and the correct ribonucleotide, the one that pairs with it, is brought into the enzyme. The enzyme then forms a bond between that nucleotide and the end of the RNA chain, releasing a small molecule called pyrophosphate. The enzyme then shifts one position along the template, ready for the next cycle.

Each part of this cycle has a purpose. The correct nucleotide must pair with the template base, or the RNA will carry the wrong message. The enzyme therefore selects nucleotides with care, and most wrong choices are rejected before they can be added. The chemical bond is formed in the active site, which holds the nucleotides in position and helps the reaction proceed. The shift along the template makes sure the enzyme reads each base exactly once. Repeating these steps thousands of times produces a faithful copy of the gene.

A key structure during elongation is the "transcription bubble". This is the short region of DNA, about a dozen or so base pairs wide, that is held open by the enzyme. The bubble moves with the enzyme as it travels. Ahead of the bubble, the DNA is still a double helix. Behind it, the two strands rejoin, and the newly made RNA is released from the template and passes out of the enzyme. The bubble is therefore a moving window in which the RNA and the template are paired, and the window is where the copying happens.

As the enzyme moves, it has to deal with the twisting of the DNA. Each turn of the helix ahead of the enzyme becomes tighter, and each turn behind becomes looser. This twisting is relieved by enzymes called topoisomerases, which cut and rejoin the DNA to remove the strain. In a living cell, transcription is therefore never a single isolated event. It is linked to the activity of other enzymes that maintain the structure of the chromosome.

Hahn (2004) describes the eukaryotic elongation complex as a large assembly of the polymerase with several elongation factors. These factors help the enzyme move through chromatin, which is the packed form of DNA described in the previous chapter. The nucleosomes that lie along the gene must be passed by the enzyme, and elongation factors help the enzyme pass them without losing its place. This is one of the reasons that elongation is more complicated in eukaryotes than in bacteria.

Mistakes do happen during elongation. Occasionally the enzyme adds a nucleotide that does not match the template, or it becomes stuck. For these cases, the enzyme has a remarkable correction system. It can move backward along the RNA, a process called "backtracking". When the enzyme backtracks, the end of the RNA slides out of the active site and into a region where it can be removed. Special factors, such as TFIIS in eukaryotes and GreA and GreB in bacteria, help the enzyme cut off the wrong RNA ends and restart from the right position. This proofreading keeps the error rate of transcription very low.

Backtracking is a double-edged feature. It helps the enzyme correct mistakes, but it can also cause the enzyme to stop. A backtracked enzyme may stay in its new position for a while before it can resume copying. Landick (2006) explains how this kind of backward movement contributes to the pausing of the enzyme, and how pausing can become a point of regulation. The next chapter discusses pauses in more detail.

Speed is another feature of elongation. The enzyme does not move at a fixed rate. Its speed varies with the sequence of the template, with the presence of chromatin and with the presence of pauses. Bacterial polymerases typically add tens of nucleotides each second in good conditions, while the eukaryotic enzyme is slower on average, because of the chromatin and the many factors that surround it. The exact rate matters, because the time taken to transcribe a gene affects how much RNA the cell makes from it.

The C-terminal domain of RNA polymerase II, the CTD described in the previous chapter, has an important role during elongation. As the enzyme moves, the CTD is chemically modified in a pattern that changes along the gene. Hsin and Manley (2012) review how these changes help to coordinate transcription with the processing of the RNA, including the addition of a protective cap at the start of the RNA and the removal of non-coding pieces called introns. In this way, the enzyme is not just a copying machine. It is also a platform that organizes the steps that follow.

A useful way to picture elongation is a train on a track. The train moves steadily along the rails, stops at stations for short periods, and sometimes has to back up to fix a problem. The track is the DNA, the train is the polymerase, the stations are pauses, and the repair crew is the set of factors that help the enzyme back up and restart. The train carries a load, which is the growing RNA, and the load is delivered in pieces that match the order of the track.

It is worth summarizing what elongation achieves. The enzyme moves along the template, adds one nucleotide at a time, keeps the bubble open at the right position, corrects its own mistakes when needed and coordinates with the processing machinery. The result is a long, accurate RNA that matches the gene it came from. The chapter has described the normal flow of elongation, and the next chapter looks at the times when the enzyme slows down or stops, and at the controls that decide whether it continues.
