Every transcription event has a beginning and an end. The previous chapters followed RNA polymerase from the promoter through the first bonds, the steady copying of elongation and the controlled pauses that can slow it down. This final chapter describes how the enzyme stops, and then gathers the whole process into a single picture. The end of transcription is not simply a loss of power. It is a process with its own signals, and it is closely connected to what happens to the RNA afterward.

In bacteria, transcription can end in two main ways. The first is called "intrinsic termination", because the signal is contained within the RNA and the DNA itself. An intrinsic terminator has two parts. The first is a stretch of DNA that encodes an RNA able to form a hairpin, a short stem-loop structure in which part of the RNA pairs with another part of itself, and which is often rich in G and C bases. The second is a run of about six to eight U residues in the RNA, which are encoded by A residues in the DNA template. The hairpin causes the enzyme to pause, and the weak pairing of the U run lets the RNA slip off the template. The enzyme then falls away from the DNA, and transcription is over.

The second bacterial mechanism is called "Rho-dependent termination". This needs a protein called Rho, which is a ring-shaped motor that binds to the RNA as it emerges from the polymerase. Ciampi (2006) reviews how Rho recognizes the RNA, moves along it in an energy-using way, and eventually catches up with the enzyme when it pauses. When Rho reaches the polymerase, it pulls the RNA away from the template, and the enzyme is released. Rho-dependent terminators are common in bacteria, and they allow the cell to stop transcription at a point it chooses.

The two bacterial mechanisms show the same principle in two forms. Both rely on the enzyme pausing, and both use the RNA to tell the enzyme where to stop. In the intrinsic case, the RNA folds into a shape that causes the pause and then releases itself from the template. In the Rho-dependent case, a protein acts on the RNA to achieve the same result. The variety of mechanisms reflects the variety of signals that bacteria need to read.

In eukaryotes, the end of transcription is more tightly linked to the processing of the RNA. For messenger RNA produced by RNA polymerase II, the RNA is cut at a specific site, and a long chain of adenine nucleotides, called a poly(A) tail, is added to the new end. The cutting and the addition are carried out by a complex of enzymes that recognize a signal sequence in the RNA. After the cut, the polymerase continues to transcribe for a while before it is released, and the release is linked to the degradation of the RNA still attached to the enzyme. Hsin and Manley (2012) review how the CTD of the polymerase coordinates these events, so that termination is not a separate step but part of a larger process.

The idea of coordination is central to eukaryotic transcription. In the previous chapter, the CTD was described as a platform that changes its partners along the gene. At the end of the gene, the CTD carries marks that recruit the enzymes that cut the RNA and add the tail. These enzymes, in turn, help to trigger the release of the polymerase from the DNA. The end of transcription is therefore shaped by the same platform that organized the beginning and the middle, showing how the cell links the steps of gene expression.

Termination also has a practical consequence. A gene that is not terminated properly may produce RNA that runs into the next gene, creating messages that are too long or that contain the wrong sequences. Cells have developed careful signals to prevent this, and mistakes in termination can alter the levels of proteins in the cell. This is one reason why studies of the signals that end transcription are important for understanding disease, and it is one reason why the field keeps returning to these questions.

Kornberg (2007) summarizes how the eukaryotic transcription machinery is organized as a whole. The chapters of this module have described the parts in order, but the enzyme does not work in order in a simple way. Initiation, elongation and termination are connected. A signal at the promoter can change the behavior of the enzyme at the end of the gene, and a change in the chromatin can change the rate of elongation. The cell is a network of linked decisions, and transcription is one of its most important networks.

It is worth bringing the whole process together now. Transcription begins when RNA polymerase finds a promoter. In bacteria, a sigma factor helps the enzyme recognize the -10 and -35 elements. In eukaryotes, general transcription factors and the Mediator complex bring the enzyme to the start site, often through an activator and a chromatin that has been opened. The enzyme opens the DNA, makes its first nucleotides, and escapes the promoter. It then elongates along the gene, adding one nucleotide at a time and correcting its errors. It may pause, and the pause may be released by a signal. Finally, the enzyme reaches the end of the gene and terminates, and the RNA is released to be processed or to do its job.

Each step of this sequence is a possible point of regulation. The promoter sets the baseline, the factors set the efficiency of initiation, the pauses set the speed of elongation, and the terminators set where the copying ends. A cell that changes any of these steps changes the amount of RNA it makes, and therefore the amount of the protein it makes from that RNA. This is why transcription is often described as the first and most important point of gene control.

Several misunderstandings are common among beginners, and it is worth naming them here. One is that transcription is a simple copying process that always produces the same result. In fact, the result depends on many signals. Another is that the enzyme acts alone. In fact, it works with dozens of partners in eukaryotes and several helpers in bacteria. A third is that a gene is either on or off. In reality, transcription is a continuum, and the level of activity can vary across a wide range.

The module has followed one transcription event from its beginning to its end. You now have the vocabulary to read the research literature on the subject: the promoter, the transcription start site, the holoenzyme, the bubble, the pause and the terminator. You also have a sense of how these parts work together. The next step, in other modules, is to see how the same basic process is changed by disease, by development and by the modern tools that allow scientists to watch transcription happen in living cells.
