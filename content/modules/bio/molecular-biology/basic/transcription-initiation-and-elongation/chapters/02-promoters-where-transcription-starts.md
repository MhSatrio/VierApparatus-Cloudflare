Before RNA polymerase can copy a gene, it has to find the right place to begin. The DNA sequence that marks this place is called the "promoter". A promoter is a region of DNA, usually just upstream of a gene, which means on the side of the gene where transcription has not yet reached. It is the signal that says, in effect, "start reading here". Without a promoter, the enzyme has no reliable way to tell one stretch of DNA from another, and the cell would copy the wrong parts of its genome.

The exact point where the first RNA nucleotide is added is called the "transcription start site". By convention, the nucleotide of the DNA at this site is numbered +1, and the positions before it are numbered with negative numbers. Positions upstream of the start site therefore carry negative numbers, such as -10 or -35, and positions downstream carry positive numbers. These numbers are a useful shorthand for describing where each signal lies, and you will see them throughout the research literature.

It is important to separate two ideas. The promoter is the sequence of DNA that the enzyme recognizes. The transcription start site is the point where RNA synthesis actually begins. The two are related, because the promoter guides the enzyme to the start site, but they are not the same thing. A promoter can be a few dozen base pairs long and contain several short signals, while the start site is a single nucleotide.

In bacteria, the promoter is usually recognized by a complex of the RNA polymerase core and a sigma factor. Two short sequences inside the promoter matter most. One sits about 10 base pairs upstream of the start site, and the other about 35 base pairs upstream. These are called the "-10 element" and the "-35 element". The -10 element is often called the "Pribnow box", after the scientist who first described it, and its consensus sequence is roughly TATAAT. The -35 element has the consensus sequence TTGACA. The two elements are separated by a spacer of about 17 base pairs, and the closer the real sequence matches the consensus, the more strongly the promoter is used.

Browning and Busby (2004) describe how these small differences in sequence allow bacteria to tune many genes at once. The word "consensus" needs a short explanation. Real promoters are not identical. A consensus sequence is the most common base found at each position when many promoters are lined up side by side. A promoter that matches the consensus closely is usually used often, while one that differs at several positions is used less often. This is one of the main ways a bacterial cell controls how much of each gene it makes. The cell does not need a switch for every gene. It only needs promoters that are more or less similar to the consensus.

Eukaryotic promoters are more varied. The RNA polymerase II promoter has a "core promoter", which is the minimal region needed to start transcription at the right place. Smale and Kadonaga (2003) review the elements that make up this core. The best known is the "TATA box", a sequence rich in T and A bases that lies around 25 to 30 base pairs upstream of the start site. Other elements include the "initiator", which overlaps the start site itself, and the "downstream promoter element", which lies a little after it. Not every promoter contains every element. Many eukaryotic promoters have no TATA box at all, which surprised researchers when they first looked at large numbers of genes.

Eukaryotic promoters also have partners that work from a distance. Regions called "enhancers" can lie thousands of base pairs away from the gene they control, sometimes on the other side of the gene. They loop through the DNA so that their bound proteins can contact the promoter. Enhancers do not replace the promoter; they add to its activity. For a beginner, the simplest picture is that the core promoter is the start button, and enhancers are the volume controls that tell the machinery how loudly to play.

A striking finding in recent years is that transcription does not always start at a single point. Core et al. (2008) mapped the newly made RNA at human promoters with high precision. They found that RNA polymerase II often pauses just after the start site, after it has made a short RNA, before it continues. They also found that many promoters start transcription in both directions, producing a short RNA on each side. This pattern is sometimes called "divergent" initiation. The discovery showed that a promoter is better understood as a region that can start transcription in several ways, rather than as a single fixed point.

The idea of promoter-proximal pausing matters for later chapters. The enzyme may stop for a while just downstream of the start site, and whether it continues or leaves is an important point of control. Chapter 7 returns to this in detail. For now, the key lesson is that the start of transcription involves a region rather than a single letter of DNA, and that the cell can regulate many steps within that region.

Promoters also differ in the way they are used. Some are highly active in almost every cell, because the genes they control are needed all the time. These are called "housekeeping" promoters. Others are active only in particular cell types or at particular times, responding to hormones, stress or developmental signals. In many human genes that are used all the time, the promoter region contains a stretch of DNA with many CG pairs, called a "CpG island". This feature helps mark the region as one to be kept open for transcription, although the exact rules differ from gene to gene.

Reading a promoter is therefore a little like reading the first lines of a set of instructions. The sequence tells the enzyme where to start, how strongly to start, and sometimes what other signals to listen for. The next chapter describes how the bacterial enzyme recognizes these signals with the help of its sigma factor. Chapter 5 describes the more complicated eukaryotic system. Both start from the same idea introduced here: the promoter is the signal that tells RNA polymerase where to begin.
