Every living cell stores its genetic information as DNA, but DNA is rarely used directly. Most of the time, the cell first makes a copy of part of its DNA in the form of RNA. This copying step is called "transcription". The word describes rewriting a message from one form into another, much as a scribe copies a text from one book into another. In transcription, the "template" is one of the two strands of the DNA double helix. The "product" is a single-stranded RNA molecule whose sequence matches the template, with one important difference: RNA uses the base uracil (U) where DNA uses thymine (T).

The machine that performs transcription is an enzyme called "RNA polymerase". Its job is to read the DNA template and link together ribonucleotides, which are the building blocks of RNA, into a chain. The chain always grows in one direction, from the 5' end to the 3' end of the new molecule. Enzymes that copy DNA, called DNA polymerases, need a short starter piece of nucleic acid called a "primer" before they can begin. RNA polymerase does not. It can start a new chain without one, which is one reason transcription can begin at many different points along the genome.

Why does a cell go to this trouble? The answer lies in the flow of information from gene to protein. A gene is a stretch of DNA that carries the instructions for making a product, usually a protein. Proteins are made in a separate step called translation, which takes place on structures called ribosomes. Ribosomes cannot read DNA directly. They read messenger RNA, usually shortened to "mRNA", which is the copy made by transcription. The idea that information passes from DNA to RNA to protein is often called the "central dogma" of molecular biology. The name is a little misleading, because RNA also has many jobs of its own, but the basic flow remains a useful guide for beginners.

Transcription also protects the original DNA. In eukaryotic cells, which are cells with a nucleus, the DNA stays inside the nucleus while the mRNA copy travels out to the ribosomes in the cytoplasm. This separation keeps the master copy of the genetic information safe from damage during the many steps of protein production. In bacteria, which have no nucleus, the DNA sits in a region of the cell called the nucleoid, but transcription still produces a separate working copy of each gene.

Transcription also gives the cell control. A cell that needs a large amount of one protein can make many copies of its mRNA from that gene. A cell that needs a protein only at certain times can switch the transcription of its gene on and off. Because this decision is made at the level of RNA, before any protein is built, it is often the first and most economical point at which a cell adjusts its activity. Think of it as deciding how many copies of a recipe to print before anyone starts cooking.

Cells make more kinds of RNA than mRNA alone. Transfer RNA, or "tRNA", is a small molecule that carries an amino acid to the ribosome during translation. Ribosomal RNA, or "rRNA", forms the structural and catalytic core of the ribosome itself. In eukaryotes these classes are made by different RNA polymerases. RNA polymerase I makes most of the rRNA. RNA polymerase II makes mRNA and many small regulatory RNAs. RNA polymerase III makes tRNA and some other small RNAs. Each enzyme responds to its own set of signals and is built from many subunits. Hahn (2004) describes the RNA polymerase II machinery in detail, and it is the best studied of the three.

Bacteria have a simpler arrangement. A single RNA polymerase makes all the RNA of the cell. This enzyme is built from a "core" of several subunits, together with a separate protein called a "sigma factor" that helps the core find the right starting points on the DNA. The core and the sigma factor together form the "holoenzyme", which is the active form of the enzyme. Browning and Busby (2004) review how bacteria use different sigma factors to switch whole groups of genes on or off, for example during heat stress or when food is scarce.

Eukaryotic cells use a more elaborate system. The enzyme cannot start alone on most genes, and it needs a set of helper proteins called "general transcription factors" to recognize the start of a gene and place the enzyme in the right position. Kornberg (2007) summarizes how this system works and why it matters for development and disease. The helpers and the enzyme together are often called the "transcription machinery", and much of the rest of this module describes how that machinery functions in practice.

It also helps to compare transcription with translation, because the two are easy to confuse. Transcription keeps the same kind of alphabet: the four bases of DNA are copied into the four bases of RNA, with uracil standing in for thymine. Translation changes the alphabet entirely. The ribosome reads the RNA three bases at a time, and each group of three, called a codon, stands for one amino acid or for a signal to stop. Transcription is therefore a copy from one nucleotide text to another, while translation is a conversion from nucleotides into the amino acid chain of a protein.

Two common misunderstandings are worth clearing up early. The first is that transcription makes a copy of the whole DNA. It does not. Only a small region, the gene or part of it, is copied at any one time. The second is that a gene is always transcribed. In fact, most genes in any given cell are silent, and transcription is switched on only for the genes that cell needs at that moment. The cell's identity, whether it is a nerve cell, a liver cell or a bacterium growing in a dish, is largely a matter of which genes are being transcribed.

The rest of this module follows transcription in order. The next chapter explains the promoter, the DNA signal where transcription starts. The later chapters describe how RNA polymerase opens the DNA, makes its first bonds, moves along the gene, pauses, and finally stops. Keep three ideas from this chapter in mind: transcription copies DNA into RNA, RNA polymerase is the enzyme that does the copying, and the RNA it produces can be a messenger, a transfer molecule or a structural molecule, depending on the gene.
