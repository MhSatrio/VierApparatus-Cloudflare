In the last chapter, the promoter was described as a signal that tells RNA polymerase where to begin. This chapter looks at the enzyme that reads the signal. In bacteria, one RNA polymerase carries out all of the transcription in the cell. It is a remarkable molecule, and understanding its parts makes the rest of transcription much easier to follow.

The core of the bacterial enzyme is built from several protein subunits. Biochemists often write its composition as alpha, alpha, beta, beta-prime and omega, which is a short way of listing the subunits and how many copies of each are present. Each subunit has a job. Some form a scaffold that holds the parts together. Others form a channel for the DNA, a pocket for the growing RNA chain, and a region that grips the DNA as it is copied. The core is able to make RNA on its own, but it cannot start in the right place. It binds DNA in a general way, and on its own it would begin transcription at many random points.

The missing piece is the "sigma factor". Sigma is a separate protein that binds to the core and changes its behaviour. The sigma factor carries a set of regions that read the sequence of the promoter. When sigma is attached, the enzyme is called the "holoenzyme", which means the complete, active form. Murakami et al. (2002) described the structure of the holoenzyme at 4 Å resolution, which is detailed enough to show how the sigma factor sits against the core and how the DNA is positioned in the enzyme's groove. Their structure gave researchers a physical picture of what had previously been known mainly from genetics and biochemistry.

The sigma factor recognizes the -10 and -35 elements of the promoter that were described in the previous chapter. One region of sigma reads the -35 element, and another reads the -10 element. Because the sigma factor touches DNA in two places, it can tell a promoter apart from the much larger amount of DNA that is not a promoter. The reading is not perfect, and it is affected by the exact sequence, the spacing and the surrounding bases. This imperfect reading is part of the reason that promoters differ in strength.

Most bacterial genomes contain several sigma factors, not just one. The main one, usually called sigma-70 in Escherichia coli, is responsible for most of the transcription during normal growth. It is often called the "housekeeping" sigma factor, because it drives the genes that every cell needs all the time. Other sigma factors are used under special conditions. Browning and Busby (2004) review how these alternative sigma factors allow bacteria to respond to heat, to starvation, to the presence of certain nutrients and to other stresses. When a stress arrives, the cell may produce a different sigma factor, which then directs the polymerase to a new set of promoters, and the cell switches its pattern of gene use very quickly.

This arrangement has an elegant logic. A bacterium does not need a separate polymerase for every kind of job. It needs one polymerase whose target can be changed by swapping its sigma factor. Each sigma factor carries its own recognition rules, so each one selects a different group of promoters. The result is a flexible system that can reprogram the whole transcription pattern of the cell in a short time, using one enzyme and a small family of helper proteins.

Several terms are useful to learn at this point. The "promoter specificity" of a sigma factor is the set of promoter sequences it prefers. A "regulon" is a group of genes that share a common control by the same sigma factor or regulatory protein. A "sigma cycle" refers to the process by which the sigma factor is released from the polymerase after the first steps of transcription and can be reused. Each term is a small piece of the larger picture, and each will return in later chapters.

It is worth noting that the bacterial system is simpler than the eukaryotic one. Kornberg (2007) explains that eukaryotic cells need many more helper proteins before the polymerase can start on a typical gene. In bacteria, the holoenzyme can recognize a promoter and open the DNA with the help of the sigma factor alone. This simplicity makes bacteria a useful model for studying the basic mechanism, and much of what we know about how the enzyme works first came from studies of bacterial systems.

The sigma factor does not stay attached forever. After the enzyme has made the first few bonds, sigma is usually released, and the core continues alone along the gene. This release is important. It means the same sigma factor can be used again on a different promoter, and it means the enzyme that is copying the gene is not the same enzyme that found the promoter. Chapter 4 describes what happens during these first steps, including the moment when sigma leaves.

A useful way to think about the bacterial polymerase is as a machine with a removable steering wheel. The core is the engine. It is powerful, but it has no sense of direction. The sigma factor is the steering wheel, which decides where the engine goes. Changing the steering wheel changes where the engine starts, and therefore which genes are copied. Bacteria exploit this design every day, and it is one of the main reasons that a single enzyme can serve the needs of a whole cell.

Finally, remember that the holoenzyme is a dynamic structure. Its parts move, and the DNA it holds is bent and opened. Structures such as the one described by Murakami and colleagues are snapshots of particular states, and they help explain function. But the enzyme in a living cell is constantly changing shape as it searches, binds, opens and releases DNA. The next chapter follows the enzyme through the start of transcription, when the DNA is opened and the first bonds of the RNA chain are formed.
