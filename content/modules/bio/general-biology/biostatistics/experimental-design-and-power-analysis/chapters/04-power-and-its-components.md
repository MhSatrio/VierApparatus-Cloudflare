A power calculation links four quantities, and fixing any three determines the fourth. These are the effect size, the variance of the response, the significance level and the sample size, and the power is the probability of obtaining a significant result when the effect of the stated size is real. Power is therefore a property of the design and of the effect it is planned to detect. It is not a property of a finished result, and a study does not have a power once its data are known.

The significance level is the probability of a type I error, which is a false positive, and the power is one minus the probability of a type II error, which is a false negative. Lowering the significance level to reduce false positives raises the sample size needed for a given power. The two errors trade against each other at a fixed sample size, and the choice between them is a judgement about the costs of each kind of error in the particular biology.

A non-significant result from an underpowered study is not evidence that the effect is absent. Button et al. (2013) argued that low power reduces the probability of detecting a true effect and also lowers the probability that a significant result reflects a real effect, which is why the findings of small studies are unreliable. An absence of evidence from a study with little power is weak evidence of absence, and a report must state the power so that the reader can make this distinction.

Cumming (2014) argues that the interval of an estimate conveys more than a p-value, because it shows the range of effects compatible with the data. Ioannidis (2005) noted that the pressure to find significant results encourages the selection of underpowered designs, and the four components should be reported together so that the study can be judged on the scale of its design rather than on a single significant or non-significant answer.
