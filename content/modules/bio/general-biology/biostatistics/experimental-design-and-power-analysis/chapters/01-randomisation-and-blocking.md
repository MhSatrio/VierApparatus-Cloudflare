Randomisation allocates units to treatments by a procedure that does not depend on their characteristics, so that the groups differ only by chance in anything the experimenter did not measure. Without it, the comparison may reflect which animals were chosen first, which cages stood nearest the door or which plates were read in the morning, and no analysis of the data can separate these from the treatment. A random sequence generated by a stated procedure, with its seed recorded, is the simplest guarantee that the allocation was not chosen by the experimenter.

Blocking groups units that are alike in a known source of variation, such as the day of the experiment, the litter of an animal or the batch of a reagent. Treatments are then allocated at random within each block, and the comparison is made within blocks. The variation between blocks is removed from the error term, so the comparison becomes more precise. A block that is ignored in the analysis leaves its variation in the error, while a block that is included reduces it, and the decision to block should be made before the data are collected.

The unit of randomisation must match the unit of inference. If a treatment is applied to a cage but the outcome is measured in each animal, the animals in one cage are not independent observations, and the effective sample size is the number of cages. Cumming (2014) argues that the precision of an estimate depends on the number of independent units, and an analysis that counts the animals as if they were independent overstates that precision.

The randomisation and blocking are choices that a flexible analysis can undo. Ioannidis (2005) described how a free choice of units and outcomes can produce a significant result from a design that does not support it. The procedure, the blocks and the unit of allocation should be stated in the protocol and deposited with the data, as Sandve et al. (2013) recommended, so that a reader can judge whether the comparison was made in the way that it claims.
