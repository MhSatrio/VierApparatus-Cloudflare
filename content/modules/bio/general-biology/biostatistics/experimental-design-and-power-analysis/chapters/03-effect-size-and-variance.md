A power calculation requires an effect size, which is the difference between the treatments that the study should be able to detect. The effect size is a choice, not a fact, and it should be set by the biology. A reduction of tumour volume of ten per cent may matter to a clinician and be trivial to a physiologist, and the calculation is meaningless until the smallest difference of interest has been stated in the units of the measurement.

The variance of the response is the second input. It is the spread of the measurement within a group, and it is usually taken from pilot data, from a previous study with the same assay or from the literature. The standardised effect size divides the difference by the standard deviation, which makes effects on different scales comparable. Cumming (2014) argues that the effect should be stated with its unit and its interval, so that the reader can judge its size without the calculation.

A published effect size is usually an overestimate of the true one. Studies that report large effects are more likely to reach significance, and a small study that happens to find a large effect is more likely to be published. Ioannidis (2005) described how selection of this kind inflates findings, and a power calculation based on the published effect will plan a study too small for the true effect. Button et al. (2013) found that the power of many studies was low for the effects they reported, and this pattern follows from the same selection.

A single value is an unreliable basis for a plan, so the calculation should be repeated across a range of plausible effects and variances. A sample size that gives adequate power only for the most optimistic effect is a poor plan, and one that remains adequate across the range is a sound plan. The chosen range and its justification should be written down before the data are collected, since a range chosen after the results are known protects nothing.
