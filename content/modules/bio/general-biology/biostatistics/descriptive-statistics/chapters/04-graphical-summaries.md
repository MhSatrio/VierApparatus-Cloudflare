A graph of a biological variable should show its distribution, and the choice of graph determines how much of the distribution a reader can see. A histogram shows the shape by counting values in bins, and its appearance depends on the bin width, which must be chosen with care. A box plot shows the median, the quartiles and the extreme values, and it summarises the spread compactly. A dot plot or strip chart shows every value, which is the most complete form and the one that reveals small samples and gaps in the data.

The most common graph in biological papers is the bar of means with error bars. It shows one number per group and an uncertainty, and it hides the shape of the data. Two groups with the same mean and the same spread may have very different distributions, one symmetric and one bimodal, and the bar graph gives the reader no way to see the difference. Cumming (2014) argues that the individual values should be shown, since the figure should let the reader see the data that the statistics summarise.

Error bars must also be interpreted correctly. A bar of standard deviation describes the spread of the values, a bar of standard error describes the uncertainty of the mean, and a confidence interval describes the range of plausible means. A figure that does not say which bar it shows invites a misreading, and a reader may take a standard error for the spread of the data, which makes it look far more precise than it is.

Bland and Altman (1986) used a plot of differences against averages to show the agreement between two methods of measurement, and that plot shows a relationship that a scatter of one method against the other would hide. The choice of plot should follow the question the figure answers.

Sandve et al. (2013) recommended that the figures of an analysis be generated by the code that produced them, so that they can be regenerated from the data.
