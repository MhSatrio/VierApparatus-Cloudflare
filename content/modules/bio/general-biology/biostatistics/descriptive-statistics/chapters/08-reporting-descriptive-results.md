Descriptive results are the first part of a statistical report, and they are often the part that a reader uses most. A reader needs to know the size of each group, the centre and spread of each variable with the measures named, and the handling of missing values and outliers, since these change the summary. Cumming (2014) argues that the report should give enough of the data for the reader to see the distributions and to judge the conclusions drawn from them.

The sample size of each group is the first item. The number of observations in each group, and the number lost to missing values or exclusion, should be given alongside the summary. A mean of ten observations and a mean of a thousand are not equally informative, and the reader must see the difference.

The measures are the second item. The centre and the spread should be given as a pair, named, with the reason for the choice of mean or median and of standard deviation or interquartile range. The transformations applied and the scale of the reported values should be stated. Wasserstein and Lazar (2016) warn that a statistic is not the phenomenon it describes, and the measures should be chosen with the phenomenon in view.

The figures are the third item. A figure should show the distribution of each variable, as the earlier chapter on graphical summaries describes, and it should be generated from the data by code. Peng (2011) argued that the figures of a computational study should be reproducible, and a figure generated by a script meets that standard.

The data are the fourth item. The data behind the descriptive results should be deposited with an identifier, and the code that produced the summaries should accompany them. Wilkinson et al. (2016) set out the principles under which such data should be findable and reusable, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended keeping the outputs of an analysis with its inputs and steps.
