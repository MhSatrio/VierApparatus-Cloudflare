Descriptive statistics summarise a dataset before any conclusion is drawn from it. They answer the questions a reader asks first: where are the values, how widely do they spread, what shape do they take, and are there values that do not fit. Cumming (2014) argues that a summary should display the data, and that a single number such as a mean can conceal the distribution behind it. The topic covers the measures and the graphs that make a dataset understandable before it is tested.

The chapters begin with the centre and spread, then the shape of distributions and the transformations that simplify skewed data. Graphical summaries, correlation between variables, outliers and the reporting of descriptive results follow. Each choice of summary is an argument about the data: a mean is appropriate for a symmetric variable and misleading for a strongly skewed one, and a graph that shows only the mean hides the spread that a reader needs.

Descriptive statistics also carry risks. A summary chosen after looking at the data, or one that omits the values that do not fit, can give a false picture. Bland and Altman (1986) showed that agreement between two measurements must be judged from their differences, not from their correlation alone, and the summary must be chosen with its purpose in mind. Wasserstein and Lazar (2016) warn that a statistic is not the same as the phenomenon it describes, and the descriptive results should be read with that in mind.
