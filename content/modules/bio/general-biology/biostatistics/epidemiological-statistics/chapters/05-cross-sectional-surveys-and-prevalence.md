A cross-sectional survey measures exposure and disease in a population at one time. Each individual is classified by the exposure and by the presence of the condition at the moment of the survey, and the result describes the population as it is rather than as it changes. The design is quick and inexpensive, and it is the standard way to estimate the prevalence of a condition, the distribution of a risk factor or the burden of disease across a region.

The design estimates prevalence in each exposure group, and the ratio of the two prevalences is the prevalence ratio. This ratio is a measure of association, but it is a comparison of existing conditions rather than of new ones, since it combines the risk of falling ill with the duration of the illness. Cumming (2014) argues that such estimates should be reported with their intervals, since a survey with a modest sample can show prevalences that differ by chance alone.

The main limitation of the design is that it cannot establish the order of events. If an exposure and a condition are measured together, the survey cannot show whether the exposure came first, and a condition may even have changed the exposure. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording what each measurement represents, so that a reader does not mistake a cross-sectional association for a causal one or a temporal sequence.

Representativeness depends on the sampling frame and the response rate. Samples drawn with unequal probabilities must be weighted to give valid population estimates, and the weights should be reported. Wilkinson et al. (2016) set out principles under which the data behind such a survey should be findable and reusable, and depositing the survey data with its weights and frame allows the estimates to be reproduced and compared with later surveys.
