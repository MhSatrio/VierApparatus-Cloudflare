A figure is an argument about data, and its design decides whether a reader can check that argument. Biological datasets are often complex, with several groups, repeated measurements and wide ranges, and a poor figure can hide the very pattern it is meant to show. The discipline of visualisation is the set of choices that connect the structure of the data to the visual channels that a reader decodes most accurately, and it is part of statistical practice rather than decoration.

The topic begins with perception. Cleveland and McGill (1984) ranked the visual encodings by how accurately people judge them, and position along a common scale is judged more accurately than area or colour saturation. Rougier et al. (2014) turned such findings into practical rules for figures, and the chapters that follow apply them to the choice of chart, the display of uncertainty and the avoidance of distortion.

The central claim of the topic is that summaries hide information. Weissgerber et al. (2015) showed that a bar chart of means conceals the distribution of the observations behind them, and the same mean can arise from very different data. Cumming (2014) argues that estimates should be shown with their intervals, and the raw observations should be displayed wherever the sample is small enough to allow it.

The later chapters address colour, multivariate displays and the production of figures. A figure must be accessible to readers with colour deficiency and must reproduce in greyscale. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended that every figure be generated by code from the data, so that a figure can be regenerated, corrected and checked, and the figure's source should be deposited with the results it displays.
