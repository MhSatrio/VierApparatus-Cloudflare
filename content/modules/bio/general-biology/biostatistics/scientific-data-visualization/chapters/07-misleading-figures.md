A figure can mislead without any intention to deceive, and the commonest distortions are the result of default settings or of a wish to make a result look clearer. The aim of the topic is to recognise these distortions, so that the author can avoid them and the reader can detect them. Cleveland and McGill (1984) showed that visual judgement is accurate for some encodings and inaccurate for others, and most distortions exploit the inaccurate ones.

A bar chart whose baseline does not start at zero exaggerates the ratio between two bars, since the reader judges their lengths from the baseline. For a difference, a truncated axis is less of a problem, but it still exaggerates the visual size of the difference relative to the values. A truncated axis may be acceptable for points, whose comparison is one of position, and it should be stated whenever it is used. Weissgerber et al. (2015) showed how such choices hide the distributions behind bars.

Dual axes allow two series with different units to be plotted together, and their apparent relation depends on the scales chosen for each axis. By changing the scales, almost any two series can be made to appear to rise and fall together, so a dual-axis plot can show a relation that exists only in the choice of scale. Three-dimensional rendering distorts areas and angles, which Cleveland and McGill (1984) showed are judged inaccurately, and the perspective can make the larger bar appear smaller.

Selection of the time window or the threshold can also mislead. A pattern that appears only within a chosen period, or only above a chosen cut-off, may be the product of the selection rather than of the biology. Ioannidis (2005) described how such flexibility produces findings that do not hold, and Cumming (2014) argues that an effect should be shown across the full range of the data with its interval, so that the choice of window is visible.
