Inferential statistics uses a sample to say something about the population from which it was drawn. A laboratory cannot measure every cell in a tissue or every individual in a species, and it measures a subset and infers the properties of the whole. The inference is valid only if the sample was drawn in a way that represents the population, and the methods of this topic assume that the sampling design has been respected. A sample chosen for convenience or for the result it gives cannot support the inference that the statistics claim.

The topic covers the logic of estimation and its uncertainty. Estimators are chosen for their properties, such as being unbiased and having small variance, and the confidence interval expresses the uncertainty of the estimate as a range of population values consistent with the data. Likelihood and maximum likelihood estimation provide the basis of many biological models, and the Bayesian approach offers an alternative in which the parameters themselves carry a probability distribution.

Every inferential method rests on assumptions, and the topic ends with the checks that test them. Wasserstein and Lazar (2016) remind readers that a p-value and an interval are conditional on the model, and Ioannidis (2005) describes how the choice of analysis can produce findings that are less reliable than they appear. The inference is only as sound as the design and the assumptions that support it.
