All the cells of the blood arise from a small population of haematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow. These cells can renew themselves, and they can give rise to the progenitors of each lineage. The progenitors divide and mature into red cells, white cells and platelets, and each step narrows the potential of the cell. Alberts et al. (2022) describe this hierarchy as a set of decisions in which each cell commits to a lineage, and the signals it receives determine which branch it follows.

Till and McCulloch (1961) measured the radiation sensitivity of marrow cells by transplanting them into irradiated mice and counting the colonies that formed on the spleen. Each colony arose from a single cell that had proliferated and differentiated, and the number of colonies reflected the number of cells that could repopulate the marrow. The method revealed a population of cells with the capacity to regenerate the blood, and it established the principle that a single cell can give rise to several lineages.

The lineages are regulated by growth factors. Erythropoietin drives the production of red cells, and colony-stimulating factors drive the white cell lineages. The receptors for these factors are present on the progenitors, and the response depends on the concentration and the duration of exposure. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result obtained with a single culture condition may not reflect the behaviour of cells in the body, and the conditions of the colony assay must be stated.

The balance between renewal and differentiation is maintained in the marrow, and its disturbance produces the disorders of the blood. A stem cell that expands without maturing can give rise to a malignant clone, and a failure of production leaves the blood deficient in one or more lineages.

The measures of blood cells in circulation therefore reflect a flow of production from the marrow, and a change in one lineage may reflect a change in its progenitors.
