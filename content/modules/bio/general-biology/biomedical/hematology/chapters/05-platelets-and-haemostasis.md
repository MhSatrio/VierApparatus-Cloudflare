Platelets are small fragments of cells released from megakaryocytes in the marrow. They circulate without a nucleus and are ready to act when a vessel is injured. The endothelium normally prevents them from adhering, but when it is damaged, the underlying collagen is exposed, and platelets bind to it through receptors that recognise the matrix. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the adhesion receptors and the matrix proteins they bind, and this first contact is the start of haemostasis.

Once bound, platelets are activated. They change shape, release the contents of their granules and expose receptors that bind fibrinogen, which allows them to link together into an aggregate. The granules carry signals that recruit further platelets and that promote the vessel's own response. The plug that forms is temporary, and it is reinforced by the coagulation cascade described in the next chapter. Ross (1999) described how the injury to the vessel wall, and the platelet response to it, are central to the early stages of atherosclerosis as well as to haemostasis.

Bleeding can arise from a low platelet count or from a defect in platelet function. A low count, below about one hundred thousand per microlitre, is usually associated with a risk of spontaneous bleeding, and the risk rises as the count falls. A defect in function may leave the count normal while the platelets fail to aggregate, and the bleeding can be severe. The two conditions are distinguished by testing the function of the platelets, not by counting them.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a measure that is normal does not exclude a problem with the function it is supposed to reflect, and the platelet count alone is not a test of haemostasis. Platt (1964) recommended comparing alternatives, and a bleeding history is most informative when the count and the function are measured together.

The balance of haemostasis is a response that must stop bleeding without causing clots where they are not needed, and both failure and excess are clinically important.
