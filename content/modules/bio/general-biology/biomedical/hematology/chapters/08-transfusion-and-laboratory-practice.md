Blood groups are defined by molecules on the surface of the red cell. The ABO groups are carbohydrate structures added to a membrane protein and lipid by specific glycosyltransferases, and the antibodies that people make against the structures they lack are the basis of incompatibility. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the glycosylation of membrane proteins, and the ABO system is one of its clearest clinical consequences. The rhesus system is defined by membrane proteins whose structure determines which antigens are present, and Anfinsen (1973) showed that the antigenic surface depends on the fold of the protein that carries it.

Compatibility testing is performed before a transfusion. The patient's serum is tested against the donor's red cells to detect antibodies that would destroy the transfused cells, and the donor and patient blood groups are determined. The test is designed to detect incompatibility before harm occurs, and its result must be confirmed by a second sample to avoid an error in labelling.

Component therapy gives the patient only the component they need. Red cells, platelets and plasma are prepared from a donation, and each is stored under conditions that preserve its function for a limited time. The storage changes the cells, and the age of the unit and its handling are recorded.

The laboratory maintains quality through controls, calibration and traceability. Each test is run with controls of known reactivity, the equipment is calibrated and every sample and result is linked to its source. Ioannidis (2005) described how errors in identification or labelling can produce serious harm, and the traceability of each sample is a safeguard against this.

Platt (1964) recommended a process that reveals its own failures, and the quality system of the laboratory is designed on that principle, so that a discrepancy in one check is detected by another before it reaches the patient.
