Red cells are produced in large numbers, about two million every second in a healthy adult, from erythroid progenitors in the marrow. The progenitors divide and their nuclei are extruded as the cells mature, leaving a cell packed with haemoglobin and without a nucleus. Erythropoietin, a hormone produced mainly by the kidney in response to low oxygen, drives the survival and division of the progenitors. Till and McCulloch (1961) established the progenitor populations from which these cells derive, and erythropoietin is the principal control on the rate of production.

Haemoglobin is a tetramer of two pairs of globin chains, each carrying a haem group that binds one oxygen molecule. The globin chains fold around the haem, and the correct fold is what places the iron in a position to bind oxygen. Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein's function depends on its folding, and the globins illustrate this clearly: a change in the sequence of a globin can alter its fold, its stability and its oxygen binding.

Haemoglobin binds oxygen cooperatively. The binding of one oxygen molecule alters the structure of the tetramer so that the next binds more readily, which produces the sigmoidal curve of oxygen saturation. The cooperativity allows haemoglobin to load oxygen in the lungs and release it in tissues where the partial pressure is lower. Factors such as acidity, carbon dioxide and temperature shift the curve, and they change the amount of oxygen released to the tissues.

The number of red cells and the amount of haemoglobin they carry are reported in the blood count. Ioannidis (2005) described how a single value of haemoglobin can be interpreted as a property of the patient when it depends on the volume of plasma as well as the red cell mass, and the context of the measure must be considered.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the structure of the proteins and the cellular processes by which red cells lose their nucleus and organelles during maturation.
