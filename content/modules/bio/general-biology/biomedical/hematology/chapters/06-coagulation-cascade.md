Clotting is a cascade of enzyme activations in the plasma. Each enzyme in the sequence is present as an inactive precursor, and it is activated by the enzyme before it, so that a small initial signal is amplified at each step. The cascade ends with thrombin, which converts fibrinogen, a soluble protein, into fibrin, which polymerises into a mesh that stabilises the platelet plug. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the proteolytic activation that drives the cascade, and each step removes a small piece of a precursor to release an active enzyme.

The cascade is started by tissue factor, a protein expressed outside the vessel that is exposed when the vessel is injured. Tissue factor binds a plasma protease, which initiates the activation of the downstream factors. Many of the factors depend on vitamin K for their function, because the vitamin is needed to add carboxyl groups to their glutamate residues, which allows them to bind calcium and the phospholipid surface on which the reactions occur. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the function of a protein depends on its structure, and the calcium binding and the phospholipid interaction are part of the structure that makes the factors active.

The cascade is restrained by natural anticoagulants, which inhibit the active enzymes and prevent the clot from spreading beyond the injury. Antithrombin inhibits thrombin and other factors, and the protein C system inactivates two of the cofactors that drive the cascade. A defect in one of these restraints can allow clotting where it is not needed.

Two clinical tests reflect parts of the cascade. The prothrombin time measures the extrinsic route and the common pathway, and the activated partial thromboplastin time measures the intrinsic route and the common pathway. A prolonged result indicates a deficiency in one of the parts measured, but neither test measures every factor, and the results are interpreted together.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a test that is abnormal in one setting can be taken as a diagnosis without the other tests that distinguish the causes, and Platt (1964) recommended comparing the alternatives that the test patterns separate.
