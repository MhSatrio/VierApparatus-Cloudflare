Molecular diagnosis detects the nucleic acid of a pathogen rather than the organism itself. Saiki et al. (1988) established the amplification on which the method depends, and it can detect a few copies of a target sequence. The advantage is that the pathogen need not be alive or cultivable. Oliver (2010) described organisms that enter a state in which they cannot be grown, and a molecular test can detect such organisms where culture fails. The method is also faster than culture for many organisms, and it can detect fastidious pathogens that would take days to grow.

The sensitivity has a cost. A test that detects a few copies will also detect contamination from previous reactions, colonisation by organisms that are present without causing disease, and nucleic acid from dead organisms that are no longer a threat. A positive result therefore shows that the target is present, not that it is causing the disease, and the interpretation depends on the site, the organism and the clinical picture. A respiratory sample positive for a common colonising organism is not the same as one from a sterile site.

Molecular tests also detect resistance genes, which allows the laboratory to predict susceptibility without growing the organism. The prediction is valid only when the gene is known to confer resistance and when its expression is consistent with the phenotype. A gene that is present but not expressed does not reliably predict resistance, and the molecular result is confirmed by phenotypic testing where the decision depends on it.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a highly sensitive test can produce positives that are real but clinically irrelevant, and the threshold for reporting must consider this. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the alternatives, and a molecular result is most useful when it separates a true infection from colonisation or contamination.

The power of the method lies in its speed and sensitivity, and its value depends on the judgement applied to what it detects.
