A microbiology result reaches the clinician as a report, and the report is the form in which the laboratory's work is judged. A good report names the organism, states its quantity and its significance for the specimen, gives the susceptibility of the agents tested and notes any qualification that limits the result. A report that states an organism without its significance can lead to treatment of a colonisation, and a report that omits a qualification can lead to a false reassurance. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result presented without its limits is accepted more readily than it should be, and the report is the place where those limits are stated.

The significance of an organism depends on the specimen and on whether the organism is a known pathogen at that site. A coagulase-negative staphylococcus in a blood culture from one bottle may be a contaminant, while the same organism in several bottles may be a true infection. The laboratory reports the finding with the number of positive samples and the comment that its interpretation requires the clinical context, rather than presenting a single name as the answer.

The laboratory also acts as a sentinel. The organisms it isolates are counted over time, and an unusual increase in a pathogen or in a resistance pattern can reveal an outbreak or the spread of a resistant clone. Finlay and Falkow (1997) described the features of pathogens that allow them to spread between hosts, and surveillance detects when these features give an advantage in a population. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could reveal the alternatives, and the comparison of isolates by typing distinguishes a true outbreak from a chance increase.

Infection control uses the laboratory's findings. Isolation of patients, cleaning of surfaces and the screening of staff are guided by the organisms detected and their resistance. The effectiveness of these measures is assessed by the same surveillance that detected the problem.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the transmission and the host defences that determine how an infection spreads and where it can be controlled.
