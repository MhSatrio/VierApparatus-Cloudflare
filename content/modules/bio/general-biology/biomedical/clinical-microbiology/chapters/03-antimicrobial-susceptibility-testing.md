Susceptibility testing asks whether an antimicrobial agent can inhibit the growth of an isolate, and the answer guides the choice of treatment. In disk diffusion, a paper disk impregnated with the agent is placed on a lawn of the organism, and the agent diffuses outward, creating a zone in which growth is inhibited. The diameter of the zone is measured and compared with a standard. In broth dilution, the isolate is grown in a series of concentrations of the agent, and the minimum inhibitory concentration is the lowest concentration that prevents visible growth. Both methods depend on a standardised inoculum, because a dense inoculum requires more drug to inhibit it.

The minimum inhibitory concentration is a continuous measure, and the clinical decision requires a category. Breakpoints divide the continuum into susceptible, intermediate and resistant, and they are set by considering the drug concentration achievable in the body, the distribution of the measurements and the clinical outcomes of treatment. A breakpoint is therefore a judgement that combines pharmacology and clinical data, and it changes when either changes.

Finlay and Falkow (1997) described the mechanisms by which bacteria resist drugs and cause disease, and resistance is the reason susceptibility must be tested rather than assumed. The test detects resistance that is expressed under the test conditions, and a mechanism that is not expressed in vitro may still act in the patient, so the result has limits.

Quality control is essential. Reference strains with known susceptibility are tested with each batch of media and disks, and their results must fall within stated ranges before the patient results are released. A failed control shows that the medium, the disk or the inoculum is faulty, and the batch is discarded. Ioannidis (2005) described how an uncontrolled measurement can give a result that looks precise but is biased, and the control strains are the means of detecting such bias.

Platt (1964) recommended testing against alternatives, and the susceptibility result is most useful when it discriminates between drugs that would otherwise be chosen by convention.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular targets that antimicrobials act upon, which explain why some agents work against some organisms and not others.
