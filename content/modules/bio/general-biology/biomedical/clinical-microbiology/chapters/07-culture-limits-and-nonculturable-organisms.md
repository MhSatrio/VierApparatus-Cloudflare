A negative culture is one of the most common results in microbiology, and it is also one of the most often misread. Culture detects organisms that can grow on the media and under the conditions provided, and many pathogens cannot. Oliver (2010) described bacteria that enter the viable but nonculturable state, in which they remain alive and capable of causing disease but do not form colonies under standard conditions. Staley and Konopka (1985) estimated that a large fraction of microbes in natural habitats are not cultivable by standard methods, and the same limit applies to pathogens recovered from patients.

Some pathogens cannot be grown on ordinary media because they depend on host cells. Viruses, chlamydiae, rickettsiae and some other organisms replicate only within living cells, and culture requires cell lines or the organisms must be detected by other means. Others grow slowly, or require specific nutrients that are absent from standard media, and the incubation period may be too short to show growth. A negative result after a short incubation does not exclude the organism.

Antibiotics given before a specimen is taken reduce the yield of culture. The organism may be killed or inhibited in vivo, so that the culture is negative in the presence of an infection. The clinical history must therefore be taken into account when a result is interpreted, and the timing of treatment must be recorded with the specimen.

The consequence is that a negative culture must be interpreted with knowledge of the sensitivity of the method for the expected pathogen. Finlay and Falkow (1997) described the features that pathogens use to invade host cells, which explain why some are invisible to culture from the sites where they act. Ioannidis (2005) described how a test whose sensitivity is not known is taken as a test of absence, and the sensitivity should be stated for each pathogen.

Platt (1964) recommended testing a claim against an alternative, and the question of whether a negative culture excludes infection is answered by a test that could detect the organism by another means.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the intracellular niches in which some pathogens survive, which explain their invisibility to culture.
