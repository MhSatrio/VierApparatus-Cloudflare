The result of a microbiological test is limited by the specimen it is performed on, and a poor specimen cannot be rescued by a better test. The choice of site depends on the disease. A respiratory infection is sampled from the lower airway where possible, because the upper airway carries many commensal organisms, while a urinary infection is sampled by a clean-catch or catheter method to avoid contamination from the urethra. Finlay and Falkow (1997) described the sites that pathogens colonise and invade, and the specimen should be taken from the site where the pathogen is expected.

The timing of collection matters. Specimens taken before antimicrobial treatment have a higher chance of yielding the pathogen, and specimens taken after treatment may be sterile even when the infection is present. The time between collection and processing also matters, because fragile organisms die in transit, and the transport medium is chosen to preserve them.

Contamination is the central risk. Normal flora are present on the skin and mucosa, and a sample taken through them may contain organisms that are not the cause of disease. The laboratory distinguishes between organisms that are expected in a sample and those that are not, and it reports the significance of each. A specimen that is heavily contaminated is rejected or qualified, and the rejection is explained to the clinician.

The laboratory applies criteria for rejection. A specimen that is leaking, that has been stored too long or that is labelled incorrectly cannot be trusted, and its rejection prevents a misleading result. Ioannidis (2005) described how an unreliable input produces a result that appears precise, and the criteria must be applied consistently.

Platt (1964) recommended a process that could reveal its own failures, and the record of specimen rejection and the reasons for it are a means of monitoring the quality of the collection.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the normal microbial communities of the body, whose presence in a specimen is expected and must be distinguished from infection.
