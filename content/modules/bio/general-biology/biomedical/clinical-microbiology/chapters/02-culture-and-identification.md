A clinical specimen is plated on several media at once, chosen to recover the pathogens expected from the site. Blood agar supports most bacteria that cause infection, while chocolate agar is used for fastidious organisms. Selective media suppress normal flora, and differential media reveal the metabolic properties of the colonies. The plates are incubated in the appropriate atmosphere, and they are examined at intervals, since some pathogens grow slowly. Staley and Konopka (1985) described the difficulty of cultivating organisms from natural habitats, and the same difficulty applies to fastidious pathogens, which often require specific media and conditions.

The colonies are first examined by their appearance: size, colour, the presence of haemolysis on blood agar and the odour. A Gram stain of the colony distinguishes Gram-positive from Gram-negative bacteria by the retention of a dye in the cell wall, and the shape of the cells narrows the identity further. Each colony type is then identified by a set of tests, typically biochemical reactions that reveal enzymes characteristic of the species.

Identification by mass spectrometry has become common. The proteins of the colony are ionised and their masses are compared with a library of reference spectra, which gives a species identification within minutes. The method depends on the completeness of the library, and an organism that is not in the library cannot be identified by it. The result is confirmed where the identification matters.

Mixed cultures are common. A specimen may yield two or more organisms, and each must be identified separately, with its significance judged in the context of the specimen. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that is taken as the complete answer may miss a second organism that matters, and the examination of all colony types is part of the method.

Platt (1964) recommended a test that could distinguish between the alternatives, and the identification is most secure when the features that separate the candidate species are tested directly.

The identification of an organism is a sequence of tests, each of which narrows the possibilities, and the final name is only as reliable as the weakest of them.
