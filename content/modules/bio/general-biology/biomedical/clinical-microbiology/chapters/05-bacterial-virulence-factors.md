Pathogenic bacteria succeed in a host by passing through a sequence of steps, each of which requires particular properties. Finlay and Falkow (1997) described the common themes in these properties. The bacterium must first adhere to the surface of the host, often through fimbriae or other adhesins that bind specific host molecules. It must then colonise and, in some cases, invade the cells or tissues, using secretion systems that inject proteins into the host cell or that export toxins. Once established it must evade the immune response, for example by forming a capsule that masks its surface, and it must cause damage, often through toxins that disrupt membranes or host signalling.

The secretion systems are a particularly important class. These are multi-protein machines that span the bacterial envelope and deliver effector proteins into the host cell. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the export of proteins across membranes, and the secretion systems are specialised versions of such export machinery. The effectors they deliver can alter the cytoskeleton, the signalling of the host cell or its death pathways, which lets the bacterium survive inside cells that would otherwise destroy it.

Toxins are classed by their action. Some damage membranes by forming pores, some inhibit protein synthesis by modifying ribosomal components, and some act on the signalling of the cell by modifying its G proteins. The effect depends on the toxin reaching its target, which is determined by its binding to a host receptor.

The presence of a virulence factor in an isolate helps the laboratory decide whether an organism is likely to be causing disease. The detection of a toxin gene in a stool sample, for example, is evidence for the role of the organism, while its absence does not exclude it. Ioannidis (2005) described how the presence of a factor can be taken as proof of causation, and the link between the factor and the disease must be shown in the clinical setting.

Platt (1964) recommended comparing alternatives, and the virulence profile is most informative when it distinguishes a pathogen from a commensal organism with similar features.

The virulence of a bacterium is the sum of the properties it uses at each step, and the laboratory's interpretation depends on which of these properties are present and which are needed for the disease in question.
