Antimicrobial resistance arises when a bacterium changes in a way that allows it to survive a drug that would otherwise kill or inhibit it. The mechanisms fall into a few classes. The target may be modified, so that the drug binds it poorly: changes in the ribosome reduce the binding of antibiotics that act on protein synthesis, and changes in the enzyme that the drug inhibits reduce its effect. The drug may be inactivated by an enzyme, as beta-lactamases hydrolyse the beta-lactam ring of penicillins and cephalosporins. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the activity of an enzyme depends on its structure, and the enzymes that inactivate drugs are proteins whose specificity is set by their fold.

The drug may be pumped out of the cell by efflux systems that remove it faster than it can act. The membrane may be made less permeable, so that the drug enters more slowly. Each mechanism reduces the effective concentration of the drug at its target, and a bacterium may carry several mechanisms that together produce high-level resistance.

Resistance genes spread between bacteria. They are often carried on plasmids, which are small circular DNA molecules that can be transferred between cells by conjugation, and on transposons, which move within and between genomes. This transfer means that resistance can appear in a species that did not previously carry it, and a species name alone does not reliably predict the resistance of an isolate.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the mobile genetic elements and the processes of gene transfer that allow this spread. The laboratory's choice of treatment must therefore consider the resistance of the particular isolate, and not only the expected profile of its species.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a pattern that is common in one setting may not hold in another, and the local pattern of resistance must be monitored. Finlay and Falkow (1997) described the virulence that often accompanies resistance, and the two may be transferred together.

Resistance is an evolutionary response to the selective pressure of the drugs used, and the choice of antimicrobial use shapes which mechanisms spread.
