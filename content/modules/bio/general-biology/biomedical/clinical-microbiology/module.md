# Clinical Microbiology

The Microbial Cultivation topic described how microbes are grown and counted in research. Clinical microbiology applies the same methods to the diagnosis of infection in patients, where the aim is a result that is accurate, timely and interpretable by a clinician. The laboratory must recover the organism from a specimen that often contains many other microbes, identify it, test the drugs that would treat it and report the result in a form that guides treatment.

The chapters follow a diagnostic sequence. Specimen collection comes first, because the quality of the result depends on the site, the timing and the handling of the sample. Culture and identification follow, then the testing of antimicrobial susceptibility. Molecular detection of pathogen nucleic acid is described as an alternative and complement to culture, and the virulence and resistance of bacteria are then explained as the reasons the results matter. The final chapters address the limits of culture and the reporting that makes a result usable.

The diagnosis of infection depends on matching the organism to the disease, and a positive result from a site where normal flora are present may not indicate infection. Finlay and Falkow (1997) described the common features of pathogens, and the interpretation of a culture requires knowing which organisms are likely pathogens in a given specimen. Ioannidis (2005) described how a positive result can be treated as the cause without the evidence that links it, and Platt (1964) recommended comparing the candidate organisms against one another.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the host defences and the cell biology of infection that determine what a culture reveals.
