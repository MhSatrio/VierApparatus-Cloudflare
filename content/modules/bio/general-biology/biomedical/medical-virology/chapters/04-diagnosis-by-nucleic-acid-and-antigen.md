Viral diagnosis detects either the viral nucleic acid or a viral antigen. Nucleic acid detection uses amplification, as Saiki et al. (1988) established for DNA, and for RNA viruses it is preceded by reverse transcription. Bustin (2000) described the quantitative form of the method, which allows the amount of virus to be measured as well as its presence. Amplification is highly sensitive and specific, and it can detect the virus early in infection, when the amount is small, but it detects the genome whether or not the virus is still infectious.

Antigen detection looks for a viral protein, usually with an immunoassay of the kind described for other proteins. It is faster and cheaper than amplification, and it can be performed at the bedside, but it is less sensitive, because the antigen is present in smaller amounts than the nucleic acid in many infections, and its detection depends on the time since the onset of symptoms.

The timing of the test determines its result. Early in infection, the viral load may be below the detection limit of the antigen test, and a negative result does not exclude infection. Later in infection, the viral load may fall below the level that the test can detect, or the virus may have been cleared, and the nucleic acid may persist after the infection is no longer active. The result must therefore be interpreted with the day of symptoms and the likely course.

A positive result also requires interpretation. Nucleic acid can be detected in people who carry the virus without disease, and the detection of a virus in a symptomatic patient does not establish that it caused the symptoms. Ioannidis (2005) described how a highly sensitive test can detect irrelevant findings, and Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the alternatives, such as a second virus or a non-infectious cause of the same symptoms.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the viral replication that produces the material detected, and the amount of material depends on the stage of the infection.

The choice of method and the interpretation of its result are both determined by the time since infection and the question being answered.
