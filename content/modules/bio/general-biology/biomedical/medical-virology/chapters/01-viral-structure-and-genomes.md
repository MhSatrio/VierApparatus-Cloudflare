A virus particle, or virion, consists of a genome surrounded by a protein shell called a capsid, and some viruses have an additional lipid envelope derived from the host membrane. The capsid may be helical or icosahedral, and its symmetry is determined by the repeated assembly of identical protein subunits. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the self-assembly of such structures, which allows a small genome to specify a large and stable particle.

The genome may be DNA or RNA, and it may be single or double stranded. Watson and Crick (1953) described the double helix, which is the form of most DNA genomes, but many viruses have single-stranded DNA or RNA, and RNA genomes may be positive sense, able to act directly as messenger RNA, or negative sense, which must first be copied. These differences determine the strategy of replication, since the virus must supply the enzymes needed to copy the genome that the host cell does not provide.

The form of the genome also determines how it is detected. Nucleic acid amplification must be designed for the genome type, and an RNA virus requires a reverse transcription step before it can be amplified. The genome may also vary between strains, and the regions chosen for detection must be conserved for the test to be sensitive across the variants.

Viruses mutate rapidly, particularly RNA viruses, because their replication enzymes lack proofreading, and this produces a population of related genomes within each infected person. The diversity has consequences for diagnosis, for drug resistance and for vaccines, and it means that a single genome sequence describes only one member of a population.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a test designed against one sequence may fail against a variant that differs in the target, and the evidence for sensitivity should be measured against the variants that circulate. Platt (1964) recommended comparing the alternatives, and the detection of a variant in the population is a test of this kind.

The genome determines the rules of replication, and understanding it is the first step in understanding the infection.
