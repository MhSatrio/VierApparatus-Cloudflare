Viruses emerge in human populations when a virus that circulates in another species acquires the ability to infect and spread among people, or when a virus already present in people changes in a way that allows it to spread more widely. The mechanisms are mutation, which alters the proteins that bind host receptors, recombination, which exchanges segments of genomes when two viruses infect one cell, and the transmission of the virus across species, which brings it into contact with a new host. Watson and Crick (1953) described the molecular basis of the genetic change that these processes depend on, and the high mutation rate of RNA viruses makes them particularly prone to such change.

Surveillance detects the emergence. Laboratories report the detection of viruses, the proportion of tests that are positive and the clinical severity of the cases, and unusual patterns prompt investigation. Sequencing of the viral genome reveals the relation between isolates, allowing the origin and spread of a virus to be traced. The value of surveillance depends on the consistency of the reporting and the coverage of the population tested.

The distinction between a true increase and a change in detection is essential. A new test or an increase in testing will reveal infections that were already present, and the number of positive results rises without any change in the virus. Ioannidis (2005) described how such changes in detection can be mistaken for changes in disease, and the surveillance must account for the testing effort. The denominator, the number of people tested, is as important as the numerator, the number positive.

Platt (1964) recommended comparing alternatives, and the test of whether a virus is truly spreading is to compare its proportion among tests over time with its proportion in a fixed sample, which discriminates a real change from one caused by the testing.

Emergence is a process of selection acting on variation, and the surveillance system is the means of seeing it as it happens.
