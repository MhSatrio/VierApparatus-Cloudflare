After a viral infection, the immune system produces antibodies against the virus, and their appearance follows a predictable sequence. IgM antibodies appear first, typically within days to a week of onset, and they are followed by IgG antibodies, which appear later and persist for years. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the clonal expansion and class switching that produce this sequence, and the later antibodies are of higher affinity because of the maturation that occurs during the response.

Serology detects these antibodies by immunoassay, using the enzyme-linked principle that Engvall and Perlmann (1971) introduced and the labelling principle of Coons et al. (1941). The result is usually reported as a titre, the highest dilution of the serum that still gives a positive result. A single positive result shows that antibody is present, but it does not show when the infection occurred, since IgG can persist long after recovery.

The diagnosis of recent infection relies on a rising titre. A paired sample, taken early in the illness and again two to four weeks later, shows a fourfold or greater rise in the titre of IgG in a recent infection, and a stable titre shows that the antibody was present before the illness. The paired comparison is more informative than either sample alone, because it measures the change rather than the presence of antibody.

The presence of IgM is evidence of recent infection, but it is not decisive. IgM can persist for weeks or months, and it can be produced in reactivation of latent virus and in cross-reactive responses to related viruses. The laboratory must therefore interpret IgM with the IgG result and the clinical picture.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a single test can give a result that is taken as a diagnosis, and Platt (1964) recommended comparing the alternatives, such as a cross-reacting virus, that could produce the same pattern. The serological result is most useful when it discriminates between recent and past infection.

The serological response is a record of exposure, and its interpretation depends on the timing and the change between samples.
