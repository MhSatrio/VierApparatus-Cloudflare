The replication cycle of a virus begins with attachment to a receptor on the surface of a susceptible cell. The virus then enters, either by fusing its envelope with the membrane or by being taken up into a vesicle, and it releases its genome into the cytoplasm or nucleus. The genome is replicated and its genes are expressed using the cell's machinery, together with any enzymes the virus carries, and the components are assembled into new particles that leave the cell by budding or by lysis. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular pathways that viruses hijack, and each step depends on the host's proteins.

Hershey and Chase (1952) showed in bacteriophage that the nucleic acid is the component that enters the cell and directs the production of new particles, while the protein coat remains outside. The result established that the genetic information of a virus resides in its nucleic acid, and the replication cycle follows from this principle.

Two broad outcomes follow. In the lytic cycle, the virus produces many progeny and destroys the cell, and the tissue damage is the consequence of this destruction together with the immune response. In the latent cycle, the viral genome persists in the cell without producing new particles, and it may reactivate later. Herpesviruses are the best known example, and the latent state explains why infection recurs without re-exposure. Some viruses instead transform the cell, altering its growth so that it divides without control, and this is the link to the cancers discussed in the Oncology module.

The pattern of disease reflects the cycle. A virus that destroys cells in a tissue causes damage proportional to the number of cells infected, while a virus that persists causes disease through its effect on the cell and on the immune response. The timing of the cycle also determines when the virus can be detected and when the antibody response appears.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the presence of a virus in a tissue does not establish its role in a disease, and Platt (1964) recommended testing the alternatives, such as whether the damage follows the replication or the immune response to it.

The replication cycle links the molecular life of the virus to the clinical course of the infection, and each stage is a potential target for treatment.
