A vaccine induces immunity without causing the disease, so that the body can respond quickly when the virus is encountered. Vaccines take several forms. Live attenuated vaccines contain a virus that has been weakened so that it replicates without causing serious disease, and they induce strong and long-lasting responses. Inactivated and subunit vaccines contain killed virus or a viral protein, and they induce mainly antibody responses. Nucleic acid vaccines deliver the gene for a viral protein, which the host cells express and present to the immune system. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the presentation of antigens that underlies the induction of these responses, and the form of the vaccine determines which arm of the response is strongest.

The response that protects is not always the one that is measured. Neutralising antibodies that block the virus from entering cells are often a correlate of protection, but for some viruses the protection depends on T cells, and the antibody level does not predict it. A correlate of protection is a measured immune response that is shown to predict protection in trials, and its use allows a new vaccine to be judged without a full efficacy trial. Coons et al. (1941) established the labelling of antibodies that underlies the assays that measure these responses.

The efficacy trial is the decisive test. Participants are randomly allocated to the vaccine or a control, and the number of cases of the disease is compared between the groups over a defined period. Platt (1964) described the strong inference that such a comparison makes possible, since a vaccine that does not prevent disease will show no difference. The trial must be large enough to detect the effect, and the case definition must be specified in advance.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that is based on surrogate measures can be overinterpreted, and the surrogate should be validated before it replaces the trial outcome. The evaluation of a vaccine is therefore a chain of evidence, from the immune response to the prevention of disease in a population.

A vaccine is judged by whether it prevents disease in people, and the immune response is the means of predicting that outcome rather than the outcome itself.
