Antiviral drugs act at each stage of the replication cycle. Some block attachment or entry, some inhibit the viral polymerase that copies the genome, some inhibit the protease that cleaves viral polyproteins into functional proteins, and some block the release of new particles. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the steps of replication, and each class of drug targets a step that the virus performs with its own proteins, since these are the most specific targets. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the activity of a protein depends on its structure, and drugs that bind the active site of a viral enzyme exploit the shape of that site.

Antiviral drugs are harder to design than antibacterial agents, because the virus uses the host cell's machinery for most of its replication. A drug that acts on a host process would also affect the cell, so the targets are largely the viral proteins. The number of such targets is small, and the drugs are often specific to a single virus or a small group.

Resistance arises when a mutation in the viral target reduces the binding of the drug while preserving the function of the protein. Viral populations are large and mutate rapidly, particularly RNA viruses, so resistant variants are present before treatment in many cases, and the drug selects for them. Combination therapy reduces this risk, because a variant must acquire resistance to several drugs at once, which is less likely.

The emergence of resistance is detected by genotypic testing, which sequences the target gene and looks for known resistance mutations, and by phenotypic testing, which measures the growth of the virus in the presence of the drug. Ioannidis (2005) described how a mutation that is associated with resistance may not produce it in every setting, and the phenotypic test is the more direct evidence.

Platt (1964) recommended comparing alternatives, and the comparison of a drug with a placebo or with another drug is the means of showing whether an effect is real.

The drugs act on the virus at the points where it depends on its own machinery, and resistance follows from the same mutation that gives the virus its variability.
