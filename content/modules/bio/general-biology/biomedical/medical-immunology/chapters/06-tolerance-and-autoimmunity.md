The immune system must recognise and attack foreign molecules while leaving the body's own tissues intact. Tolerance to self is maintained in two ways. Central tolerance eliminates or inactivates developing lymphocytes that react strongly with self antigens, in the thymus for T cells and in the bone marrow for B cells. Peripheral tolerance acts on the lymphocytes that escape this selection, and it includes the suppression of responses by regulatory T cells, the anergy of cells that receive a signal without co-stimulation and the deletion of cells that are chronically stimulated. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signals that control the survival and activation of lymphocytes, and the balance of these signals determines whether a cell is tolerised or activated.

Matzinger (2002) proposed that tolerance is maintained by the absence of danger signals as well as by the recognition of self. A cell that encounters self antigen in the absence of inflammation does not receive the signals needed for activation, and this explains why most self molecules are tolerated. The danger model also explains how autoimmunity can arise when tissue is damaged and the damage releases signals that activate responses to self antigens that would otherwise be ignored.

Autoimmune disease occurs when tolerance fails, and the failure may be genetic, environmental or both. Genetic variants that alter the selection of T cells or the function of regulatory cells increase the risk, and infections or tissue damage can provide the danger signals that trigger a response to self. The specific autoantibodies or autoreactive T cells found in a patient are used as markers, but their presence does not by itself establish the disease, since many healthy people carry some of them.

Ioannidis (2005) described how an association between an autoantibody and a disease may reflect the disease rather than cause it, and the evidence for causation requires that the antibody be shown to contribute to the tissue damage. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate these explanations, such as the transfer of the response or its removal, to show whether it is responsible for the disease.

Tolerance is an active state maintained by many mechanisms, and its failure is a loss of control rather than the appearance of a new response.
