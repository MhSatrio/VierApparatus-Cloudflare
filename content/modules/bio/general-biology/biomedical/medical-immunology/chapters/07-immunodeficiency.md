An immunodeficiency is a failure of part of the defence, and the pattern of infections it causes reveals which part has failed. A defect in antibody production leaves the patient prone to infections by encapsulated bacteria, because antibody and complement are needed to clear them. A defect in T cells predisposes to viral, fungal and intracellular infections, while a defect in neutrophils predisposes to bacterial and fungal infections of the skin and lungs. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cells and the signals whose failure produces these patterns, and the infection that occurs is a guide to the defect.

Primary immunodeficiencies are inherited, and they are classified by the component affected. Some involve the development of the lymphocytes, so that few or no T or B cells are produced. Others involve the function of an individual cell, such as the killing of pathogens by phagocytes or the production of a particular antibody class. Inherited disorders of complement have a distinctive pattern of infection by encapsulated bacteria and of autoimmune disease, because complement helps clear immune complexes.

Secondary immunodeficiency is more common. It follows infection, malnutrition, malignancy or treatment with drugs that suppress the immune system. The deficiency may be transient or permanent, and its cause must be sought, since the treatment depends on the mechanism.

The laboratory assesses each component. The number and function of lymphocytes are measured by flow cytometry, the levels of immunoglobulin are measured by immunoassay, and the function of phagocytes is tested by their ability to kill bacteria or to produce reactive oxygen species. Ioannidis (2005) described how a single measurement of a component may not reflect the function of the system, and the functional assays are needed alongside the counts.

Platt (1964) recommended a test that could distinguish the explanations of an infection pattern, and the assessment of each component in turn is the means by which the defect is located. Matzinger (2002) proposed that the immune system responds to danger, and the failure of the response to a danger signal can be a sign of deficiency in the sensing or the signalling.

An immunodeficiency is identified by the pattern of infection and confirmed by the measurement of the component that fails, and both are needed for a secure diagnosis.
