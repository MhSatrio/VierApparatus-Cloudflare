The clinical immunology laboratory uses a small set of assays, each designed to answer a particular question. Immunoassays measure antibodies or antigens in serum, and the enzyme-linked format introduced by Engvall and Perlmann (1971) is the most common. Flow cytometry, built on the labelling principle of Coons et al. (1941), counts and characterises cell populations, and functional assays measure whether the cells can act. Complement assays measure the activity of a cascade of serum proteins. Each assay answers a question about one part of the response, and a result from one part does not describe the others.

The matching of assay to question is the first decision. A test for antibody shows that exposure has occurred or that a response is present, but not whether it is protective. A test for a cell population shows how many cells are present, but not whether they function. The choice must follow the clinical question, and an assay used for a question it cannot answer produces a misleading result.

Reporting requires the uncertainty and the reference interval. An immunoassay has a precision that depends on the concentration, and a result near a cut-off is less secure than one far from it. The reference interval depends on the method and the population, as described for other analytes, and a result must be compared with the interval for the same method. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result presented without its uncertainty appears more definite than it is, and the report should state the precision with the value.

The clinical context gives the result its meaning. An antibody that is raised in a healthy person after vaccination is different from one raised in a patient with disease, and the same value may have different meaning in different settings. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the alternatives, and the context allows the laboratory to frame the question so that the result can discriminate.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the components of the response that each assay measures, and the report should connect the result to the component it measures.
