Köhler and Milstein (1975) fused antibody-producing spleen cells with a myeloma cell line, creating hybrid cells that secreted a single antibody and could be grown indefinitely. The hybridoma combined the specificity of the B cell with the immortality of the tumour cell, and each clone produced antibody of one defined specificity. The method made it possible to produce unlimited quantities of an antibody against a chosen antigen, which had previously been obtainable only as a mixture from immunised animals.

The clones are screened for the desired specificity, and the chosen clone is grown and maintained. Subclones are made to ensure that the line remains stable, since hybridomas can lose their antibody production or change their specificity over time. Coons et al. (1941) established that an antibody carrying a label can reveal its target, and the monoclonal antibody is used in the same way, with the advantage that the reagent is identical from batch to batch.

Monoclonal antibodies are used in diagnosis to detect antigens in tissues and in fluids, in therapy to block a target or to direct a toxic agent to cells that carry it, and in research as a reagent. Each use requires validation. The antibody must recognise its intended target and not a related molecule, and its performance must be measured in the sample type in which it will be used. A monoclonal antibody that is specific in one assay may cross-react in another.

Therapeutic antibodies raise further questions. The antibody must reach its target in the body, act on it without unacceptable effects and be cleared at an acceptable rate. The clinical trials that test them must be designed to show benefit against the alternatives, as Platt (1964) recommended for any comparison.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a reagent that is assumed to be specific may not be, and the validation of each monoclonal antibody against its intended target is the means of checking this assumption.

The monoclonal antibody is a clone of a single B cell and carries its specificity faithfully, but the specificity must still be shown to fit the question for which the antibody is used.
