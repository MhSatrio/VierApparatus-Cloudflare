A T cell does not recognise a free antigen. It recognises a peptide that is displayed on the surface of another cell, bound to a molecule of the major histocompatibility complex. Antigens are taken up by antigen-presenting cells, particularly dendritic cells, and degraded into peptides. Peptides from the cytosol are loaded onto MHC class I molecules, which present them to cytotoxic CD8 T cells, while peptides from endocytosed material are loaded onto MHC class II molecules, which present them to helper CD4 T cells. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the pathways of degradation and loading, and the separation of the two routes explains why different T cells respond to different sources of antigen.

The MHC molecule is a folded protein that forms a groove, and the peptide sits within it. Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein's structure determines its function, and the groove must be the correct shape to bind peptides of the right length and sequence. The variability of the MHC genes means that each person presents a different set of peptides, which affects the immune response to infection and the risk of some diseases.

T cell activation requires two signals. The first is the recognition of the peptide-MHC complex by the T cell receptor, and the second is a co-stimulatory signal from the antigen-presenting cell. Without the second signal, the T cell may become unresponsive, which is one way the immune system avoids responding to self. Helper T cells then secrete cytokines that direct other immune cells, and cytotoxic T cells kill infected or abnormal cells by inducing their death.

Matzinger (2002) argued that the co-stimulatory signal depends on danger, which means that an antigen presented without danger is less likely to provoke a response. The danger model thus connects the presentation of antigen to the context in which it occurs.

The measurement of T cell responses is performed with assays that count cells producing cytokines, and each assay answers a specific question. Ioannidis (2005) described how the choice of assay and its threshold can change the result, and the criteria should be fixed in advance. Platt (1964) recommended comparing alternatives, which is the purpose of including appropriate controls.

The T cell response is the link between recognition and the destruction of infected cells, and its coordination determines the quality of the defence.
