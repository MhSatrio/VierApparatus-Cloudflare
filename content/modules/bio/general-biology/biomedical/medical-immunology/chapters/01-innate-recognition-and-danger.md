Innate immunity responds within minutes to hours to molecules that are shared by many microbes and that the host recognises as foreign. Pattern recognition receptors, expressed on macrophages, dendritic cells and neutrophils, detect these conserved patterns, including components of bacterial cell walls, bacterial nucleic acids and the flagellin of motile bacteria. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the receptors and the signalling pathways they activate, which lead to the production of inflammatory mediators and to the expression of molecules needed for the adaptive response.

Matzinger (2002) argued that the immune system responds not simply to what is foreign, but to what is harmful. The danger model proposes that tissue damage releases signals from injured cells that alert the immune system, so that a response is directed where damage occurs. Commensal bacteria that cause no harm are tolerated, while the same microbes that invade tissue trigger a response. The model explains why the immune system tolerates much that is foreign, such as food and the normal flora, and why autoimmunity can arise when damaged tissue releases molecules that stimulate the response to self.

The danger signals are molecules that are normally inside the cell and are released or exposed when the cell dies. Their recognition by receptors links tissue damage to the inflammatory response, which explains why sterile injury can also cause inflammation. The distinction between a microbial pattern and a damage signal is not always clear, and the same receptor may respond to both.

The model has been debated, and some of its predictions have been difficult to test. Ioannidis (2005) described how a model that fits many observations can be accepted before its specific predictions are checked, and the strength of the danger model rests on experiments that test its predictions directly.

Platt (1964) recommended testing alternatives, and the comparison of a danger-based response with a self and non-self response is one such test, carried out by manipulating the signals and measuring the outcome.

The innate response is the first decision the immune system makes, and its direction determines the character of the adaptive response that follows.
