An antibody is a Y-shaped molecule made of two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains, each folded into immunoglobulin domains. The two arms carry the variable regions, which form the antigen-binding sites, and the stem is the constant region, which determines the class of the antibody and its interaction with other immune components. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the folding of a protein determines its function, and the immunoglobulin domains are folded units whose structure places the variable loops in the right geometry to bind antigen.

The variable region is generated by the recombination of gene segments during the development of B cells, which creates an enormous diversity of binding sites. The specificity of each antibody is set by the amino acids of the loops, and a small change in the sequence can alter the affinity or the specificity of binding. Coons et al. (1941) established that an antibody can carry a label and remain specific, and the antigen-binding property is the basis of all immunoassays.

The constant region defines five classes: immunoglobulin G, which is the most abundant in serum and crosses the placenta; immunoglobulin M, which is the first produced in a response and forms pentamers; immunoglobulin A, which is the main antibody of mucosal secretions; immunoglobulin E, which mediates allergic responses; and immunoglobulin D, whose role is less clear. Each class has different effector functions, such as activation of complement or binding to receptors on phagocytes, and these functions determine how an antibody protects the body.

The class of antibody is measured in the laboratory, and the proportion of each class changes with the stage of an immune response and with disease. The measurement answers different questions, and a change in one class alone does not establish the diagnosis. Ioannidis (2005) described how a single laboratory value can be interpreted as a diagnosis without the context that gives it meaning, and the class measurement should be read with the clinical picture and the other tests.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell biology of antibody production, which links the structure of the molecule to the cells that make it.
