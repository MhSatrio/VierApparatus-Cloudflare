A B cell carries surface immunoglobulin of a single specificity, and it is activated when this receptor binds its antigen. Activation is strongest when the B cell also receives help from a T cell that recognises a peptide from the same antigen, and the T cell provides cytokines and surface signals that drive the B cell to divide. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signals that control the division and differentiation of lymphocytes, and the B cell response depends on the timing of these signals as well as their presence.

The activated B cell expands into a clone of identical cells, and its progeny differentiate in two directions. Plasma cells secrete large amounts of antibody of the same specificity and survive for a time in the bone marrow, which produces the antibody that protects the body in the days and weeks after an infection. Memory cells persist for years and respond more quickly when the same antigen returns. The balance between these fates is controlled by the strength and duration of the signals received.

During a response, the antibody improves in affinity. Somatic mutation introduces changes into the variable genes of the activated B cells, and the cells whose antibodies bind antigen more tightly are selected and expanded. This process, called affinity maturation, explains why antibodies produced later in a response bind more strongly than those produced early. Köhler and Milstein (1975) demonstrated that a single B cell clone can be fused with a tumour cell to produce a line that secretes one antibody indefinitely, and this approach exploited the clonal nature of the response.

Monoclonal antibodies derived from such lines are now used in diagnosis and therapy, and their specificity is a consequence of the clonal origin of the B cell. The properties of a monoclonal antibody must be verified for each use, since the selection of the clone determines its performance.

Platt (1964) recommended testing against alternatives, and the claim that a response reflects a single clone or several is tested by sequencing the antibodies and by measuring clonal expansion directly. Ioannidis (2005) described how an apparently uniform response can conceal heterogeneity, which the same measurements reveal.

The B cell response is a process of selection, and its outcome is an antibody whose quality reflects the history of the response.
