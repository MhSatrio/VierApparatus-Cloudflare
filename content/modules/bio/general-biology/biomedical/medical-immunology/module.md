# Medical Immunology

The Immunology Fundamentals and Immunology topics described the cells and molecules of the immune system in general terms. Medical immunology applies these to the patient: how the defences protect against infection and cancer, how they go wrong in autoimmunity and immunodeficiency and how the laboratory measures them. Clinical decisions in this field depend on distinguishing a normal response from an abnormal one, and on knowing which assay answers which question.

The chapters follow the response from its first recognition to its measurement. Innate recognition, and the model of danger that Matzinger (2002) proposed to explain it, comes first. Antigen presentation and the T cell response, the structure and classes of antibody, and the B cell response that Köhler and Milstein (1975) made available as monoclonal antibodies follow. The later chapters address tolerance and autoimmunity, immunodeficiency, and the assays by which the laboratory measures the response.

Immunological tests are highly sensitive to conditions, and results are commonly misread when the reference population or the assay is not matched to the question. Ioannidis (2005) described how such misreadings arise, and Platt (1964) recommended tests that discriminate between the explanations of an abnormal result. The chapters therefore link each assay to the question it can answer.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cells and the molecular signals by which the responses are coordinated.
