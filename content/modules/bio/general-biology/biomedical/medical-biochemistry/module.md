# Medical Biochemistry

The Biochemistry and Metabolism modules described the pathways that convert nutrients into energy and building blocks, and the Enzymology module described how the enzymes that drive them are measured. Medical biochemistry applies these principles to the health of the body: how the regulation of metabolism fails in disease, how the failure appears in measurable analytes and how a clinical laboratory turns those measurements into information that can be acted upon.

The chapters move from the principles of metabolic regulation to their clinical expression. Enzyme kinetics and the clinical assays that measure enzymes come first, followed by the analytes whose reference intervals define normal and abnormal values. Lipid and glucose homeostasis are then described, with the mitochondrial disorders that affect energy production, and the inborn errors of metabolism that arise from single enzyme defects. The final chapter addresses the quality and interpretation of results.

Mitchell (1961) proposed that the energy of electron transport is stored as a proton gradient that drives ATP synthesis, and disorders of this process link the pathways of the cell to disease. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the structure of an enzyme determines its activity, so that a mutation that alters the fold can disable a pathway.

The clinical use of these measurements is a decision made under uncertainty. Ioannidis (2005) described how a diagnostic or prognostic claim that is not tested against an alternative can mislead, and Platt (1964) argued for tests that could show a clinical interpretation to be wrong. The chapters treat the reference interval, the sensitivity and the specificity of an analyte as the basis of any judgement made from it.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the metabolic pathways whose disruption produces the disorders discussed here.
