Blood glucose is held within a narrow range by a balance between its supply from food and from the liver, and its removal by muscle and adipose tissue. Insulin, released when glucose rises, promotes uptake into muscle and fat, stimulates storage as glycogen and fat, and suppresses the production of glucose by the liver. Glucagon, released when glucose falls, has the opposite effects. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling of these hormones, and the balance between them determines the glucose level at any moment.

Insulin acts through a receptor whose activation triggers a cascade of phosphorylation events. The uptake of glucose into muscle and fat depends on the translocation of a glucose transporter to the cell surface, which is stimulated by insulin. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the structure of a protein determines its activity, and the insulin receptor and its transporters must fold correctly to respond to the hormone. A failure of the receptor or the transporter reduces the response, and the glucose remains high.

Two laboratory measures are commonly used. Fasting glucose reports the concentration at one moment, after a period without food, and it changes quickly with diet and stress. Glycated haemoglobin reports the proportion of haemoglobin that has been modified by glucose, and because red cells live for weeks, it reflects the average glucose over the preceding two to three months. The two measures answer different questions, and a single value of either does not describe the other.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a diagnostic threshold chosen from a population can misclassify individuals when the measure is noisy, and the threshold must be judged against its false-positive rate. Platt (1964) recommended comparing alternatives, and glucose measures are best read together over time.

The homeostasis of glucose is a dynamic balance, and a single measurement shows a point on a curve rather than the position of the system.
