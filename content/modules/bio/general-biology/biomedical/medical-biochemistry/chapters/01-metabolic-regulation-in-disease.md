Metabolism is regulated at several levels. Allosteric effectors bind enzymes and change their activity within seconds, feedback inhibition shuts down a pathway when its product accumulates, and hormones such as insulin and glucagon alter the expression and activity of many enzymes over minutes to hours. Alberts et al. (2022) describe these controls as a layered system in which each level compensates for the others. Disease alters one or more layers, and the balance of the whole system shifts.

When a control is disturbed, the concentrations of the intermediates change before the products do. A pathway whose end product is held in check by feedback may accumulate its substrates when the feedback is lost, and the intermediates build up in the blood or in the tissue. The clinical laboratory measures these concentrations, and an elevated or reduced analyte therefore reports the state of a pathway, not necessarily the failure of a single enzyme.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the activity of an enzyme depends on its structure, and allosteric regulation works by changing that structure. A mutation or a modification that alters the shape of a regulatory site can therefore change the response of the enzyme to its effectors without changing its catalytic site.

The interpretation of a changed analyte is therefore uncertain. The same concentration may arise from a change in the synthesis of the analyte, its breakdown, its clearance or its distribution between tissues. Ioannidis (2005) described how a single biomarker is often taken to indicate a single cause, and the alternative explanations should be considered before the cause is assigned.

Platt (1964) recommended that a clinical interpretation be stated so that it could be tested against an alternative. Each measured change is therefore read as one of several possible states of the regulatory system.
