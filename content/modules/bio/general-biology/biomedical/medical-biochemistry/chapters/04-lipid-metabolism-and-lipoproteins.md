Fats are insoluble in water, so they are carried in the blood within lipoprotein particles. Each particle has a core of triglycerides and cholesteryl esters surrounded by a shell of phospholipid, free cholesterol and apolipoproteins. The apolipoproteins are proteins that give the particle its structure and identify it to the enzymes and receptors that act on it. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the lipid transport system as a set of particles of different density, each with its own origin and fate, and the classes are defined by their density and their composition.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein's function follows from its folded structure, and the apolipoproteins illustrate this. Their amphipathic helices bind the lipid surface of the particle, and a change in their sequence can alter how the particle is recognised by the receptors that remove it from the blood. Many of the inherited disorders of lipid transport are disorders of this recognition.

The clinical laboratory measures the total cholesterol, the triglycerides and the cholesterol carried in some classes, but these are not the particles themselves. The concentration of the cholesterol in a fraction is a property of many particles, and the number of particles may differ from the mass of lipid they carry. A measurement of particle number, where it is available, gives a different picture from one of mass, and the two can disagree.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a biomarker that is correlated with a condition can be taken as a cause of it, and the lipid measures are a frequent example. The association must be tested against the alternatives, and the measurements are read as one part of a larger picture.

Platt (1964) recommended comparing alternatives, and the interpretation of a lipid profile is most informative when the classes are read together rather than in isolation.
