An analyte is a substance measured in a clinical sample, such as a metabolite, a protein or an enzyme. Its meaning is defined by a reference interval, the range of values found in a healthy population under stated conditions. The interval is usually set as the central ninety-five per cent of the values from a reference group, which means that about one in twenty healthy people will fall outside it by chance alone. This expected rate of false positives must be kept in mind whenever a single result is interpreted.

The reference interval is specific to the method. Two methods that measure the same analyte may give different values, because they respond differently to related molecules or to the matrix, and an interval established with one method cannot be applied to another. The same is true of the population: age, sex, diet, body size and ethnicity may all shift the distribution, and the group from which the interval is drawn must be described.

Conditions of collection also matter. Fasting state, time of day, posture and the time before analysis can change the concentration of many analytes, and the collection protocol is part of the reference interval. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the structure of a protein determines its behaviour in solution, and the stability of an analyte in the sample depends on the handling after collection.

A result outside the interval is not automatically abnormal. Its meaning depends on the probability that it arose by chance, the clinical context and the other results. Ioannidis (2005) described how a single positive result from a test with a modest positive predictive value is more likely to be false than true when the condition is rare, and this is a central consideration in the interpretation of any analyte.

Platt (1964) recommended comparing alternatives, and a result is most informative when it is compared with other measures that separate the possible explanations. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biological variation of the metabolic systems that produce the analytes.
