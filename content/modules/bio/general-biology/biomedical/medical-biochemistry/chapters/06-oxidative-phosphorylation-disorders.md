Mitchell (1961) proposed that electron transport pumps protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane, and that the resulting gradient drives ATP synthesis through the ATP synthase. The electron transport chain and the synthase are therefore coupled by an electrochemical gradient rather than by a direct chemical link. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the complexes of the chain and the synthase, and each is a multi-protein assembly whose subunits must be made, folded and assembled correctly for the system to function.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein's structure determines its function, and the complexes of the chain depend on the correct assembly of many subunits. A defect in one subunit can disable a complex, and the loss of activity reduces the flow of electrons and the production of ATP. Many of the inherited disorders of this system are defects of assembly, in which the subunits are produced but do not form a functional complex.

Tissues that depend most on oxidative metabolism are the most affected. Brain, heart and skeletal muscle have high energy demands, and a reduction in oxidative capacity impairs them before other tissues. The symptoms of these disorders therefore often appear in these organs, although the defect itself may be present in all cells. The pattern is a consequence of the energy demand of the tissue rather than of the location of the defect.

The investigation of these disorders combines biochemical and genetic methods. The activities of the individual complexes are measured in a tissue sample, and the genetic basis is identified by sequencing the nuclear and mitochondrial genomes. Ioannidis (2005) described how a single measured deficiency may be attributed to a cause that is not established, and the link between activity and genotype must be shown directly.

Platt (1964) recommended comparing alternatives, and a biochemical profile is most informative when it distinguishes between defects in different complexes or in the synthase.
