An inborn error of metabolism is an inherited defect in a single enzyme, usually caused by a mutation that reduces its activity or stability. The enzyme catalyses one step of a pathway, and its loss has two consequences: the substrate of the step accumulates, and the product is no longer made. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the pathways in which these steps lie, and the effects of a block depend on which intermediates build up and which products are lost. Some substrates are toxic at high concentration, and others are harmless, which determines the clinical picture.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the activity of a protein depends on its folded structure, and many of these defects are missense mutations that change the fold. The enzyme may be produced in normal amount but misfold, be degraded or lose its catalytic site. The biochemical consequence is the same, but the genetic and therapeutic implications differ, and the mechanism is identified where possible.

Detection often begins with a screening test that measures the accumulated substrate or a characteristic metabolite. A screening result is a probability, not a diagnosis, and it is followed by confirmatory tests. Ioannidis (2005) described how a screening test with modest specificity produces many false positives when the condition is rare, and the confirmation is essential before any decision is made.

Confirmation uses enzyme assays in cells or tissue, and genetic sequencing that identifies the mutation. The concordance between the enzyme deficiency and the genotype is checked, since a variant may be benign and not the cause. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the explanations, and the demonstration that the variant reduces enzyme activity is such a test.

Early detection changes the outcome for some of these disorders, because a diet or a treatment that reduces the substrate can prevent harm before it appears. The value of screening depends on the availability of an effective intervention, and this must be shown for each condition rather than assumed.
