Many enzymes that act inside cells are found in the blood in small amounts. They are released when cells are damaged or when cells turn over, and their activity in serum is measured as a clinical indicator. The enzyme is detected by its catalytic activity: a substrate is supplied under defined conditions, and the rate at which it is converted to product is measured, usually by a change in absorbance. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the intracellular location of many of these enzymes, and their presence in serum reflects the tissues from which they leak.

The activity is measured under standard conditions, because it depends on temperature, pH, the concentration of substrate and the presence of cofactors. The temperature is held constant at a stated value, the substrate is supplied in excess so that the rate is zero-order in substrate, and the cofactors are included at a defined concentration. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the activity of a protein depends on its folding and environment, and each standard condition is chosen to keep the enzyme in its active form.

Results are reported in units of activity, which express the amount of product formed per minute under the standard conditions. Standardisation allows a result from one laboratory to be compared with another, and the reference intervals are defined for the method used. A change in method or in reagent lot changes the activity measured, and each change requires a new reference interval.

The measured activity does not show which tissue released the enzyme or whether the release is recent. Isoenzymes, which are variants of the same enzyme found in different tissues, can sometimes be distinguished by their different properties, and this helps to attribute the source. Ioannidis (2005) described how an apparently specific marker can reflect several sources, and the attribution should be treated as a probability.

Platt (1964) recommended comparing alternatives, and a clinical enzyme result is most informative when it is read together with other measures that distinguish the possible sources.
