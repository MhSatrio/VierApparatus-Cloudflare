# Medical Parasitology

Parasites are organisms that live on or in a host and draw their nourishment from it, and they include the protozoa, which are single-celled, and the helminths, which are multicellular worms. Parasitic disease affects a large part of the world's population, and its burden falls heavily on regions where diagnosis and treatment are least available. The Host-Pathogen Interactions and Microbiology topics described the principles of infection, and medical parasitology applies them to eukaryotic organisms with complex life cycles.

The chapters follow the life cycle of the parasite and the points at which it can be found, treated and interrupted. The life cycles of the major human parasites come first, with malaria as a central example. The methods of diagnosis by microscopy, antigen detection and molecular detection are then described, followed by vector biology, immune evasion, drug resistance and the burden of disease. The last chapter addresses laboratory quality and the reporting of results.

Sachs and Malaney (2002) estimated the economic and social costs of malaria, and the scale of the burden shows why the diagnosis and control of parasites are a priority. Ioannidis (2005) described how the statistics of disease burden can be uncertain, and Platt (1964) recommended comparing the alternatives that explain a diagnosis or a trend.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the eukaryotic cell biology that underlies the life cycles of parasites, which differs from that of bacteria in ways that affect treatment.
