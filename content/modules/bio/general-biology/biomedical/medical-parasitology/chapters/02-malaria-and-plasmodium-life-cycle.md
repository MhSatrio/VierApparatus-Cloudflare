Malaria is caused by Plasmodium parasites, which are transmitted by the bite of an infected female Anopheles mosquito. The sporozoites injected by the mosquito travel to the liver, where they invade hepatocytes and multiply without causing symptoms. Each liver cell releases thousands of merozoites into the blood, and these invade red cells. Inside the red cell the parasite multiplies in a cycle of about two days for the most lethal species, and the rupture of the infected cells releases new merozoites and toxic products that cause the fever and the periodic chills of the disease. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell biology of the red cell that the parasite invades, and the removal of the host cell's contents is part of the parasite's strategy.

The symptoms and the anaemia of malaria arise from the destruction of red cells and from the inflammatory response to the released products. The infected red cells also adhere to the walls of small vessels in some organs, which obstructs blood flow and contributes to the most severe forms of the disease. This adherence is mediated by parasite proteins expressed on the surface of the infected cell, and they are a target of the immune response.

A small proportion of red-cell stages develop into gametocytes, which are taken up by a mosquito when it feeds on the blood. In the mosquito the gametocytes mature and fuse, and the resulting parasites develop in the gut and then in the salivary glands, from which sporozoites are injected at the next bite. The transmission cycle therefore depends on the mosquito as well as on the human host.

Sachs and Malaney (2002) estimated the economic and social costs of the disease, and the scale of the burden makes the study of the life cycle a practical priority. The cost of the disease is a consequence of its biology, and the stages that cause symptoms and those that transmit it are targets for control.

Ioannidis (2005) described how an estimate of burden can be presented with a precision that the data do not support, and Platt (1964) recommended comparing the alternative explanations of an outbreak, such as a change in transmission against a change in detection.

Each stage of the life cycle is both a target for treatment and a point at which the parasite can be detected, and the choice of target depends on the question being asked.
