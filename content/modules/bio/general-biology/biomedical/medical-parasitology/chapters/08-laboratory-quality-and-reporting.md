Parasitology results depend heavily on the skill of the person who reads them. A microscopic diagnosis of malaria or of intestinal parasites requires the recognition of species that differ in small features, and errors in identification or in counting density are common among observers who do not practise regularly. Proficiency testing addresses this by sending samples of known content to the laboratory and comparing its results with the expected ones. Ioannidis (2005) described how an error that is not measured cannot be corrected, and the proficiency test is the means of measuring the error.

Antigen and molecular assays also require verification. A new lot of an antigen test or a new primer set must be checked against samples of known status, and the sensitivity and specificity must be measured in the population where the test will be used. Coons et al. (1941) established the labelling principle underlying immunoassays, and the performance of a labelled reagent can change between lots, so each lot is checked. Platt (1964) recommended a process that reveals its own failures, and the regular use of positive and negative controls is the means of detecting a drift in performance.

The report must state the species identified, the density of parasites where this is measured, the method used and the limits of the method. A report that gives a density without the method and the counting procedure cannot be compared with another. A negative report must state the sensitivity of the method and the volume or the number of fields examined, since a negative result from a small sample is not the same as a negative from a thorough examination.

The results also serve public health. The reports from a laboratory are aggregated to monitor the spread of disease, and the consistency of the reporting determines whether the aggregate is reliable. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biology of the parasites reported, and the reports should identify the species accurately, since the treatment and the control depend on the species.

The laboratory's report is the link between the parasite in the sample and the decision that a clinician or a health authority makes, and every element of the report must support that decision.
