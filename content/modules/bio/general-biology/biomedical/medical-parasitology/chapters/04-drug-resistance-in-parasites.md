Antiparasitic drugs exert selective pressure on parasite populations, and resistance develops in the same way as in bacteria and viruses. A mutation in the gene for the drug's target can reduce the binding of the drug, and a change in a transporter can reduce its accumulation within the parasite or increase its efflux. Some resistance involves the amplification of a gene, so that more of the target or the transporter is produced. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the gene amplification and the transporter activity that these mechanisms depend on, and each can be selected when the drug is used.

Resistance spreads when the drug is given to a population with a mixture of sensitive and resistant parasites. The sensitive parasites are killed while the resistant ones survive and multiply, so the proportion of resistant parasites rises with each treatment. The spread is faster when treatment is incomplete, because low doses or short courses leave the parasites exposed to the drug without killing them, which favours the resistant forms.

Sachs and Malaney (2002) described the economic consequences of malaria and the importance of its treatment, and the loss of an effective drug has large costs. The emergence of resistance to a widely used drug can reverse years of progress, and the monitoring of resistance is therefore a central part of control.

Resistance is detected by measuring the drug sensitivity of parasites in the laboratory and by sequencing the genes known to confer resistance. The sensitivity test is the more direct measure, but it requires culture of the parasite, which is not always possible. The genetic test can be applied to a sample directly, but it depends on the mutations being known, and a new mechanism would be missed. Ioannidis (2005) described how a marker that predicts resistance in one population may not do so in another, and the association should be confirmed by the phenotypic test.

Platt (1964) recommended comparing alternatives, and the test of whether a drug has failed is a comparison of the treatment outcome in people infected with sensitive and resistant parasites.

Resistance is a consequence of the drugs used, and the choice of treatment and its completeness shape the parasite population that the next patient will carry.
