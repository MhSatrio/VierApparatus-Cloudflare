Parasites that persist for years in a host must avoid the immune response that would otherwise clear them, and they have evolved several strategies to do so. Finlay and Falkow (1997) described the general features of pathogens that allow them to survive in the host, and parasites use many of these, often in more elaborate forms. Antigenic variation is one of the most striking. The parasite changes the molecules on its surface over time, so that antibodies that recognise one form are not effective against the next, and the immune response must start again for each new form.

Other strategies mask the surface. Some parasites coat themselves in host molecules, which makes them appear as self, and others shed the molecules that the immune system would recognise. Some parasites reside inside host cells, where they are shielded from antibodies, and they may modulate the death pathways of the cell to survive. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell death and the signalling that these parasites must control, and their survival depends on preventing the host cell from destroying them.

The modulation of the immune response is a further strategy. Some parasites induce regulatory cells or cytokines that suppress the response, and this allows them to persist at low densities without eliciting a full response. The suppression may also reduce the response to vaccines and to other infections, which is one reason parasitic infections can increase the risk of other diseases.

The consequences are that infections are chronic, that vaccines are difficult to design and that diagnosis may be complicated by variable surface molecules. A vaccine against a surface molecule may fail when the parasite switches to another form, and an antibody test may miss an infection whose antigens are changing. Ioannidis (2005) described how a mechanism that is proposed to explain persistence may be accepted before its specific predictions are tested, and Platt (1964) recommended tests that could separate the alternatives.

The parasite survives by changing what the host recognises, and this capacity sets the limit of what immunity and diagnosis can achieve.
