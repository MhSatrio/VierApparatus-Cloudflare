Vectors are the arthropods that carry parasites from one host to another, and the most important are mosquitoes, which transmit malaria, and the flies and bugs that carry trypanosomes and other agents. The vector is not a passive carrier. The parasite must survive in the vector's gut, cross its wall, reach its salivary glands and be injected at the next blood meal, and each of these steps imposes conditions that the parasite must meet. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular barriers and the transport across them, and the passage of the parasite through the vector's tissues follows similar principles.

The vector's feeding behaviour determines how often it bites and on whom. Some mosquitoes prefer to feed on humans, while others feed on animals, and the proportion that feed on humans shapes the risk of transmission. The timing of feeding, often at dusk or in the night, determines how well people are protected by bed nets. The longevity of the vector matters as well, because a parasite must complete its development in the vector before the vector dies, and the lifespan of the vector sets the number of cycles of transmission possible.

Vector control strategies target these features. Insecticide-treated nets exploit the nocturnal feeding of the vector, and indoor spraying kills vectors that rest on walls. Environmental measures remove the breeding sites in water and reduce the population. The effectiveness of each measure depends on the behaviour of the vector in the area, and the resistance of the vector to insecticides, which is an evolutionary response similar to that of the parasite to drugs, reduces their effect over time.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the effect of an intervention measured in a trial may not hold when the conditions differ, and the vector's behaviour is one of the conditions that differs between places. Platt (1964) recommended comparing the alternatives, and the evaluation of a vector control measure is a comparison of transmission with and without the measure.

The vector links the parasite to the human population, and the biology of the vector sets the terms on which the parasite can be controlled.
