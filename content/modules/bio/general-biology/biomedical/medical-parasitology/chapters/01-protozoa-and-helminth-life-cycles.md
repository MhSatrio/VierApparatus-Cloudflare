A parasite's life cycle is the sequence of forms through which it passes, and each form has its own biology, its own location in the host and its own role in disease or transmission. Protozoa are single cells that multiply within the host, often in a specialised form, and they may be transmitted directly between hosts or through a vector. Helminths are worms whose eggs or larvae leave the host and must develop in the environment or in an intermediate host before they can infect another person. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the eukaryotic cell biology of these organisms, and their complex life cycles reflect their adaptation to different environments.

The life cycle determines the sample in which the parasite is found. A protozoan that multiplies in the blood is found in a blood film, while one that lives in the intestine is found in stool. A helminth that lays eggs in the gut is detected by examining the stool for the eggs, while a larval stage that migrates through the lungs may be found in sputum. The clinician who requests a test must know the stage the organism is likely to be in, and the laboratory must know the stages it is looking for.

Finlay and Falkow (1997) described the features that pathogens use to establish themselves, and the parasitic life cycle often includes stages specialised for entering the host, for surviving within it and for leaving it. These features explain why the disease may be produced by one stage and transmitted by another.

Infections are often asymptomatic, and the detection of a parasite in a person without symptoms does not establish that it is causing disease. Ioannidis (2005) described how the presence of a finding can be taken as the cause of a condition without the evidence that links them, and the clinical picture must be considered with the result.

The life cycle is the map of the parasite's interaction with its hosts, and every diagnostic and control measure follows from its features.
