Microscopy has been the mainstay of parasite diagnosis for over a century. A thick blood film concentrates the parasites so that they can be seen among the red cells, and a thin film shows their morphology and allows the species to be identified. Stool is examined directly or after concentration for the eggs and cysts of helminths and protozoa. The method is inexpensive and reveals the stage and the density of the parasite, which matters for treatment. Its sensitivity depends on the skill of the observer and on the density of the organisms, and a few parasites in a large volume of sample can be missed.

Antigen detection uses an immunoassay, similar to the methods described for other pathogens, to detect a parasite protein in blood or urine. Coons et al. (1941) established the labelling principle that underlies such assays, and the rapid tests used in the field are simple versions of these methods. Antigen tests do not require microscopic expertise, and they can be performed where microscopy is unavailable, but they may remain positive after the parasite has been cleared, and their sensitivity is lower at low densities.

Molecular detection amplifies parasite nucleic acid by PCR, as Saiki et al. (1988) described for DNA. It is highly sensitive and can distinguish species that look alike under the microscope, and it can detect low-density infections that microscopy misses. The cost and the need for a laboratory with the equipment limit its use, and it may detect DNA from a cleared infection.

The choice of method depends on the question. A clinician who needs the density of parasites to guide treatment requires microscopy, while one who needs to know whether any parasite is present may use a molecular test. A negative microscopy result in a patient with a compatible illness does not exclude infection, because the parasites may be present at a density below the level of detection, and the result should be interpreted with the timing of the sample and the density expected.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the performance of a diagnostic test is often reported in ideal conditions, and the performance in the field may be lower. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could discriminate between alternatives, and combining methods often gives the answer that no single method can.

Each method reveals a different aspect of the infection, and the diagnosis is strongest when the methods agree and their differences are understood.
