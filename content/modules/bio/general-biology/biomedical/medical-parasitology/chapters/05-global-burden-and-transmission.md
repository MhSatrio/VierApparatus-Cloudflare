The burden of a parasitic disease is measured in several ways. Deaths and illness are counted, and the years of healthy life lost are estimated by combining the number of cases with the severity and the duration of the disability. Sachs and Malaney (2002) examined the economic costs of malaria, including the losses from reduced work, lower school attendance and reduced investment, and showed that these costs fall heavily on poor countries. The estimates depend on assumptions about the number of cases, which are often poorly measured in the regions most affected, and the estimates must be read with their uncertainty.

Transmission depends on the parasite, the vector and the people who live in the area. For vector-borne parasites, the abundance of the vector, its longevity and its preference for human hosts determine how many infectious bites occur. The vector's development depends on temperature, rainfall and the availability of breeding sites, and the environment therefore sets the limits of transmission. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biology of the cells and organisms whose interaction produces these patterns, and the transmission is an emergent property of many such interactions.

Control measures act on different parts of the chain. Bed nets and insecticides reduce the contact between vector and human, drugs clear the infection in people, and environmental measures reduce the breeding sites. The effect of each measure depends on its coverage and on the local conditions, and the combination of measures is usually more effective than any one alone.

The estimates of burden and of the effect of control are themselves uncertain. The number of cases is often estimated from models rather than counted, and the models depend on the data available and the assumptions made. Ioannidis (2005) described how the apparent precision of a model can mislead, and the uncertainty of each estimate should be stated with the value.

Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the alternative explanations of a change in burden, such as a true decline in transmission against a decline in testing, and the evaluation of control measures requires such comparisons.

The burden of a parasite is the sum of its transmission and its harm, and each is shaped by the environment and by the measures taken against it.
