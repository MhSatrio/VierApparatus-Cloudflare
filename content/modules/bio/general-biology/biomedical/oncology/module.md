# Oncology

Oncology is the clinical study of cancer, and it draws on every biological discipline in this curriculum. The Cancer Biology module described the processes by which cells become malignant, and the Human Pathology and Molecular Pathology modules showed how tumours are diagnosed. Oncology asks what can be done about a tumour, how to show that a treatment works and how to tell patients what they can expect. Each of these questions requires evidence, and the quality of the evidence is the central concern of the discipline.

The chapters move from the biology of cancer to its management. The hallmarks and the genetic landscape of tumours come first, as described by Hanahan and Weinberg (2000; 2011) and by Vogelstein et al. (2013). The principles of therapy follow, with the logic by which a treatment is chosen to exploit a property of the tumour. The design of clinical trials and the interpretation of tumour markers are then described, together with screening, early detection and the reporting of outcomes.

The evidence in oncology is often uncertain, and the history of the field includes many treatments that appeared effective in early studies and were not confirmed later. Ioannidis (2005) described how the bias of small or selectively reported studies can overstate the benefit of a treatment, and Platt (1964) argued that a trial should be designed to discriminate between the treatment and the alternatives. The chapters treat the design of evidence as part of the clinical question.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell cycle and the signalling pathways that cancer therapies aim to disrupt, and the mechanism of a therapy is the biological basis for its use.
