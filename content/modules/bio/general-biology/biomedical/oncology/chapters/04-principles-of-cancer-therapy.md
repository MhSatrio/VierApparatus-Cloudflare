Cancer therapy is built on differences between tumour and normal cells. Cytotoxic chemotherapy targets cells that divide rapidly, because the agents act on DNA synthesis or on mitosis. Since normal tissues such as the marrow and the gut also divide quickly, these drugs cause toxicity in those tissues, and the selectivity is only partial. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell cycle and the checkpoints that the drugs disrupt, and the therapeutic window is the range of doses in which the tumour is damaged more than the normal tissue.

Targeted therapy exploits a specific capability that the tumour has acquired. A drug that inhibits an activated kinase suppresses the growth signal that the tumour depends upon, and the normal cell, which does not depend on that signal in the same way, is spared to a greater degree. Hanahan and Weinberg (2000) described the capabilities that such therapies target, and the choice of drug follows from the capability that is present in the tumour.

Hormonal therapy works on tumours that depend on a hormone for growth, by blocking the hormone or its receptor. Immunotherapy releases the brakes that tumours place on the immune response, or it directs immune cells against the tumour. Radiotherapy damages the DNA of cells in the irradiated field, and it spares tissue outside the field, which gives it a spatial selectivity.

Resistance is the central problem. A tumour that is treated with a single agent selects for cells that survive, and a subclone that is resistant grows in the place of the sensitive population. Combinations of therapies with different mechanisms reduce the chance that a single resistant clone will survive, and this principle guides most modern treatment.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a therapy that works in a model may fail in patients, and Platt (1964) recommended testing a therapy against the alternatives that could explain its effect. The chapters on trials describe the means by which these tests are made.
