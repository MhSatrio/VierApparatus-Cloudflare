Screening aims to find cancer before it causes symptoms, in the hope that earlier treatment will reduce death. The idea is intuitive, but the evidence that a screening programme saves lives must be obtained directly, because two biases make a screened group appear to do better than an unscreened one even when no lives are saved. Ioannidis (2005) described how such biases can produce apparent benefits that disappear when the comparison is made correctly, and the screening literature contains many examples.

Lead time bias arises because screening detects a cancer earlier, so the time from diagnosis to death appears longer even when death occurs at the same age. The survival is lengthened on paper, not in life. Length bias arises because screening preferentially detects slowly growing cancers, which have a better outlook regardless of treatment, while fast-growing cancers appear between screens and are found clinically. The screened group therefore contains a higher proportion of indolent disease, which improves its apparent survival.

Overdiagnosis is a harm of screening. Some cancers that screening detects would never have caused symptoms in the person's lifetime, and treating them exposes the patient to the harms of therapy without benefit. Vogelstein et al. (2013) described the genetic diversity of cancers, which helps explain why some tumours are indolent and others aggressive, and this diversity is why screening detects a mixture of both.

False positives are a further harm. A screening test that is positive in a person without cancer leads to further tests, anxiety and sometimes invasive procedures, and the rate of false positives depends on the specificity of the test and the prevalence of disease. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could show the screening to be harmful as well as beneficial, and a complete evaluation weighs the reduction in death against the overdiagnosis and the false positives.

The decisive evidence is a randomised comparison in which the screened and unscreened groups are followed for deaths from the cancer. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the growth of tumours that determines when a cancer becomes detectable, and that biology sets the limits of what screening can achieve.
