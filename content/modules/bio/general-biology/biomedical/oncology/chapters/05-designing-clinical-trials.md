A clinical trial tests whether a treatment changes the outcome for patients, and its design determines whether the change can be attributed to the treatment. Platt (1964) described the strong inference that a trial should make: it compares the treatment with an alternative that the trial could show to be as good, so that the result discriminates between the two. The control group receives the standard care or a placebo, and the comparison is valid only when the two groups differ in nothing except the treatment.

Randomisation achieves this. Patients are allocated to the treatment or the control by chance, so that the known and unknown factors that affect outcome are distributed evenly between the groups. Without randomisation, the groups may differ in age, fitness or the stage of disease, and the difference may be mistaken for the effect of the drug. Ioannidis (2005) described how the bias of unrandomised comparisons can produce apparent benefits that disappear in randomised trials, and this explains many reversals in oncology.

The endpoint is the outcome measured. Overall survival is the most direct measure of benefit, while response rates, which measure shrinkage of the tumour, are faster to observe but do not always translate into longer life. The endpoint must be specified in advance, because a trial that reports whichever endpoint is favourable exaggerates the benefit.

Multiple comparisons inflate the chance of a false finding. A trial that tests several endpoints, several subgroups or several interim analyses increases the number of opportunities for an apparent effect, and a correction is needed to keep the overall error rate fixed. Cancer biology is complex, as Hanahan and Weinberg (2011) emphasised, and that complexity multiplies the subgroups that can be examined.

The trial is judged by its design as well as its result. A large, randomised trial with a prespecified endpoint and a correction for multiplicity gives strong evidence, while a small or post hoc analysis gives weak evidence whatever its outcome. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biology that the trial is meant to test, and a result that is consistent with the biology is more convincing than one that is not.
