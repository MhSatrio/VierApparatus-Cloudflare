Cancer genes fall into two broad classes. Oncogenes are normal genes whose activity drives growth when it is increased or made constitutive, through a point mutation that locks a protein in its active form, an amplification that produces too much of it or a fusion that joins it to a strong promoter. Tumour suppressors are genes whose products restrain growth, and their loss removes that restraint. Vogelstein et al. (2013) described the genes that most often change in cancers, and both classes appear among them. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling networks in which these genes act.

Knudson's two-hit model explains the loss of tumour suppressors. Each cell carries two copies of a suppressor gene, and a tumour usually requires the loss of both. An inherited mutation in one copy means that a single somatic event in the other is sufficient, which explains why inherited cancers often arise early and in several tissues. Watson and Crick (1953) described the double-stranded structure of DNA, and a diploid cell carries two copies of each gene, so the second hit is the loss of the remaining allele by mutation, deletion or epigenetic silencing.

The distinction matters for treatment. A tumour that depends on an activated oncogene may respond to a drug that inhibits its product, while the loss of a suppressor cannot be reversed by a drug that acts on a protein that is no longer present. Hanahan and Weinberg (2000) described the capabilities that these changes confer, and the therapy that targets the capability is chosen with the mechanism in mind.

The classification of a gene as an oncogene or a suppressor is made from the evidence of its role. A gene found mutated in a tumour is not automatically a driver, and the distinction between a driver and a passenger must be made by showing that the change contributes to growth. Ioannidis (2005) described how a gene that is frequently altered can be assumed to be causal without the test that would show it, and Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the alternatives.

The biology of these genes is the foundation of targeted therapy, and the evidence for each classification is therefore part of the clinical reasoning.
