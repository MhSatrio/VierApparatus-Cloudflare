The outcome of a cancer treatment is measured in several ways, and each answers a different question. Overall survival is the time from the start of treatment to death from any cause, and it is the most direct measure of benefit, because it cannot be biased by how the disease is assessed. Progression-free survival is the time until the disease grows or the patient dies, and it is observed sooner than survival, which makes it useful when trials must be completed quickly. It does not measure benefit directly, because a treatment may delay progression without extending life, and the two measures must be read together.

Quality of life is measured with validated questionnaires, and it captures the burden of treatment on daily life. A therapy that extends life at the cost of severe harm may be a poorer choice for some patients, and the trade-off must be stated. Platt (1964) recommended that a claim be tested against its alternatives, and the comparison of benefit with burden is the test that a therapy must pass before it is recommended.

Survivorship concerns the people who have finished treatment. Some effects of therapy appear years later, including second cancers, cardiac damage, infertility and cognitive change. Hanahan and Weinberg (2000) set out the capabilities that tumours accumulate over time, and the late effects of treatment are a reminder that therapy also changes normal tissues over time. The follow-up of survivors must be long enough to detect such effects.

Reporting must allow the benefit and harm to be judged fairly. The report states the number of patients, the randomisation, the endpoints as specified in advance, the losses to follow-up, the adverse events with their frequency and the analysis performed. Ioannidis (2005) described how selective reporting can make a treatment appear more beneficial than the data show, and the complete report is the safeguard against this.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biology of the tissues that treatment affects, and the report should state the known mechanisms of the harms observed.
