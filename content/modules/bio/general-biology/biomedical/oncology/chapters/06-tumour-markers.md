A tumour marker is a substance whose concentration in blood or tissue is associated with the presence or activity of cancer. Markers include proteins secreted by tumour cells, enzymes, hormones and fragments of DNA and cells. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the normal functions of many of these molecules, and a tumour may produce them in excess or in forms that are not normally present. The marker is used for three purposes: to aid diagnosis, to monitor the response to treatment and to detect recurrence after treatment has ended.

The usefulness of a marker depends on its sensitivity and specificity. Sensitivity is the proportion of patients with cancer whose marker is raised, and specificity is the proportion of people without cancer whose marker is normal. A marker with high sensitivity but low specificity will identify most cancers and also many people without cancer, and the false positives then require further investigation. For screening, where most people tested do not have cancer, even a marker of good specificity produces many false positives, and this limits the screening value.

Many markers rise in benign conditions. Inflammation, infection, pregnancy, liver disease and kidney disease can all raise the concentration of a marker without any tumour. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the proteins measured depend on their structure and their clearance, and a change in clearance raises the level without a change in production. The benign causes must be considered before a rise is attributed to cancer.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a marker that is assessed in patients who already have cancer may appear more accurate than it is in the population that would be tested, because the comparison group is not representative. The performance should be measured in the population in which the test will be used.

Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the alternatives, and a marker is most informative when its change distinguishes between recurrence and a benign cause of the same rise. A single value is less informative than a trend over time, which is why markers are most often used for monitoring.
