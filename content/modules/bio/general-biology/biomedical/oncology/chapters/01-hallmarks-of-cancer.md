Hanahan and Weinberg (2000) proposed that the diverse cancers share a set of capabilities that cells acquire during their transformation. These include sustaining proliferative signalling, evading growth suppressors, resisting cell death, enabling replicative immortality, inducing angiogenesis and activating invasion and metastasis. The list summarised decades of work on the cell cycle, apoptosis and the tumour microenvironment, and it gave the field a framework in which the diversity of tumours could be compared. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling pathways and the control of the cell cycle that the capabilities subvert.

In 2011 the same authors expanded the list. They added the reprogramming of energy metabolism, which allows tumour cells to use glycolysis even in the presence of oxygen, and the evasion of immune destruction. They also described genome instability and tumour-promoting inflammation as enabling characteristics that drive the acquisition of the hallmarks. The expansion reflected a larger body of evidence, and it showed that the framework was open to revision as knowledge changed.

Each hallmark suggests a therapeutic target. A tumour that depends on a growth signal may respond to a drug that blocks the signal, and a tumour that evades apoptosis may be sensitive to agents that restore the death pathway. The principle is that a therapy exploits a capability the tumour has acquired and the normal cell has not. This principle explains why some therapies are selective for cancer and why tumours resist them when the capability is replaced by another.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a framework can encourage the interpretation of every result within it, and the hallmarks should be treated as a guide to hypotheses rather than as proof that a particular capability is present in a particular tumour. Platt (1964) recommended testing alternatives, and each hallmark should be tested for its contribution rather than assumed.

The hallmarks are therefore a way of organising the biology of cancer, and their value lies in the questions they suggest.
