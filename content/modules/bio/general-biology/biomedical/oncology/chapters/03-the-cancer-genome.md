Sequencing of tumours has shown that cancers carry many genetic changes, often numbering in the thousands of mutations in a single tumour, most of which have no effect on growth. Vogelstein et al. (2013) described the landscape of these changes across many cancer types. A small number of genes are altered in a large fraction of tumours, and these are likely to be drivers that contribute to growth. Most of the other changes are passengers, which arise during the many divisions of the tumour cell and are carried along without consequence.

The landscape therefore has two parts. The drivers are shared across tumours and form the basis of the classification of cancer by its genetic causes. The passengers are largely private to each tumour and reflect its history of divisions and its exposure to mutagens. The diversity of the passengers makes each tumour unique at the genetic level, which complicates any classification based on a single change.

The classification of a mutation as a driver rests on evidence that it contributes to growth, and not simply on its frequency. A frequently altered gene may be a driver, but a gene that is altered frequently because it is large or fragile may be a passenger. Hanahan and Weinberg (2011) described the capabilities that drivers confer, and a mutation that does not contribute to any of them is less likely to be a driver.

The diversity challenges treatment. A therapy directed at one driver may be effective in tumours that carry it, but many tumours carry several drivers, and the drivers differ between tumours of the same type. The genetic profile of a tumour therefore determines which therapies are likely to act, and the profile must be measured in each patient.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a frequent finding can be read as more important than the evidence supports, and Platt (1964) recommended a test that could distinguish a driver from a passenger, which is the functional demonstration that the change promotes growth.

The genome of a tumour records its history, and the task of oncology is to separate the changes that matter from those that merely accompany them.
