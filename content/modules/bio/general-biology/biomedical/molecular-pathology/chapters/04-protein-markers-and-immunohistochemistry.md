Protein markers are detected in tissue by the immunohistochemistry described in the earlier topic, and they are used clinically to classify tumours and to guide therapy. A marker stain reveals whether the target is present and where, and the score translates the intensity and the proportion of positive cells into a category that a clinician can act upon. Coons et al. (1941) established the labelled antibody on which the stain depends, and the clinical use requires that the stain be reproducible from one laboratory to another.

Standardisation is the central requirement. The scoring scale must be defined, the cut-off that separates positive from negative must be fixed, and the scorers must agree. The cut-off is often derived from a study, and its performance in a new population must be checked, since a threshold that works in one setting may classify poorly in another. Ioannidis (2005) described how a cut-off chosen to fit one data set can be overfitted, and the cut-off should be validated prospectively.

The result depends on the handling of the tissue. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the structure of a protein determines the epitopes that an antibody recognises, and fixation alters that structure. A tissue fixed for too long may lose the epitope and give a false negative, while a tissue fixed too briefly may degrade. Antigen retrieval reverses some of this masking, but it must be standardised, since different retrieval conditions give different results.

Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the alternatives, and a marker is most useful when it distinguishes between treatments or diagnoses that would otherwise be chosen differently. A marker that changes no decision adds cost without benefit.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular proteins that markers measure, and their expression reflects the state of the cell at the time of sampling.
