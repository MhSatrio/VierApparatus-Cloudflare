A tumour is not a uniform population. It arises from a single cell, but over time its descendants acquire different mutations, and the tumour becomes a mosaic of subclones. Hanahan and Weinberg (2011) described the genetic instability that generates this diversity, and Vogelstein et al. (2013) described the genetic landscape of cancers, in which a few genes are altered across many tumours and many changes are specific to individual tumours. The consequence is that a sample from one region may not contain the mutations present in another, and a biopsy can give an incomplete picture.

Heterogeneity has a clinical consequence. A subclone that carries a mutation conferring resistance to a treatment may be absent from the biopsy and present elsewhere, and the treatment may then fail to act on the whole tumour. Sampling more than one region, or sampling the tumour over time, reduces this risk, but each additional biopsy carries its own burden for the patient.

Circulating tumour DNA is fragments of DNA shed from tumour cells into the blood. It offers a way to sample the tumour as a whole, because the fragments derive from many sites. The sensitivity is limited by the amount of tumour DNA in the blood, which is often a small fraction of the total cell-free DNA, and by the depth of sequencing that can be afforded. A negative result in blood therefore does not exclude the tumour, and the limit of detection must be stated.

Sources of error are several. Normal cells also shed DNA, and their fragments can be mistaken for tumour signal when the tumour fraction is low. Clonal haematopoiesis, the expansion of blood stem cell clones with age, produces mutations in the blood that are not from the tumour. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that depends on a low signal is vulnerable to such confounding, and the sources must be separated before a mutation is attributed to the tumour.

Platt (1964) recommended a test that could show the result to be wrong, and the comparison of blood and tissue results in the same patient is such a test, since their disagreement shows where the sampling or the signal has failed.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell death and turnover that release DNA into the circulation, and this process is the source of the signal.
