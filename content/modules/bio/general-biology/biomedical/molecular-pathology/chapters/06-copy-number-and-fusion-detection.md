Changes in the number of copies of a gene and rearrangements that join two genes are common in cancer, and both can be diagnostic and therapeutically relevant. Amplification increases the number of copies of a gene, and deletion decreases it. A fusion arises when a chromosomal break joins a segment of one gene to a segment of another, creating a hybrid gene whose product may drive growth. Vogelstein et al. (2013) described the range of such changes across cancers, and the detection methods are chosen according to which change is sought.

Fluorescence in situ hybridisation detects these changes in a tissue section. A labelled probe that binds the gene of interest is hybridised to the nuclei, and the signals are counted in each cell. Watson and Crick (1953) established the complementary pairing on which the probe binds its target, and the hybridisation depends on the same principle. The number of signals per nucleus gives the copy number, and a fusion is detected when two probes that flank a breakpoint are separated, or when a probe pair that is normally together is split.

Thresholds must be set. The number of signals varies between nuclei because of the sectioning and the overlap, and a cut-off is needed to call amplification. The cut-off is set from normal tissue and from validated samples, and its performance is reported with its sensitivity and specificity. Ioannidis (2005) described how a threshold set after the results are seen can produce an apparent difference, and the threshold should be fixed before the study.

Sequencing can also detect copy number and fusions, and the two methods may disagree. The disagreement must be examined rather than resolved by choosing the more favourable result. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the explanations, and comparing the methods in the same samples is such a test.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the chromosomal breaks and repair processes that produce amplifications and fusions.
