Expression profiling measures the transcripts of many genes at once, so that a tumour can be described by the pattern of its gene activity rather than by a single marker. The measurement may be made by quantitative PCR on a panel of genes, as Bustin (2000) described for the quantitative method, or by sequencing of the transcripts across the genome. The output is a profile, a vector of values across the genes, and the profile is compared with those of tumours of known type and outcome.

A gene signature is derived by finding genes whose expression differs between groups, and then using the pattern to classify new samples. The derivation is prone to overfitting, because with thousands of genes a set can be found that separates the training samples by chance. Ioannidis (2005) described this problem in general terms, and the remedy is validation in an independent cohort that was not used to derive the signature. A signature that fails in an independent cohort is not a diagnostic tool.

The profile depends on the tissue. Tumour samples contain normal and stromal cells, and the normal tissue contributes transcripts that dilute the tumour signal. A profile may reflect the proportion of stroma rather than the biology of the tumour, and the tumour content must be considered. The handling of the sample also changes the transcripts: the time between removal and preservation, and the method of fixation, alter the expression of many genes.

Platt (1964) recommended a test that could show the signature to be wrong, and a signature that does not separate the classes it claims to separate in a new cohort has failed that test. The validation must be designed in advance, with the cut-off for classification fixed before the new cohort is measured.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the regulation of gene expression that determines the profile, and the transcripts measured reflect the state of the cell as well as its identity.
