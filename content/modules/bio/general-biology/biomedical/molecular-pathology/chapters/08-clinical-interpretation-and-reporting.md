A molecular result is useful only when it is interpreted correctly, and the interpretation depends on what the variant does and on the context in which it was found. Vogelstein et al. (2013) showed that most of the changes in a cancer are passengers that do not drive it, and only a few are drivers whose presence changes the biology. A variant of known significance is one that has been shown to drive the disease or to predict response to a treatment. A variant of uncertain significance has not been characterised, and it must not be treated as if its effect were known.

The report must distinguish these categories. A variant of known significance is reported with the evidence for it, and a variant of uncertain significance is reported as such, without implying an effect that has not been shown. Ioannidis (2005) described how an uncertain finding can be reported as a clinical fact when it is presented without its limits, and the report should state what is and is not known.

The report also states the features that limit the result: the tumour content, the sample quality, the method and its sensitivity, and any failure of the controls. A negative result is qualified by the limit of detection, so that the reader can judge whether a mutation at the expected frequency would have been found. Platt (1964) recommended that a claim be stated in a form that could be shown false, and a report that states what the test could and could not detect allows such a judgement.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the consequence of an amino acid change depends on the structure it produces, and a change that is predicted to be damaging must be judged by its structural and functional effect rather than by its presence alone.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the processes that a variant disrupts, and an interpretation that explains the mechanism is more secure than one that rests on the association alone.
