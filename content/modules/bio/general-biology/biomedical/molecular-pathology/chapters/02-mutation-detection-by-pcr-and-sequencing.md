Targeted PCR detects a known mutation by amplifying the region around it and testing the product. Saiki et al. (1988) established the amplification on which the method depends, and the specific forms used in diagnosis include allele-specific and digital methods that are sensitive to a mutant allele among many normal ones. The method is fast, inexpensive and precise for the mutations it targets, but it can detect only the changes it was designed to find, and a new or unexpected mutation will be missed.

Sequencing reads the region without prior knowledge of the change. It detects a wide range of mutations in a single test, which makes it valuable when the mutations are not known in advance. Its sensitivity for a mutation present in a small fraction of cells is lower than that of targeted methods, because the mutant reads are diluted among the normal ones.

Tumour content limits the detection. A tumour sample contains normal cells, stromal cells and inflammatory cells alongside the neoplastic ones, and a mutation present in the tumour cells is diluted by the normal ones in the sample. A sample with twenty per cent tumour content carries a mutant allele at a fraction of the expected frequency, and a negative call may reflect the dilution rather than the absence of the mutation. The proportion of tumour cells must be estimated and reported with each result.

The interpretation of a mutation call must consider the tumour content and the sensitivity of the method. A call of absence is valid only when the method could have detected the mutation at the frequency expected in that sample. Ioannidis (2005) described how a negative result from an insensitive test is taken as proof of absence, and Platt (1964) recommended a test that could show the mutation if present.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the mutations that arise in cells and their effects on growth, which give the biological basis for the targets chosen.
