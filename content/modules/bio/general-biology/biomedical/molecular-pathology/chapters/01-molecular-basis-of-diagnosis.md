A molecular diagnosis depends on finding a change in a molecule that is associated with a disease. The molecule may be a DNA sequence, the number of copies of a gene, the expression of a transcript or the level of a protein. Watson and Crick (1953) established that the sequence of DNA carries the genetic information, and a change in the sequence can alter the protein that is made from it. The molecular test looks for that change, and the change becomes the marker that supports or excludes a diagnosis.

A change found in a patient is not automatically a diagnostic marker. Most people carry many sequence variants, and most of them have no effect on disease. A variant becomes a marker only when it is shown to be associated with the disease more often than chance would allow, and when the association is reproducible across independent groups. Ioannidis (2005) described how an association that is found in one study and not replicated is frequently a false positive, and the replication is the test that separates a real marker from a chance finding.

The choice of target follows the question. A diagnosis that depends on a known mutation requires a test for that mutation, while a classification that depends on the overall pattern of expression requires a broader measurement. The tissue available also constrains the choice, because a small biopsy may contain too little material for some tests, and the sample quality may limit others.

Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the explanations, and a molecular marker is most useful when it distinguishes between two diagnoses that look alike under the microscope. A marker that does not change the decision is of limited value, however specific it is.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular processes whose disruption produces the changes measured, and a marker is most convincing when its link to the process is understood.
