# Pharmacology

Pharmacology is the study of what drugs do to the body and what the body does to drugs. The Cell Communication, Enzymology and Neuroscience modules described the targets that drugs act upon, and pharmacology explains how a drug reaches its target at a useful concentration, how strongly it acts there and how it is removed. A drug is useful only when these processes are balanced so that the effect is achieved with an acceptable risk, and the discipline is built on the evidence that establishes this balance.

The chapters follow the path of a drug. Absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion determine the concentration that reaches the target over time. The pharmacodynamic relation between concentration and effect is then described through receptor theory, followed by the physical properties that make a molecule suitable as a drug. Adverse reactions and safety monitoring come next, and the final chapter addresses the clinical evidence on which a drug's use is judged.

Pharmacology has a long history of effects that appeared in early studies and were not confirmed. Ioannidis (2005) described how such results arise, and Platt (1964) recommended trials that discriminate between a drug and its alternatives. The chapters emphasise the design of the evidence alongside the mechanism.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell biology of the receptors and enzymes that drugs act on, and the mechanism of a drug is the link between its molecular action and its effect in the patient.
