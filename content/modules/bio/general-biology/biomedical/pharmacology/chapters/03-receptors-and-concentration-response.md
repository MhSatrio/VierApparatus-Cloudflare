A drug acts by binding to a receptor, usually a protein whose shape changes when the drug attaches. Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein's function depends on its structure, and the binding site of a receptor is defined by the arrangement of residues that fit the drug. The affinity of the drug for the receptor is the strength of this binding, and it is measured as the concentration at which half the receptors are occupied. A drug with high affinity binds at low concentrations, and the affinity is a property of the drug and the receptor together.

The effect of the drug depends on the receptor occupancy, but not always in direct proportion. The response of a tissue rises with concentration along a sigmoidal curve, which is steep in the middle and levels off at high concentration. The curve is often plotted on a logarithmic scale, which makes its shape symmetrical and allows the potency of different drugs to be compared. The concentration that produces half the maximal response is a measure of potency, and it depends on both affinity and efficacy.

Efficacy is the maximal effect a drug can produce when it occupies its receptors. A full agonist produces the largest possible response, while a partial agonist produces a smaller maximum even when all receptors are occupied. An antagonist occupies the receptor without producing a response and blocks the effect of an agonist. The distinction between efficacy and affinity is central to pharmacology, since a drug with high affinity may have low efficacy.

Response can saturate before all receptors are occupied, because the cell has spare receptors that are not needed to produce the full effect. The amount of spare receptor varies between tissues and drugs, and the relation between occupancy and response is therefore not fixed. The experimenter must determine it for each system.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a concentration-response curve fitted to few points can give an estimate of potency that appears precise, and the number of concentrations and the fitting model should be reported. Platt (1964) recommended testing alternatives, and the distinction between an agonist and a partial agonist is made by comparing their maximal responses in the same tissue.

The relation between concentration and effect is the basis of the dose, and it links the molecular interaction of a drug with its clinical action.
