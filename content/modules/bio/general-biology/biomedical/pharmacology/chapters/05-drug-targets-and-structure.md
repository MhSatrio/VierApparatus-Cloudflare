Most drugs act on one of four classes of target: receptors, enzymes, ion channels and transporters. Receptors bind signalling molecules and transmit a signal to the cell, as described in the earlier chapters. Enzymes are inhibited by drugs that bind their active site or an allosteric site, and the inhibition alters the concentration of the product. Ion channels are opened or blocked by drugs that act on their pore or gating, and transporters that move substances across membranes are inhibited or driven by drugs. Alberts et al. (2022) describe these classes, and the drug's effect depends on which process the target controls.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein's function depends on its folded structure, and the drug must fit the shape of the binding site it engages. Structural biology, in which the three-dimensional structure of a target is determined by crystallography or by electron microscopy, reveals the shape of the site and the residues that line it. A drug designed to fit the site can be optimised for affinity and selectivity by changing its groups so that they form the right contacts.

Structure-based design depends on a model of the target that may differ from the target in the cell. The structure is usually determined for a protein in isolation, and the conformation in the membrane or in a complex may differ. A compound that fits the model well may bind poorly to the native target, and the measured binding must confirm the prediction.

Lipinski et al. (1997) showed that the physical properties of a compound affect its likelihood of becoming an orally active drug, and a compound that binds its target well may still fail because its properties prevent it from reaching the target. The design of a drug therefore balances the fit to the target against the properties that allow it to reach the target.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a candidate drug identified by computational screening may fail in testing, and the predictions of structure-based design should be tested experimentally. Platt (1964) recommended comparing alternatives, and the test of a designed drug is whether it acts on the intended target and not on others.

The target and the drug are complementary, and the structure of the one determines the design of the other.
