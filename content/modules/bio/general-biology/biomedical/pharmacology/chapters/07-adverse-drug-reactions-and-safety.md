Adverse drug reactions fall into several classes. The most common are extensions of the drug's intended action, such as bleeding with anticoagulants or sedation with sedatives, and these are predictable from the pharmacology. Others depend on the patient, for example reactions that follow genetic differences in drug metabolism or in a target, and these are less predictable but may be detected by testing. Immune reactions arise when the drug or a metabolite acts as an antigen or as a hapten, and they may occur in a few people in a way that is not foreseen. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the mechanisms of immune recognition that underlie the last class, and the reaction depends on the structure of the drug and its products.

Metabolites can be toxic when they form reactive species that bind proteins or DNA, and the toxicity depends on the balance between metabolic activation and detoxification. A change in this balance, through a genetic variant or through another drug, can increase the production of the toxic metabolite. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the function of a protein depends on its structure, and a reactive metabolite that modifies a protein can alter its fold and its activity.

Safety signals are detected after a drug is licensed, when it is used in larger and more varied populations than the trials. Spontaneous reports from clinicians and patients are the most common source, and they identify reactions that were not seen in trials. The reports are a source of hypotheses, not evidence of cause, since they lack a denominator and a comparison group. The number of reports for a drug depends on how much it is used and how much the reaction is noticed, and a rise in reports may reflect either a true increase in harm or a change in reporting.

Ioannidis (2005) described how an association that is detected by searching many outcomes can produce false signals, and the statistical methods used to detect signals must correct for this. Platt (1964) recommended testing alternative explanations, such as a drug effect against the natural course of the disease, and the comparison with a control population is the means of testing a signal.

Safety monitoring is an ongoing process of comparison, and the evidence for a harm grows as it is tested against alternatives.
