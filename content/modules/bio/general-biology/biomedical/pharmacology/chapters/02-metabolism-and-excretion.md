Most drugs are metabolised in the liver by enzymes that modify their structure. Phase one reactions, mainly oxidations carried out by the cytochrome P450 family, introduce or expose a polar group on the molecule. Phase two reactions, such as glucuronidation and sulphation, attach a larger polar group, which makes the molecule more water soluble and readily excreted. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the enzymes of the endoplasmic reticulum that carry out these reactions, and the cytochrome P450 enzymes are a family of proteins whose structure allows them to act on a wide range of molecules.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the activity of an enzyme depends on its folding, and the activity of the P450 enzymes varies with genetic variants that alter their structure. People differ in the speed at which they metabolise particular drugs, and some are slow metabolisers who accumulate the drug, while others are fast and may not reach an effective concentration. The variation has a genetic basis that can be measured, and it explains why a standard dose produces different effects in different people.

Metabolism usually inactivates a drug, but not always. Some drugs are administered as inactive precursors that must be metabolised to become active, and some metabolites are active or toxic in their own right. The effect of a drug in a patient therefore reflects both the parent compound and its products, and a measurement of the parent alone may not describe the activity.

The kidney excretes the water-soluble products and the drugs that were not metabolised. Excretion depends on the filtration of the unbound drug and on the active secretion and reabsorption along the tubule. Impaired kidney function reduces excretion, and the dose must be reduced to avoid accumulation and toxicity.

Ioannidis (2005) described how an interaction between two drugs may be claimed from a single case, and the mechanism through the enzyme must be shown by a controlled study. Platt (1964) recommended a comparison that could separate the alternative explanations of a change in effect, such as a change in metabolism against a change in receptor response.

Metabolism and excretion determine how long a drug remains and how much of it or its products are present, and they set the frequency with which a drug must be given.
