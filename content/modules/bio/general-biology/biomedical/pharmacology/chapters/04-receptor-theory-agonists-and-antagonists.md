Receptor theory describes how drugs produce their effects by occupying receptors. An agonist binds the receptor and activates it, producing a response. A partial agonist binds and activates the receptor but produces a smaller maximal response than a full agonist, even at full occupancy. An antagonist binds the receptor without activating it, so that it blocks the access of agonists. Some receptors show constitutive activity in the absence of any agonist, and an inverse agonist reduces this basal activity. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the receptor signalling that these drugs modulate, and the conformational change that accompanies activation is what distinguishes these classes.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the function of a protein depends on its structure, and the receptor exists in several conformations, some active and some inactive. The agonist stabilises the active conformation, the antagonist stabilises the inactive one and the partial agonist favours an intermediate state. The model explains the range of efficacy, and it shows why the same receptor can respond differently to different ligands.

Antagonism can be competitive or non-competitive. A competitive antagonist binds at the same site as the agonist, and its effect is overcome by raising the agonist concentration, so the concentration-response curve of the agonist is shifted to the right without a change in its maximum. A non-competitive antagonist binds elsewhere or binds irreversibly, and it reduces the maximum response, which is not restored by higher agonist concentration. The pattern of the shift therefore reveals the mode of action of the antagonist.

Ioannidis (2005) described how an interpretation of antagonism can rest on a single measurement, and the shift should be measured across several antagonist concentrations before the mode is claimed. Platt (1964) recommended testing the alternative explanations, and the pattern of the shift is a test that discriminates between competitive and non-competitive mechanisms.

Agonists, antagonists and their intermediate forms describe the range of effects a receptor can be made to produce, and the choice of drug depends on which effect is wanted.
