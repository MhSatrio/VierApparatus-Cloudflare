Lipinski et al. (1997) analysed the properties of orally administered drugs and identified a set of physicochemical limits within which most of them lie. The rule of five, as it became known, notes that absorption is more likely when the molecular weight is below about five hundred, the lipophilicity is below about five, the molecule has no more than five hydrogen bond donors and no more than ten hydrogen bond acceptors. The properties are related to the passage of the molecule across membranes, which depends on its size and on the balance between its solubility in water and in lipid.

The rule is a guide and not a law. Many drugs lie outside its limits and are effective, and the classes include antibiotics, some vitamins and drugs that are transported by carriers rather than crossing membranes by diffusion. The rule therefore identifies the region in which passive absorption is probable, rather than defining the drugs that can be used. Ioannidis (2005) described how a rule derived from a set of successful cases can be applied as if it were a test of success, and the rule must be used with the knowledge of its origin.

The properties are predictions that are tested by measurement. Solubility and permeability are measured in the laboratory, and their values inform the decision to take a compound forward. A compound that meets the rule but has poor permeability in a laboratory model is unlikely to be absorbed, and a compound that fails the rule may be absorbed by a transporter.

The rule also matters for the design of compounds. Medicinal chemists adjust the structure of a candidate to bring its properties within the range, while keeping the affinity for the target, and this balance is the central task of drug design. Platt (1964) recommended comparing alternatives, and the comparison of candidate compounds by their properties and their activity is the means by which the best are selected.

The rule summarises a relation between the structure of a molecule and its behaviour in the body, and it is most useful as a starting point for judgement.
