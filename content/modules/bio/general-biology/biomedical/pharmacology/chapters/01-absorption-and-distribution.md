A drug must cross the membranes between its site of administration and the blood. Most drugs cross by passive diffusion, which favours molecules that are small and that dissolve in lipid as well as in water. Ionisation matters because the charged form of a drug crosses membranes poorly, and the proportion that is charged depends on the pH of the environment and on the drug's acid or base strength. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the lipid bilayer and the transport across it, and the balance of solubility in water and lipid determines how readily a molecule passes through it.

Lipinski et al. (1997) examined the physical properties that predict whether a compound is absorbed by the body, and their analysis showed that molecular size, lipophilicity and the number of hydrogen bond donors and acceptors are related to the likelihood of good absorption. The properties are a guide rather than a rule, and many useful drugs lie outside the ranges they describe.

Once in the blood, the drug distributes into the tissues. Many drugs bind to plasma proteins, particularly albumin, and only the unbound fraction can leave the circulation and act on its target. The degree of binding therefore affects both the concentration available and the clearance, and a change in binding through disease or through a second drug can alter the effect without any change in the dose.

The volume of distribution is a parameter that summarises the spread of a drug. It is the volume that would contain the drug at the concentration measured in the plasma, and a large value indicates that the drug has spread into tissues or is bound to them. The value is not a physical volume, and it must be interpreted as a measure of distribution rather than of anatomy.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a parameter that is derived from a model can be treated as a measured property, and the assumptions of the model should be stated when the parameter is reported.

Absorption and distribution determine how much drug reaches the target, and they are the first of the processes that shape the response to a dose.
