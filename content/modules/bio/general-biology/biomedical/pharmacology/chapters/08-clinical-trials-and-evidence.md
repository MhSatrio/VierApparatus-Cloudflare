The development of a drug proceeds through phases of increasing scale. Early studies in healthy volunteers establish the safety, tolerability and pharmacokinetics of the compound. Studies in patients then explore the dose and the early signs of effect. The decisive studies are large randomised trials that compare the drug with a control, which may be a placebo or an active treatment, and that measure outcomes that matter to patients. Platt (1964) described the strong inference that such a comparison makes possible, since the trial can show the drug to be no better than the control as well as better.

Randomisation is the central safeguard. Patients are allocated to the drug or the control by chance, so that the known and unknown factors that affect outcome are distributed evenly between the groups. Without randomisation, a difference between groups may reflect who was treated rather than what was given. The control must be chosen so that the comparison answers the clinical question, and a placebo tests whether the drug is better than nothing, while an active control tests whether it is better than the current treatment.

The endpoint must be specified in advance. A trial that measures many outcomes and reports the favourable ones exaggerates the benefit, and the prespecified endpoint and the analysis plan protect against this. Ioannidis (2005) described how selective reporting and flexible analysis can make a drug appear more effective than the evidence supports, and the registration of trials and the publication of all results are the remedies for these problems.

The reporting of harms is as important as the reporting of benefit. Adverse events must be recorded in all groups, and their frequency compared, so that a harm that is rare but serious can be detected. A trial that is too small to detect a rare harm cannot show that the harm is absent, and the reader must judge the power of the trial to detect what it does not report.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biology of the target and the pathways that the drug affects, and a clinical result that is consistent with the mechanism is more convincing than one that is not, although the trial remains the decisive test.

The judgement of a drug rests on a trial whose design allows its result to discriminate between benefit and its absence, with harms reported as fully as benefits.
