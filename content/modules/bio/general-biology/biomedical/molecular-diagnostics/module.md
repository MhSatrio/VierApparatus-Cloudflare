# Molecular Diagnostics

Molecular diagnostics applies the methods of molecular biology to the detection of disease in patients. The earlier modules described the methods themselves, including PCR, sequencing and immunoassay, and the Molecular Pathology topic described their use in tissue. This topic concerns the test as a product that must be shown to work: its analytical performance, its clinical value, the systems that keep its results reliable and the conditions under which it reaches the patients who could benefit from it.

The chapters follow the life of a test. Nucleic acid and protein methods come first, with the choice of method for a question. The analytical validation that establishes what the test measures and how precisely comes next, followed by the clinical validation that shows whether the result predicts disease or changes care. The quality systems that keep the laboratory reliable are then described, together with the statistical interpretation of positive and negative results and the economic and regulatory considerations that determine adoption.

Saiki et al. (1988) established the amplification on which many of these tests depend, and the power of the method has made it possible to detect very small amounts of a target. This power is also the source of the main risk, which is that a highly sensitive test finds things that do not matter. Ioannidis (2005) described how such tests can produce apparent findings whose clinical value has not been shown, and Platt (1964) recommended validation designed to show a test to be wrong.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular targets of the tests, and the biology of the target determines what a positive result means.
