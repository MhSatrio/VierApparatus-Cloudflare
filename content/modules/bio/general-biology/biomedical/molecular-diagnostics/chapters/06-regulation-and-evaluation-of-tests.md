A diagnostic test must meet a standard before it is used in patient care, and the standard has three levels. Analytical validity asks whether the test measures what it claims to measure with acceptable accuracy and precision. Clinical validity asks whether the result is associated with the condition in the population of interest. Clinical utility asks whether the use of the result improves the outcome for patients or the efficiency of care. Each level requires different evidence, and a test that meets the first two may still fail the third.

Regulators assess the first level and often the second, and they require documentation of the validation described in earlier chapters. Health technology assessors go further, and they ask whether the test changes management in a way that benefits patients at an acceptable cost. Evans and Relling (1999) argued that a genetic test is valuable only when its result leads to a different and better decision, and this is the principle of clinical utility.

Clinical utility is the hardest to establish. A test may be accurate and still not change the decision, because the clinician would have made the same choice without it, or because no effective treatment follows a positive result. The evidence for utility comes from studies that compare a strategy with the test against a strategy without it, and these studies are demanding because they measure the outcome of care rather than the accuracy of the test.

The cost of a test must be weighed against its benefit and against the alternatives. A test that detects a condition early at a low cost may be justified even if its benefit is modest, while a costly test with a small benefit may not be. The comparison requires an estimate of the cost of the test, the cost of the disease it detects and the change in outcome, and each estimate carries uncertainty.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a test can be adopted on the strength of its analytical performance without evidence of benefit, and Platt (1964) recommended comparing the alternatives explicitly. The evaluation of a test is incomplete until its benefit has been compared with the alternatives.

A test that is accurate is not necessarily useful, and the regulation and assessment of tests exist to establish the difference.
