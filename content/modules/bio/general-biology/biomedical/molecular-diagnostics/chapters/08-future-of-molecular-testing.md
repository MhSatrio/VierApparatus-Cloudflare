Molecular diagnostics is changing as the cost of sequencing falls and as new sources of material become accessible. Whole genome and whole exome sequencing can now be performed for individual patients, and they reveal far more variation than a targeted test. Vogelstein et al. (2013) showed how much of the genetic landscape of a cancer can be revealed by sequencing, and the same breadth now applies to inherited disease. The information gained is valuable, but much of it has no known meaning, and the decision of what to report and act upon becomes more difficult as the scope of the test grows.

Liquid biopsy, the detection of tumour DNA in blood, offers a way to monitor disease without repeated tissue sampling. Its sensitivity is limited by the amount of tumour DNA present, and the evidence for its use must come from studies that show it changes management and improves outcome, not only that it detects the tumour. Point-of-care tests bring nucleic acid detection to the bedside or to remote settings, and their performance must be validated in the conditions in which they are used, where the sample handling and the operator skill may differ from those of the laboratory.

Large data sets and computational analysis are central to the future of the field. Machine methods can find patterns in genomic and clinical data that no individual could identify, but the patterns must be validated in independent populations, as the earlier chapters on association studies emphasised. Ioannidis (2005) described how the search across many variables increases the chance of spurious findings, and the prespecification and external validation of any model are essential.

Each new direction requires its own safeguards. The analytical and clinical validation must be repeated for each new application, the benefit must be shown in a trial or a well-designed study, the privacy of the data must be protected, and the costs must be justified. Platt (1964) recommended tests designed to show a new method to be wrong, and the adoption of a technology should follow a demonstration that it can fail as well as succeed.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biology that the new tests reveal, and the interpretation of their results will depend on understanding that biology as well as the technology.
