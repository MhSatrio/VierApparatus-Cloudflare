Analytical validation establishes what the test measures and how well. The limit of detection is the lowest amount of target that the assay detects with a stated probability, usually ninety-five per cent, and it is found by testing dilutions of a known sample in replicate. Saiki et al. (1988) showed that amplification can detect very small amounts of DNA, but the detection limit must be measured for each assay, since it depends on the primers, the reaction conditions and the matrix. A limit of detection quoted from the literature for a different assay does not apply.

Linearity and the range of quantification describe the amounts over which a quantitative assay gives a proportional response. Bustin (2000) emphasised that quantitative results are reliable only within this range, and samples above or below it must be diluted or reported as outside the range. Precision is the scatter of replicate measurements, measured within a run and between runs, and it sets the smallest difference between two samples that can be distinguished.

Specificity is tested against sequences that are related to the target, such as other strains, other species or the human genome, which can be amplified by mistake. A cross-reaction with a related sequence produces a false positive for the target, and the assay must be shown to avoid it at the concentrations found in clinical samples. The matrix of the sample also matters, since blood, urine, sputum and tissue contain inhibitors and background nucleic acid that change the response.

Inhibition is a particular risk. A reaction may fail to amplify the target because a component of the sample inhibits the polymerase, and this produces a false negative. An internal control, a second target added to each sample, reveals inhibition, and a negative result without a valid internal control must be reported as invalid.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a test whose performance is measured under ideal conditions may perform less well in routine samples, and the validation should include the sample types in which the test will be used. Platt (1964) recommended a validation designed to show the assay to be wrong, and the search for cross-reactions and inhibitions is such a test.

The analytical validation defines the limits of what the test can tell, and every clinical claim must stay within those limits.
