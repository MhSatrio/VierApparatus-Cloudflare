A positive molecular result shows that the target nucleic acid is present, and this is not always the same as disease. Oliver (2010) described organisms that remain present for long periods in a dormant state, and their nucleic acid may be detected long after they have ceased to cause harm. Colonising organisms may be present on the skin or in the airway without causing infection, and a sensitive test will detect them. Latent infections, in which the pathogen persists without active disease, produce positive results in people who are well. The interpretation must consider whether the result reflects the condition the test is meant to detect.

Contamination is another source of false positives. A highly sensitive amplification can detect a few copies of a target introduced into the laboratory, and the controls described in the earlier validation chapters are the means of detecting this. A positive result without a plausible clinical context, or one that appears in several unrelated samples processed together, should be suspected of contamination and repeated under strict conditions.

A negative result is informative only in relation to the sensitivity of the test. A test with a limit of detection above the amount present in the sample will be negative even when the target is there, and a negative result taken too early in an infection, before the target has reached detectable levels, may be false. The timing of the sample relative to the onset of disease must therefore be considered, and a repeat sample may be needed.

Discordance between results requires resolution. When two tests disagree, or when a result conflicts with the clinical picture, a repeat test on a new sample or a second method using a different target can resolve the question. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the alternatives, and a second independent method is such a test, since it is unlikely to fail in the same way as the first.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the confidence placed in a single result can exceed what the result supports, and the report should state what the result does and does not show.

A molecular result is a signal whose meaning depends on the clinical context, the sensitivity of the test and the timing of the sample, and each must be considered before action is taken.
