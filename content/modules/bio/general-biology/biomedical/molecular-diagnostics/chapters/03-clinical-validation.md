Analytical validation shows what a test measures. Clinical validation shows whether the result is associated with the condition it is meant to detect, in the population where it will be used. The test is compared with a reference standard, which is the best available determination of the condition, and the results are arranged in a two by two table of true and false positives and negatives. Sensitivity is the proportion of people with the condition who test positive, and specificity is the proportion without the condition who test negative. Both are properties of the test against the reference, and they do not change with the population in the same way as predictive values.

The positive predictive value is the probability that a positive result is a true case, and the negative predictive value is the probability that a negative result is a true absence. These depend on the prevalence of the condition in the population tested. In a population where the condition is rare, even a test with high sensitivity and specificity produces many false positives, because the number of true cases is small relative to the number of people without the condition. Ioannidis (2005) described this relation and showed that a positive finding is less likely to be true when the prior probability is low.

The test must therefore be validated in the population where it will be used. A test validated in a referral centre, where the prevalence is high, may perform poorly in primary care, where the prevalence is low. The spectrum of disease also matters, because a test that detects severe disease may miss mild cases, and the validation population must include the range of cases the test will encounter.

The reference standard itself may be imperfect. If it misses some cases, the sensitivity of the test is underestimated, and if it wrongly classifies people, the specificity is affected. Where no perfect standard exists, the limitation must be stated and the comparison interpreted accordingly.

Platt (1964) recommended a test that could discriminate between alternatives, and the clinical validation discriminates between a true association and an artefact of the population or the reference. A test that cannot be shown to improve the decision is not validated for use, however accurate it appears.

The clinical value of a test is the meaning of its result in the population that receives it, and this depends on prevalence as much as on the test itself.
