A molecular test begins with a question, and the method is chosen to answer it. A test for the presence of a known pathogen needs high sensitivity and speed, which amplification provides, as Saiki et al. (1988) established. A test for an unknown or highly variable pathogen needs breadth, which sequencing provides by reading the genome without prior knowledge of the target. A test that measures the amount of a target needs a quantitative method, and Bustin (2000) described the calibration that such measurement requires. The choice is therefore a trade-off among sensitivity, breadth, speed and the quantity it can report.

The sample limits the choice. A formalin-fixed tissue section yields fragmented nucleic acid, which is unsuitable for some amplicon lengths, while a blood sample may contain the target at very low fraction. The amount and quality of the material determine whether a method can be applied at all, and a test designed for fresh tissue may fail on archived specimens. The sample must be checked against the method before a result is reported.

The target level matters. A method that detects a few copies will find contamination and colonisation, and a method that requires many copies will miss an early or a low-level infection. The expected concentration in the target population sets the level of sensitivity needed, and a test that is more sensitive than necessary increases the rate of results that do not change care.

Cost and speed determine whether a test is used in practice. A method that gives a result in hours may change treatment on the same day, while one that takes days may be useful only for confirmation. The laboratory must weigh these factors against the benefit the test provides.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the choice of method can change the apparent prevalence of a condition, and the comparison of methods on the same samples reveals whether a difference reflects the disease or the test. Platt (1964) recommended that a test be chosen so that it can discriminate between the alternatives it is meant to separate.

The method is the instrument of the question, and its suitability depends on the sample, the target and the decision that will follow.
