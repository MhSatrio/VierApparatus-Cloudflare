A molecular laboratory produces results from a process with many steps, and each step can introduce an error. Reagents vary from lot to lot, instruments drift, technicians differ in their technique and contamination can enter at any stage. The quality system is the set of documented procedures and checks that detects these errors and prevents them from reaching a patient. Ioannidis (2005) described how an uncontrolled process can produce results that look consistent but are systematically wrong, and the quality system is the protection against this.

Internal controls run with each batch, including positive and negative samples and the internal control of each sample. When a control fails, the batch is investigated and the results are withheld until the cause is found and corrected. Lot-to-lot checks compare a new reagent with the previous one on a standard, so that a change in performance is detected before it affects patients. Instrument calibration and maintenance are recorded, and the trend of control values is reviewed to detect slow drift.

Contamination is monitored by the regular testing of the laboratory environment and of the reagents and by strict separation of the stages of the process, from sample preparation to amplification. Saiki et al. (1988) showed that amplification can detect minute amounts of target, and a contaminated reagent can therefore produce false positives on a large scale. The monitoring of negative controls and of surfaces is the means of detecting such contamination early.

External proficiency schemes send the same samples to many laboratories and compare their results with a reference. The scheme reveals errors that the internal controls cannot, because the internal controls share the laboratory's own biases. A laboratory that performs well in its own controls but differs from the reference in an external scheme has a problem that must be investigated.

Corrective action follows any detected error. The cause is identified, the affected results are reviewed, and the process is changed to prevent recurrence. Platt (1964) recommended a process designed to reveal its own failures, and the record of errors and their corrections is the evidence that the system works.

The quality system is what makes a molecular result trustworthy over time, and its absence can be as dangerous as an inaccurate test.
