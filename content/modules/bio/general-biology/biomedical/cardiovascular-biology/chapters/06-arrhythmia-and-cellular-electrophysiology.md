The cardiac action potential is the result of a sequence of ionic currents that flow across the membrane of the myocyte. A rapid influx of sodium depolarises the cell. A brief outward current of potassium begins the repolarisation, and the balance between a sustained calcium influx and the outward potassium currents forms the plateau. Late repolarisation is carried by further potassium currents, which return the membrane to its resting potential. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the channels that carry these ions, and each channel is a protein whose gating depends on the voltage across the membrane.

The duration of the action potential determines how long the cell is refractory, and this is important for the timing of the next beat. Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein's function depends on its structure, and the gating of a channel depends on the fold that allows it to open and close. A mutation that alters the gating of a potassium or sodium channel changes the duration of the action potential, and the change can be inherited.

Arrhythmia arises from disturbances of impulse formation and of conduction. An abnormal focus that fires spontaneously can drive a rapid rhythm, and a region in which conduction is slow or blocked can allow an impulse to circle and sustain a re-entrant rhythm. The prolongation of the action potential can cause early afterdepolarisations, which trigger further impulses, and this mechanism underlies some of the most dangerous rhythms. The cellular basis of these events is studied in isolated cells and in tissue, where the currents can be measured directly.

The link between a cellular change and an arrhythmia in a patient is inferred rather than shown directly. Ioannidis (2005) described how a mechanism that explains a finding in cells may not be the mechanism in the patient, and the clinical relevance of a cellular change must be established. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the alternatives, such as a comparison of the arrhythmia in patients with and without the proposed change.

The rhythm of the heart is therefore an emergent property of many channels, and the cellular electrophysiology explains how a single change can alter the whole rhythm.
