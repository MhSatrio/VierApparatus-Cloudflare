Cardiovascular risk is estimated from a set of factors, including age, sex, blood pressure, smoking, diabetes and the concentrations of lipoproteins. Risk scores combine these into a probability of an event over a stated period. A score is a prediction, and its usefulness is judged by how well it discriminates between people who will and will not have an event, and by how well its predicted and observed rates agree. A score can predict well without the factors in it being causes, because a factor may be a marker of another process that causes the disease.

The distinction between prediction and causation is central. Ross (1999) described the inflammatory process of atherosclerosis, and a factor that acts on this process is a plausible cause, while a factor that is associated with the disease through a shared upstream cause is not. Ioannidis (2005) described how observational associations are vulnerable to confounding, to selection and to the choice of analysis, and many factors that appeared causal in early studies were not confirmed when tested.

Two approaches test causation more directly. A randomised trial allocates the factor, or the treatment that changes it, and compares the outcome with a control group, which removes confounding by design. Platt (1964) described the strong inference that such a comparison makes possible, since the trial can show the treatment to be ineffective as well as effective. Mendelian randomisation uses inherited variants that alter a factor for life, and because these variants are allocated at conception, they are less affected by the confounding that distorts observational data. Both approaches have assumptions, and their results must be weighed with the others.

The evidence for a clinical decision combines these lines. A factor with a strong trial effect and a plausible mechanism is a firm basis for treatment. A factor supported only by observational association is a hypothesis, however strong the association, and a treatment based on it should be tested. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the mechanisms by which factors act on cells, and the mechanism strengthens a causal claim without replacing the trial.

The judgement of cardiovascular risk is therefore a judgement about the strength of evidence, and the strength depends on the design that produced it.
