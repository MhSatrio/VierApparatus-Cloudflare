The heart is a four-chambered muscular pump. The two atria receive blood from the veins and deliver it to the two ventricles, which eject it into the arteries, the right ventricle to the lungs and the left to the body. Valves at the outlets of the ventricles and between the atria and ventricles keep the flow moving in one direction. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the tissues that make up the wall, and the muscle layer of the ventricles is thick because it must generate the pressure needed to push blood against the resistance of the systemic circulation.

The cardiac cycle has two phases. In diastole the chambers relax and fill, with the atrioventricular valves open and the ventricles receiving most of their blood passively before the atria contract. In systole the ventricles contract, the valves close and the pressure rises until it exceeds that in the arteries, when the outlet valves open and blood is ejected. The timing of these events is set by the electrical activity of the heart, which is generated and conducted through a specialised system.

The conduction system begins in the sinoatrial node in the right atrium, which spontaneously generates the impulse that sets the rate. The impulse spreads across the atria and reaches the atrioventricular node, which delays it so that the atria empty before the ventricles contract. It then passes down the bundle of His and the Purkinje fibres, which distribute it across the ventricles so that they contract together. Ioannidis (2005) described how a model that fits the cycle may still be incomplete, and the description of the pump should be checked against measured pressures and volumes.

The function of the pump is described by the relation between pressure and volume in each chamber, which varies over the cycle. The area enclosed by the loop of these values gives the work done in each beat, and the loop changes when the contractility, the filling or the resistance to ejection changes. Platt (1964) recommended measurements that could distinguish between these causes of a change in function, and the pressure-volume relation provides such a distinction.

The function of the heart depends on the cells that make up its muscle, which are described in the next chapter.
