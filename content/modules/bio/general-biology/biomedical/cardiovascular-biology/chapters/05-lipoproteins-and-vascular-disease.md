Lipoproteins carry cholesterol and triglycerides through the blood to the tissues. The particles made by the liver and the intestine deliver their lipid to cells, where it is taken up, and the remnants are cleared by receptors in the liver. The clearance depends on the apolipoproteins that the particle carries, which bind the receptors and allow the particle to be removed. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the receptor-mediated uptake of molecules by cells, and this mechanism underlies the clearance of lipoproteins from the blood.

The atherogenic risk of a lipoprotein depends on its ability to enter and be retained in the arterial wall. Particles that are small and dense, or that carry apolipoprotein B, can penetrate the intima, where their lipid is retained by binding to the proteoglycans of the matrix. Ross (1999) described the retention and the subsequent inflammatory response, and the retained lipid is the trigger for the development of the plaque. The modification of retained lipoprotein, such as its oxidation, makes the particle more inflammatory, and the apolipoproteins that it carries are altered in the process. Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein's function depends on its structure, which is why such changes can alter the particle's behaviour.

The balance of production and clearance sets the concentration of the circulating particles. A higher production or a reduced clearance raises the concentration, and the time the particles spend in the circulation increases their chance of entering the wall. Inherited changes in the receptors or in the apolipoproteins alter this balance, and the consequences can be large.

The link between lipoproteins and vascular disease has been tested in many trials, in which the lowering of lipoprotein concentration is compared with the absence of such lowering. Ioannidis (2005) described how an association between a marker and a disease can be stronger in observational studies than in trials, and the trial evidence is the stronger basis for the claim. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the alternatives, and the trials test the lipoprotein explanation against the others that the observational data could support.

The vascular effect of lipoproteins depends on both their concentration and their handling in the wall, and each must be considered in judging the risk of an individual.
