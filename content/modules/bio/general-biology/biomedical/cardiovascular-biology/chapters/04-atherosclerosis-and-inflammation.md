Ross (1999) argued that atherosclerosis is an inflammatory disease, and the view has since become central to understanding the condition. The process begins when the endothelium is injured or activated, and its junctions open so that lipoproteins can enter the wall of the artery and be retained there. The retained lipoproteins are modified, and the modified particles trigger an inflammatory response. Monocytes adhere to the activated endothelium, migrate into the intima and become macrophages, which take up the modified lipid and become foam cells. The accumulation of foam cells forms the fatty streak, the earliest visible lesion.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the adhesion molecules and chemotactic signals that bring leukocytes to a site of inflammation, and the same molecules operate in the arterial wall. The lesion grows as smooth muscle cells migrate from the media into the intima, proliferate and secrete the matrix that forms a fibrous cap over the lipid core. The cap separates the lipid from the blood, and its strength determines whether the plaque remains stable.

Advanced plaques have a lipid-rich core with a thin fibrous cap, and the cap is weakened by enzymes released by macrophages that digest the matrix. Rupture of the cap exposes the thrombogenic contents of the core, and a thrombus forms on the surface. Thrombosis at the site of rupture is the usual cause of acute events, and it is the complication described in the Human Pathology module. Coons et al. (1941) established the labelling of antibodies that is used to reveal the inflammatory cells in plaques by immunostaining.

The inflammatory view changes how risk is understood. Lipids are necessary for the disease, but the response of the wall to them determines the progression, and this response varies between individuals and regions. Ioannidis (2005) described how an explanation that fits a set of observations may be accepted before its alternatives are tested, and the inflammatory explanation has been tested by trials of anti-inflammatory agents, whose results must be weighed against the alternatives.

Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the alternatives, and the trials of anti-inflammatory treatment are a means of testing the inflammatory view against the lipid view.
