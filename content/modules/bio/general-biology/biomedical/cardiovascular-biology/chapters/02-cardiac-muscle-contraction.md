The cardiac myocyte contracts when an action potential sweeps across its membrane and into its interior through invaginations called the T-tubules. The depolarisation opens L-type calcium channels, and the small amount of calcium that enters triggers the release of a much larger store from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, through ryanodine receptors. This process is called calcium-induced calcium release, and Alberts et al. (2022) describe the way in which the signal is amplified at each step. The calcium binds to troponin on the thin filaments, which moves tropomyosin away from the binding sites for myosin.

Myosin heads then bind to actin, pull the filaments past one another and release, with the energy supplied by the hydrolysis of ATP. The repeated cycles shorten the sarcomere and generate force. The force depends on the number of cross-bridges formed, which depends on how much calcium is available and how quickly it reaches the filaments. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the activity of a protein depends on its structure, and the myosin head and the troponin complex must fold correctly for the cycle to work.

Relaxation requires that calcium be removed from the cytoplasm. Most of it is pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum by an ATPase whose activity is regulated by a small protein, and a smaller amount leaves the cell through an exchanger that moves sodium in. The speed of this removal determines how quickly the muscle relaxes and how much time remains for filling. Changes in the regulation of the pump alter both the relaxation and the calcium available for the next beat.

The strength of each beat therefore reflects the calcium transient, and the rate of relaxation reflects the speed of its removal. Ioannidis (2005) described how a single measure of contractility can be interpreted as a cause when it reflects several processes, and Platt (1964) recommended measurements that could separate them, such as calcium transients and the force they generate.

The mechanism of contraction is the basis for the drugs and disorders discussed in the later chapters, and each of them acts on one of these steps.
