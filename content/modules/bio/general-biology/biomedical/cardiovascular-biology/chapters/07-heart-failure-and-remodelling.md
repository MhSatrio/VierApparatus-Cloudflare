The heart adapts to a sustained increase in its load. Pressure overload, as in hypertension, and volume overload, as after a valve leak, cause the myocytes to enlarge by adding sarcomeres, a process called hypertrophy. In the early stage the enlargement maintains the output of the heart, and the increase in wall thickness reduces the stress on each fibre. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the growth of cells in response to signals, and the hypertrophic response of the myocyte is a growth response that is regulated by similar pathways.

Remodelling extends beyond the myocytes. The fibroblasts of the heart produce more collagen, the matrix becomes stiffer, and some myocytes die and are replaced by scar. The chamber dilates and the wall thins in some forms of failure, while in others it remains thick and stiff. The change in shape alters the geometry of the chamber and the forces on the fibres, and these changes feed back on the remodelling process.

The transition from compensated hypertrophy to failure is not sharply defined, but it is marked by a decline in the contractile reserve of the heart and by changes in the calcium handling of the myocyte. The calcium transients become smaller and slower, and the pump for calcium removal is reduced in activity, which weakens the beat and slows relaxation. The energy supply changes as well: the failing heart relies more on glucose and less on the oxidation of fatty acids, and its mitochondria produce ATP less efficiently. Mitchell (1961) established the chemiosmotic principle by which mitochondria make ATP, and a reduction in this efficiency reduces the energy available for contraction.

The treatments of failure act on the processes that drive remodelling, including the neurohormonal activation that increases the load on the heart. Ioannidis (2005) described how a biomarker of failure may reflect the severity of the disease without being its cause, and the evidence that a treatment changes the course of failure comes from trials. Platt (1964) recommended comparing the treatment with its alternatives, which is the logic of those trials.

The failing heart is therefore a system whose structure, energy supply and electrical behaviour have all changed, and each of these must be considered in its treatment.
