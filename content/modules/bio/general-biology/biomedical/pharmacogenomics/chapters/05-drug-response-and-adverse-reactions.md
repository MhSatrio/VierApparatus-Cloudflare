Some of the most serious adverse drug reactions have a genetic basis that can be identified before treatment. Hypersensitivity reactions to certain drugs are strongly associated with particular alleles of the human leukocyte antigen genes, which encode the molecules that present peptides to T cells. A drug or its metabolite may bind the presenting groove of one of these molecules and provoke a response, and the allele determines how well the drug fits. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the MHC molecules and the presentation of peptides on which this association depends, and the risk is highest in carriers of the allele whose groove binds the drug most readily.

Matzinger (2002) proposed that immune responses are shaped by danger as well as by recognition, and a drug reaction in a genetically susceptible person may involve tissue damage that makes the response more likely. The association with an allele therefore reflects both the recognition of the drug and the context in which it is given, and a predictive test must account for both.

Enzyme deficiency can produce a different kind of reaction. A person lacking an enzyme that protects red cells from oxidative damage may develop haemolysis when given a drug that generates oxidants. The deficiency is inherited, and the enzyme activity can be measured before treatment, so that the drug can be avoided. The reaction is a predictable consequence of the biochemistry, and the test that identifies the deficiency is a clear example of a pharmacogenomic decision that is useful and that avoids harm.

Efficacy can also depend on genetics. A variant in a drug target or in a component of its signalling pathway may reduce the response, so that a drug that works in most patients fails in some. Such cases show that the variation in the target can matter as much as the variation in the enzymes that clear the drug.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a severe reaction that is rare may be linked to an allele by a small study, and the association must be confirmed in a population with enough cases to show a reliable predictive value. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the alternatives, such as whether the reaction depends on the allele or on the exposure of the population to the drug.

The reactions that have a clear genetic basis are the strongest examples of the value of pharmacogenomics, and they show how a test before treatment can prevent a serious harm.
