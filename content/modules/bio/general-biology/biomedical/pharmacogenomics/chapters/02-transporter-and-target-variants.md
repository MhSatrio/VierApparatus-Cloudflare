Drug transporters are membrane proteins that move drugs across barriers, including the gut wall, the liver, the kidney tubule and the blood-brain barrier. Their activity determines how much of a drug enters the body and how much reaches the organs where it acts. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the classes of membrane transporter, and the uptake and efflux pumps that drugs use are members of these families. A variant that reduces the activity of an uptake transporter lowers the concentration of the drug in the cell, while a variant that increases efflux lowers it further, and the effect depends on where in the body the transporter is expressed.

Variation in transporters can therefore change the response without changing the enzymes that metabolise the drug. The concentration at the target is the product of the dose, the absorption and the transport into the target tissue, and a change in any of these alters the response. Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein's function depends on its folding, and a variant that destabilises a transporter reduces its activity by reducing the amount that reaches the membrane.

Variation in the drug target affects the response directly. A change in the binding site may reduce the affinity of the drug, and a change in the signalling domain may alter how the receptor responds once it is occupied. Some cases show a target variant predicting response more clearly than variation in metabolism, and such cases illustrate the value of testing the target as well as the enzymes.

The effects of these variants are harder to predict than those of metabolism. A transporter may be expressed in several tissues with different roles, and a variant may have opposite effects in different organs. The target may be part of a network, so that the effect of its variation depends on the other components. The clinical significance must therefore be shown in the population that receives the drug, not only inferred from the molecular change.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a variant that changes a protein in a laboratory assay can be assumed to change the response in patients, and Platt (1964) recommended testing the alternative explanations, such as the effect of the variant on the target in patients against its effect on the drug concentration.

Transporters and targets extend the pharmacogenomic picture beyond metabolism, and they require their own evidence before a genotype is used to guide treatment.
