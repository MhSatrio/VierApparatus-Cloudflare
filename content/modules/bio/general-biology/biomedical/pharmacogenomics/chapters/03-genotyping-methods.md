A pharmacogenomic test detects a set of known variants by an assay that distinguishes the alleles. Targeted methods, often based on the amplification of the region around each variant, as Saiki et al. (1988) established for PCR, are fast and inexpensive for a small panel of alleles. Sequencing of the relevant genes detects the known variants and any new ones, and it is more informative when the gene has many alleles, but it costs more and produces data that must be interpreted. Copy number changes, which are important for some enzymes whose genes are duplicated, require a separate quantitative assay, since a standard allele assay does not reveal them.

The genotype is converted into a phenotype, the predicted enzyme or transporter activity, by a set of rules. Each combination of alleles is assigned an activity score or a metaboliser category, and the rules depend on the knowledge of each allele's effect. Evans and Relling (1999) described the importance of this translation, and the rules must be updated as the effect of each allele is studied. A genotype that is not in the rules, or that is observed in a population where its effect is unknown, cannot be given a clinical meaning.

Errors in genotyping arise from several sources. A variant may be missed if it lies outside the region the assay targets, and a sample from a patient who has received a transfusion or a transplant may carry the genotype of another person. Linked variants can be mistaken for the causal one, and an assay that detects a common allele may misassign a rare variant that differs at a nearby site.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the accuracy of a test is assumed rather than measured, and each genotyping method should be validated against a reference set of samples with known genotypes. Platt (1964) recommended a comparison of alternatives, and concordance between two methods on the same samples tests whether either is reliable.

The genotype is the starting point of a pharmacogenomic decision, and its reliability limits the value of every decision that depends on it.
