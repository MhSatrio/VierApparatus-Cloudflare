The cytochrome P450 enzymes metabolise a large share of clinically used drugs, and their genes are highly variable. A single nucleotide change can alter the amino acid sequence of the enzyme, and Anfinsen (1973) showed that a change in sequence can alter the fold and therefore the activity. Some variants abolish activity, others reduce it, and some increase it through changes in expression, such as the duplication of the gene. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the endoplasmic reticulum enzymes that carry out these reactions, and the variation in their activity follows from the variation in their structure and in their amount.

The combination of the variants a person carries produces a metaboliser phenotype. A poor metaboliser clears the drug slowly and accumulates it, which increases the exposure and the risk of dose-related toxicity. An ultrarapid metaboliser clears it quickly, so that a standard dose may not reach an effective concentration, or so that a prodrug is converted to its active form too rapidly. Intermediate and normal metabolisers fall between these. The classes are defined by the genotype and the enzyme activity measured in the laboratory, and they are simplifications of a continuous distribution.

The phase two enzymes, which attach polar groups to drugs, also show inherited variation. A reduced activity of one of these enzymes can increase the toxicity of a drug that is cleared through it, and the variant may be present in a substantial fraction of the population. The contribution of each enzyme to a drug's clearance is specific, so that a variant matters only for drugs that the enzyme handles.

Metaboliser status predicts exposure, but the prediction is not perfect. Other drugs that inhibit or induce the enzyme, the diet, the liver function and the age of the patient all change clearance, and a genotype explains only part of the variation. Ioannidis (2005) described how a genetic association that explains part of an outcome may be presented as the explanation of the whole, and the proportion explained should be stated.

Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the explanations of an unexpected response, and comparing the measured drug concentration with the predicted one for each genotype is such a test.

Metabolic variation is the most developed part of pharmacogenomics, and its predictions are only as good as the measurements of exposure that test them.
