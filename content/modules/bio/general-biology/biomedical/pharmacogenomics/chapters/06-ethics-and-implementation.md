Pharmacogenomic testing is a genetic test, and it carries the ethical obligations of any such test. The patient must understand what is being tested, what the result can and cannot predict, and what will be done with it. Consent must cover the storage of genetic data, its use for research and its disclosure to other clinicians. A pharmacogenomic panel may reveal variants that are unrelated to the drug being prescribed, and the policy on such incidental findings must be decided in advance and explained to the patient.

Privacy is a central concern. Genetic data identify a person and their relatives, and they can be re-identified even when stripped of names. The data must be stored securely, access must be limited to those with a clinical need, and the sharing of data for research must follow the consent given. The value of pharmacogenomics depends on the data being available to clinicians at the point of prescribing, and this availability must be balanced against the risk of disclosure.

Misclassification is a harm. A person wrongly labelled as a poor metaboliser may be denied an effective drug, and a person wrongly labelled as a normal metaboliser may be exposed to toxicity. The consequences of error fall on the individual, and the accuracy of the test, as discussed in the genotyping chapter, must be measured and reported to the clinician. Ioannidis (2005) described how a test with an unknown error rate can be used with unwarranted confidence, and the error rate should be part of the information given with the result.

Implementation requires more than a validated test. The rules that translate genotype into action must be agreed, the result must be available in the electronic record at the time of prescribing, the clinicians must be trained to act on it and the outcomes must be monitored after introduction. A test that is validated but not used, or that is used without the agreed rules, produces no benefit.

Platt (1964) recommended a process that reveals its own failures, and the monitoring of outcomes after implementation is the means of detecting whether the strategy works in practice as it did in the trial.

Pharmacogenomics is valuable only when the test is accurate, the result is used, and the patient is protected, and each of these requires a deliberate effort.
