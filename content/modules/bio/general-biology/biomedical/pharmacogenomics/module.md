# Pharmacogenomics

The Pharmacology module described how a drug is absorbed, metabolised and acts on its target, and it noted that the same dose produces different effects in different people. Pharmacogenomics is the study of the inherited part of that variation. It asks which genetic differences alter the handling of a drug or the response of its target, and whether knowing a person's genotype can improve the choice and dose of treatment. The field joins the genetics of the Genetics module to the clinical decisions of the Pharmacology module.

The chapters follow the logic of the subject. The genetic variants of drug metabolism come first, with the enzymes of the cytochrome P450 family as the central example, followed by variants in transporters and in drug targets. The methods by which these variants are detected are then described, and the final chapters address the evidence needed before a genotype is used clinically, and the testing of pharmacogenomic claims in trials. Evans and Relling (1999) set out the case for translating functional genomics into prescribing, and their argument is the starting point for the evidence chapters.

Pharmacogenomic associations have a history of promising findings that were not confirmed in larger studies, and this makes the design of evidence central. Ioannidis (2005) described how small studies of genetic associations often overstate the effect, and Platt (1964) recommended comparing a genotype-guided strategy with the alternatives that could explain the same outcome.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell biology of the enzymes and transporters whose variation is studied, and each variant must be understood in terms of its effect on protein function.
