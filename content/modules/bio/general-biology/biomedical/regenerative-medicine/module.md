# Regenerative Medicine

Regenerative medicine aims to restore tissue that has been lost to injury or disease, rather than to suppress the disease itself. Its tools are the stem cells described in the Stem Cell Biology module, the reprogramming of mature cells into a stem-like state and the engineering of tissues from cells and scaffolds. The field has made real progress, most clearly in the transplantation of blood-forming cells, which has been practised for decades. It has also produced many claims that have not been confirmed, and the discipline depends on separating the two.

The chapters follow the path of a regenerative therapy. The potency of the stem cell types comes first, then the derivation of embryonic stem cells as described by Thomson et al. (1998), and the reprogramming of adult cells to pluripotency as described by Takahashi and Yamanaka (2006). Haematopoietic transplantation, which rests on the progenitor populations identified by Till and McCulloch (1961), is treated as the established example. Tissue engineering, the manufacture and quality of cell products and the safety of these therapies follow, and the final chapter addresses the clinical evidence.

The history of regenerative medicine includes enthusiasm for therapies that failed when tested properly. Ioannidis (2005) described how early results that are not controlled can overstate the benefit, and Platt (1964) recommended tests that could show a therapy to be ineffective. The chapters treat the controlled trial as the standard against which every regenerative claim must be judged.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the developmental biology of the cells whose behaviour regenerative therapies depend on, and the regeneration of a tissue in the body is governed by signals that a therapy must reproduce.
