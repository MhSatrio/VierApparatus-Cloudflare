Stem cell potency describes the range of cell types a stem cell can produce. A totipotent cell, such as the zygote and its earliest descendants, can form the whole organism, including the extra-embryonic tissues. A pluripotent cell, such as the cell of the inner cell mass of the blastocyst, can form all the tissues of the body but not the extra-embryonic tissues. A multipotent cell is restricted to a lineage, and a haematopoietic stem cell, for example, gives rise to the blood cells alone. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the developmental progression by which potency narrows as cells commit to a fate, and the potency of a cell reflects its position in that progression.

Till and McCulloch (1961) demonstrated that the multipotent stem cells of the marrow can regenerate the blood, and their work established the operational definition of a stem cell by its ability to repopulate. Potency is therefore a functional property, measured by what a cell can do, and not by its appearance. A cell that expresses markers associated with stemness has not been shown to be potent until it has been tested for differentiation.

The test of pluripotency is the formation of cells of all three embryonic germ layers, both in culture and in tissue, and in some systems the formation of a whole organism. The test must be repeated for each line and each passage, since the potency of a culture can change with time in culture. Ioannidis (2005) described how a marker that is associated with a property may be taken as proof of it, and the functional test is the proof that potency requires.

The distinction between potency and identity matters. A pluripotent line that differentiates into many cell types may still contain a mixture of cells, and the proportion that can form a given tissue must be measured. A population described as stem cells may be heterogeneous, and its potency is a property of the population, not of each cell.

Platt (1964) recommended a test that could show a claim to be wrong, and the demonstration of potency by differentiation is such a test, since a cell that cannot form the expected lineages fails it.

Potency is the first property a regenerative therapy must establish, and it is measured by function rather than inferred from markers.
