Regenerative therapies have often been introduced on the strength of early and uncontrolled results, and many of these results have not survived controlled testing. An uncontrolled study compares patients before and after treatment, and any improvement may reflect the natural course of the disease, the expectations of patients and clinicians or the regression to the mean that follows selection of patients at their worst. Ioannidis (2005) described how such biases inflate apparent benefit, and the history of cell therapy contains several examples in which the controlled trial showed no effect.

Controlled designs address these biases. A randomised trial compares the cell therapy with a control, and the control may be a sham procedure that mimics the intervention without delivering the cells. The sham is important for procedures that are invasive, because the procedure itself can produce a benefit. Blinding of patients and assessors prevents expectation from shaping the outcome, and the outcome must be measured objectively where possible.

The choice of control reflects the question. A placebo or a sham tests whether the therapy is better than nothing, and an active control tests whether it is better than the current treatment. Platt (1964) described the strong inference that a comparison with a clear alternative makes possible, and a trial that compares a cell therapy with an effective standard tests the claim in the most useful way.

Small studies are unreliable as evidence of benefit. A study with few patients can detect only large effects, and the effects it does detect are often overestimated, because the studies that report large effects are the ones that are published. Contopoulos-Ioannidis et al. (2003) described how promising findings from basic research often fail to reach clinical application, and the failure is often traced to small and biased early studies.

The reporting of regenerative trials must state the source and processing of the cells, the number of patients, the randomisation and blinding, the control, the outcomes and the adverse events. A report that omits the harms or the failures does not allow the evidence to be judged.

Regenerative claims are judged by controlled trials with clear outcomes, and the early enthusiasm that precedes them is not evidence of benefit.
