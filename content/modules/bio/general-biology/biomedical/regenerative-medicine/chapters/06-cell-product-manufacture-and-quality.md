A cell therapy product is a biological material, and its properties vary from batch to batch in ways that a chemical drug does not. The manufacture must therefore be controlled at every step, from the collection of the source cells through their expansion, selection and formulation to the final container. The process is performed under conditions that prevent contamination, and it is documented so that each step can be reviewed. The product is a living population, and its quality is a property of the whole population rather than of a single chemical entity.

The release criteria define what a batch must show before it is used. Identity confirms that the cells are the intended type, purity measures the proportion of the desired cells and the absence of contaminating cells, and sterility excludes bacteria and fungi. Viability measures the proportion of living cells, and potency measures the ability of the cells to produce the intended effect. Potency is the most difficult criterion, because it requires an assay of function, and Hanahan and Weinberg (2000) described how cells acquire functional capacities that a simple marker may not reveal.

Each test has its own limits. A marker-based identity test may not distinguish a closely related cell type, and a sterility test may fail to detect a low-level contamination in a small sample. Ioannidis (2005) described how a release test that is weak can allow a poor batch to pass, and the sensitivity of each test must be established before it is used to release a product.

Traceability links each product to its donor, its source material and its process. If a problem is found in a patient, the batch and its history must be identified so that other patients who received related product can be contacted. The record must be kept for the life of the product and beyond, and it must be secure.

Platt (1964) recommended a process that reveals its own failures, and the combination of release tests, retained samples and traceability forms such a process for a product whose variability cannot be eliminated.

The quality of a cell product is the assurance that each batch does what it is meant to do and nothing it is not meant to do, and it is built into the manufacturing process rather than tested in at the end.
