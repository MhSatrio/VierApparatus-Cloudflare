Tissue engineering combines cells with a scaffold that holds them in a three-dimensional arrangement and provides the signals needed for them to form tissue. The scaffold may be natural, such as decellularised matrix, or synthetic, such as a polymer that degrades as the tissue forms. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the extracellular matrix whose structure the scaffold imitates, and the cells in a tissue depend on the matrix for their position, their shape and their signals. The scaffold must be porous enough for cells to enter and for nutrients to diffuse, and it must degrade at a rate that matches the formation of new tissue.

The biomaterial is chosen for its mechanical properties and for its interaction with the cells. Its stiffness influences the behaviour of cells, since many cells sense the rigidity of their surroundings and respond by changing their shape and gene expression. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the function of a protein depends on its structure, and the proteins adsorbed to the scaffold surface alter the signals that cells receive, so the surface chemistry matters as well as the bulk properties.

Thickness is limited by oxygen and nutrient supply. Cells more than a few hundred micrometres from a blood supply die, and a large engineered tissue will have a necrotic core unless it is vascularised. Vascularisation is achieved by seeding endothelial cells, by introducing vessels from the host, or by printing channels, and each approach has limits in the size and the connection of the vessels formed.

Translation to patients is difficult. The engineered tissue must integrate with the host, must function under the mechanical loads of its site and must not provoke a harmful immune response. The tests of these properties are often performed in animals, and their predictive value for humans must be shown rather than assumed.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a result in a model system can appear to predict success in patients when the model differs in important ways, and Platt (1964) recommended comparing the engineered tissue with the alternatives that would achieve the same repair.

Tissue engineering offers a way to build tissues outside the body, and its success depends on controlling the cell, the scaffold and the vascular supply together.
