Takahashi and Yamanaka (2006) showed that mouse fibroblasts could be reprogrammed to a pluripotent state by the introduction of four transcription factors, which are now known as the Yamanaka factors. The cells produced by this method resembled embryonic stem cells in their morphology, their gene expression and their ability to form the three germ layers. The finding showed that the identity of a mature cell is not fixed, and that its state can be reversed by changing the set of transcription factors that the cell expresses. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the transcriptional networks that maintain cell identity, and reprogramming alters these networks.

Reprogramming is a slow and inefficient process. Most of the cells receiving the factors do not become pluripotent, and the process passes through stages in which the cell gradually loses its original identity and acquires that of a stem cell. The efficiency depends on the cell type, the factors and the conditions, and the proportion of cells that complete reprogramming is typically small. The stages are revealed by the expression of markers over time, and the completion of reprogramming must be confirmed by the functional tests of pluripotency.

The factors were delivered by viruses that integrate into the genome, and this raised a central concern. Integration can disrupt genes, and the continued expression of the factors can maintain a state that is not appropriate for therapy. Non-integrating methods, including episomal vectors, RNA and protein delivery, have been developed to avoid these problems, and each must be shown to produce cells that are free of unwanted genetic change.

The risks of induced pluripotent cells are the risks of reprogramming and of the culture that follows. Genetic and epigenetic changes arise during culture, and some reprogrammed lines retain an epigenetic memory of their original tissue that biases their differentiation. Ioannidis (2005) described how a method that is rapidly adopted may be accepted before its risks have been measured, and Platt (1964) recommended tests that could show the cells to be unsafe as well as safe.

Reprogramming showed that the identity of a cell can be reversed, and its clinical use depends on demonstrating that the reversed cell is safe and effective.
