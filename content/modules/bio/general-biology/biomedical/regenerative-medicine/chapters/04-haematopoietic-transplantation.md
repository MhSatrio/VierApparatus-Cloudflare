Haematopoietic transplantation is the most established form of cell therapy. Stem cells from the marrow or from circulating blood are infused into a patient whose own blood-forming system has been destroyed, and the cells travel to the marrow, where they engraft and restore the production of all the blood lineages. Till and McCulloch (1961) showed that a small number of cells from the marrow can regenerate the blood of an irradiated animal, and this principle is the basis of the clinical procedure. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the homing of cells to their niche and the signals that maintain them, and engraftment depends on these processes.

The patient must first be conditioned, usually by chemotherapy or radiation, to remove the existing stem cells and create space in the marrow. Conditioning is itself toxic, and its intensity is a trade-off between the chance of eliminating the disease or the host's cells and the damage to the tissues. The degree of conditioning determines whether the transplanted cells are rejected or engraft.

In allogeneic transplantation, the cells come from a donor, and the immune cells in the graft may attack the tissues of the recipient. This graft-versus-host reaction can be severe, and it is controlled by immunosuppression. The same immune activity can sometimes destroy residual malignant cells, which is a benefit that makes the reaction partly desirable. The balance between these effects is central to the management of the procedure.

The sources of cells include the bone marrow, peripheral blood after mobilisation and umbilical cord blood. Each source has a different number of stem cells, a different risk of contamination and different kinetics of engraftment. The choice of source is made from the patient's condition and the donor available.

Ioannidis (2005) described how an established therapy can be extended to new indications without the evidence that the extension is beneficial, and Platt (1964) recommended comparing the new use with the alternatives. The success of transplantation is measured by survival, the reconstitution of the blood and the freedom from disease and from graft-related harm.

Haematopoietic transplantation shows that cell therapy can succeed when the biology is understood and the procedure is controlled, and it sets the standard that other regenerative approaches must meet.
