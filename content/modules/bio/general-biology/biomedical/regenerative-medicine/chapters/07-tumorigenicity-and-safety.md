A stem cell therapy carries the risk that the cells will form a tumour. Pluripotent cells that remain in the product can form teratomas, which are tumours containing tissues of several types, and even a small number of such cells can produce a tumour in an immunosuppressed host. Hanahan and Weinberg (2000) described the capabilities that a cell must acquire to become malignant, and a pluripotent cell has some of these, such as unlimited replicative potential, that make it a candidate for transformation. The risk is therefore a property of the cells themselves as well as of the culture.

Culture can introduce changes that increase the risk. Cells that are expanded for many passages acquire chromosomal abnormalities and mutations, and some of these confer growth advantages. Takahashi and Yamanaka (2006) delivered the factors by retroviruses, and the integration of foreign genes and the continued expression of the oncogene c-Myc are specific hazards of reprogramming. The genetic stability of the product must therefore be measured in each batch.

The immune system can react to the cells. Allogeneic cells from a donor are recognised as foreign, and the host's response can destroy the graft or cause injury. Autologous cells avoid this problem but require the manufacture of a product for each patient. The choice between these approaches involves a trade-off between safety and practicality.

Preclinical tests attempt to detect the risks. The product is injected into immunosuppressed animals, which are observed for the formation of tumours over a period. Karyotyping, sequencing and assays for specific oncogenic changes are performed on the cells. Ioannidis (2005) described how an animal test with a small number of animals and a short observation period can miss a rare risk, and the sensitivity of the test must be stated.

Platt (1964) recommended a test that could show a therapy to be unsafe, and the tumorigenicity test is such a test, since its purpose is to reveal a harm that would otherwise reach patients. A negative test does not exclude the risk, and the monitoring of patients after treatment remains necessary.

The safety of a stem cell therapy depends on the cells, the culture and the host, and each must be examined for the risk it could contribute.
