# Human Pathology

Pathology is the study of disease in tissues and organs, and it provides the link between the cell biology described in the earlier modules and the disorders that patients experience. The Cell Biology, Immunology and Cancer Biology modules explained the processes that go wrong. Human pathology describes what those failures look like in the tissue, how they are recognised under the microscope and how the diagnosis is made and recorded.

The chapters move from the response of the cell to injury, through the inflammatory response that follows, and the cell death and neoplastic growth that can result. The circulation and the infections that disturb tissue are then described, followed by the methods of diagnosis. The last chapter covers autopsy and the reporting of pathological findings, which is the form in which the knowledge is passed on.

Pathology is an interpretive discipline, and its conclusions depend on the appearance of tissue that is seen in a limited number of sections. Ioannidis (2005) described how a diagnosis based on a pattern can be confirmed by expectation, and Platt (1964) recommended a diagnostic approach that tests competing explanations. The chapters treat the differential diagnosis as a comparison of alternatives.

Kerr et al. (1972) identified apoptosis as a distinct form of cell death, and Hanahan and Weinberg (2000) set out the hallmarks through which normal cells become cancerous. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell biology that underlies both.
