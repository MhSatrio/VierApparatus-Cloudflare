A post-mortem examination establishes the cause of death and the disease present in the body. It is performed by examining the organs in turn, describing their size, colour, consistency and any lesions, and sampling the tissue for microscopy. The examination records the findings that were present, and it is the last opportunity to observe the disease as a whole. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the organ systems and their normal structure, against which the findings are judged.

The examination distinguishes three kinds of finding. The cause of death is the condition that led directly to death. The underlying disease is the process that gave rise to the cause, and it may be several steps removed from the death. Incidental findings are lesions that did not contribute to the death, and they are reported so that the record is complete. The separation of these categories is essential, because a report that does not distinguish them can attribute the death to a finding that was incidental.

Microscopy confirms and refines the gross findings. The sections are stained, and the immunostains described in the immunohistochemistry module are applied where the diagnosis needs them. Coons et al. (1941) established the principle of revealing antigens by labelled antibodies, which is still used to identify the nature of a lesion after death.

The report states the diagnoses and the certainty of each. A diagnosis confirmed by microscopy and by a specific marker is stated firmly, and a diagnosis that rests on the gross appearance alone is stated with its limits. Ioannidis (2005) described how an overconfident report can mislead the families, the clinicians and the research that relies on it, and the certainty must be stated honestly.

Platt (1964) recommended that a conclusion be stated in a form that could be shown false, and a report that lists the alternatives considered and the reasons they were excluded is more useful than one that states only the conclusion.
