A biopsy is a sample of tissue removed from a living patient for diagnosis. The sampling must capture the abnormal area, which is not always visible, and an unrepresentative sample can miss the lesion or the most aggressive part of it. The sample is then fixed, which preserves the tissue as described in the immunohistochemistry module, processed into paraffin, sectioned and stained. Each step can change the appearance of the tissue, and the pathologist must know the processing to read the sections correctly.

Frozen sections give a rapid result during surgery. The tissue is frozen and cut quickly, and a diagnosis is given while the patient is still in theatre. The method sacrifices some architectural detail and the quality of the stain, and its accuracy is lower than that of paraffin sections. The result is used to guide the surgery, and the final diagnosis is made on the permanent section.

The diagnosis combines morphology with special stains and immunohistochemistry. Coons et al. (1941) established that labelled antibodies reveal the presence of an antigen, and the immunostain confirms or excludes a diagnosis that morphology alone cannot settle. Anfinsen (1973) showed that an antigen's recognition depends on its structure, which is why the choice of antibody and the fixation are part of the diagnostic process.

Confidence in a diagnosis is stated. A pattern that is typical of one lesion and that is confirmed by a specific marker supports a firm diagnosis, while a pattern that fits several lesions supports only a provisional one. Ioannidis (2005) described how a diagnosis made with confidence can be wrong when the alternatives have not been excluded, and Platt (1964) recommended that the diagnosis be made by excluding the alternatives one at a time.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular features that the pathologist recognises, and the diagnosis should state the features on which it rests.
