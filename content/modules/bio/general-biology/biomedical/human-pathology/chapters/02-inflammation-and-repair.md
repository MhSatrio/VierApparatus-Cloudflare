Inflammation is the response of living tissue to injury or infection. In its acute form it begins within minutes. Blood vessels near the injury dilate and become more permeable, so that fluid and plasma proteins leave the circulation and the tissue swells. Neutrophils adhere to the endothelium, migrate between the endothelial cells and move into the tissue, following chemical signals released by the injured cells. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the adhesion and migration of leukocytes that underlie this sequence, and the cells that arrive are then recognised in tissue sections by their nuclear shape and their granules.

The response is controlled by mediators, which include the prostaglandins, cytokines and complement fragments. Coons et al. (1941) introduced the labelling of antibodies to reveal antigens in tissue, and the same logic now identifies the cells and mediators of inflammation by immunostaining. The pattern of the infiltrate reveals the nature of the stimulus: neutrophils predominate in acute bacterial infection, while lymphocytes and macrophages predominate in chronic inflammation.

Repair follows inflammation. Where the tissue can regenerate, cells of the same type divide to replace those lost, and the architecture is restored. Where the damage is extensive, or the tissue cannot regenerate, a scar of fibrous connective tissue is laid down. The scar restores the continuity of the tissue, but it does not restore its function, and the distinction is central to pathological assessment.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a pattern of inflammation that is attributed to one cause may have several, and the criteria used to assign the cause should be defined. Platt (1964) recommended comparing alternatives, and the differential diagnosis of an inflammatory pattern is a comparison of the causes that could produce it.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that activity depends on structure, which is why each mediator acts only through a receptor that fits its shape.
