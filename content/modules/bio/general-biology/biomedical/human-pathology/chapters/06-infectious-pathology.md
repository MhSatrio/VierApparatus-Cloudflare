Infectious agents damage tissue in two ways. Some act directly, by killing cells, releasing toxins or disrupting the membranes of the cells they invade. Others act indirectly, through the response of the host, which produces inflammation that can damage the tissue it is meant to protect. The pattern of injury in the tissue therefore reflects both the agent and the immune response, and the pathologist reads the two together. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the host defences and the mechanisms by which pathogens evade them, which underlie these patterns.

Bacterial infection commonly produces neutrophilic inflammation and necrosis, forming abscesses when the pus is walled off. Viral infection may cause the death of infected cells, the formation of characteristic inclusion bodies within the nucleus or cytoplasm and a lymphocytic response. Fungal infection often shows granulomas with the organisms visible within them, and parasitic infection may produce eosinophilic infiltrates and chronic inflammation. Each pattern is a clue that narrows the differential diagnosis.

Identification of the agent uses special stains and immunohistochemistry. Coons et al. (1941) established the use of labelled antibodies to reveal their antigen, and the modern stains use antibodies against the organism's own proteins to reveal its presence in the tissue. A stain that identifies the organism is more informative than the pattern alone, because many agents produce similar patterns.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the presence of an organism in a sample does not establish that it caused the disease, and the causal link must be shown by the pattern and the context. Platt (1964) recommended comparing alternatives, and the differential diagnosis of an infection weighs the candidate agents against one another.

The findings in infectious pathology are most reliable when the stain, the pattern and the clinical context agree.
