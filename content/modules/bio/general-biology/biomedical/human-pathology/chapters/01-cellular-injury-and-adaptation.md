A cell that is exposed to stress responds in one of several ways. Some responses are adaptive: a cell that is challenged by a sustained demand may enlarge, a process called hypertrophy, or may divide more often, which is hyperplasia. A cell may shrink, which is atrophy, or change its type to one better suited to the environment, which is metaplasia. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling that controls cell size and division, and these adaptive responses are alterations of those controls in response to the demands placed on the tissue.

Injury occurs when the stress exceeds the capacity of the cell to adapt. Reversible injury is marked by cell swelling, caused by the failure of the membrane pumps that regulate water and ions, and by the accumulation of fat in the cytoplasm. These changes can be reversed when the cause is removed. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the structure of proteins depends on their environment, and the loss of the correct environment in injured cells leads to the misfolding and aggregation of proteins that is visible under the microscope.

Irreversible injury is marked by the loss of the membrane integrity and the release of the contents of the cell. The nucleus may shrink and fragment, and the cytoplasm becomes eosinophilic. The distinction between reversible and irreversible injury is made from the morphology, and the pathologist must judge the state of the cell from a single section.

Kerr et al. (1972) distinguished apoptosis from the necrosis that follows severe injury, and the two forms of death leave different patterns in tissue. The judgement is therefore made on the appearance of the cell death, and the features used must be stated.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a diagnosis based on an appearance that is ambiguous can be settled by the expectation of the observer, and the criteria for each change should be defined.
