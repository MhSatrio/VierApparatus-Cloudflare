A neoplasm is a new growth of tissue whose cells have lost the normal control of division. Neoplasms are classed as benign or malignant. A benign tumour grows in a cohesive mass that remains within a capsule or boundary, and its cells look relatively like those of the tissue of origin. A malignant tumour invades the surrounding tissue, and its cells show atypia: enlarged nuclei, irregular chromatin, abnormal shapes and frequent mitoses. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the controls on cell division that the neoplastic cell has escaped, and these controls explain the growth pattern seen in tissue.

Hanahan and Weinberg (2000) described the hallmarks through which cells acquire the capacity to form malignant tumours. These include self-sufficiency in growth signals, insensitivity to growth-inhibitory signals, evasion of cell death, limitless replicative potential, sustained angiogenesis and tissue invasion with metastasis. Each hallmark is a property that the pathologist can sometimes see in the tissue: the proliferation index, the vascular pattern, the invasive front and the nuclear features of the cells.

Malignancy is acquired through a series of changes rather than a single event. Each change confers a growth advantage that allows the clone to expand, and the clone carries the accumulated changes to its descendants. The stepwise pattern explains why most cancers arise in older tissue, where the changes have had time to accumulate.

The distinction between benign and malignant is not always clear. Some lesions show features of both, and the diagnosis depends on the pattern of growth and on the extent of invasion. Ioannidis (2005) described how a category assigned to an ambiguous lesion can influence the treatment and the outcome, and the criteria used should be explicit.

Platt (1964) recommended comparing alternatives, and the differential diagnosis of a mass is a comparison of its features against those of the possible neoplasms.
