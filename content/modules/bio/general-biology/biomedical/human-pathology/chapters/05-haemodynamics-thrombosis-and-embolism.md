Blood flow in the body is normally smooth and the blood normally does not clot within the vessels. Thrombosis is the formation of a clot inside a vessel, and it is favoured by three factors that were described in the nineteenth century as the triad of Virchow: injury to the endothelium, changes in the flow of blood and changes in the constituents of the blood. Each factor is visible in tissue. An injured endothelium exposes the collagen of the wall, which activates platelets, and stasis allows clotting factors to accumulate. Ross (1999) described how the wall of the vessel responds to such injury through inflammation, which is the basis of atherosclerosis.

A thrombus is classed by its appearance. A white thrombus, formed in fast flow, is made mainly of platelets, while a red thrombus, formed in slow flow, contains a large number of red cells trapped in fibrin. The layering seen in tissue reflects changes in the flow during formation, and the pathologist uses this to date the clot.

A thrombus may detach and travel as an embolus, which lodges in a vessel downstream and blocks it. The consequence depends on the site. An embolus that lodges in a vessel supplying a tissue without an alternative route causes infarction, which is necrosis caused by the loss of blood supply. The pattern of the infarct, pale in solid organs and haemorrhagic in organs with a dual supply, is seen in the section.

Circulatory disturbances also cause oedema, the accumulation of fluid in tissue, and congestion, the engorgement of tissue with blood. These are caused by changes in the pressure within the vessels and in the osmotic balance, and they are described by the flow of fluid across the capillary wall.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a cause that is assumed for a finding can be accepted without testing, and the features of a thrombus and its source should be examined before a cause is assigned. Platt (1964) recommended a comparison of alternative causes, and the separation of thrombosis from other causes of infarction is such a comparison. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the coagulation pathways that the blood uses to form a clot.
