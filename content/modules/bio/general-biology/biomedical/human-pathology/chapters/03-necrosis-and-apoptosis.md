Necrosis is the death of cells in living tissue, and it results from injury that the cell cannot survive. The cell swells, its organelles break down and its membrane ruptures, releasing its contents into the surrounding tissue, where they provoke inflammation. Necrosis takes several forms in tissue. Coagulative necrosis, seen in the heart and kidney after ischaemia, preserves the outline of the dead cells for a time. Liquefactive necrosis, typical of the brain and of abscesses, dissolves the tissue. Caseous necrosis, in tuberculosis, has an amorphous appearance, and fat necrosis is seen after tissue trauma.

Kerr et al. (1972) described apoptosis as a different process. The cell shrinks, its chromatin condenses and fragments, and its membrane forms blebs that enclose the fragments in membrane-bound bodies. The bodies are removed by neighbouring cells or by phagocytes without releasing their contents, so that apoptosis does not provoke inflammation. The process is active and requires the cell's machinery, which is why it is described as programmed.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the caspases and the signalling pathways that drive apoptosis, and the pathway that is activated determines the morphology seen in the section. Apoptosis is normal in development, in the turnover of tissues and in the removal of damaged cells, and it is abnormal when it is excessive in degenerative disease or insufficient in cancer.

The distinction between necrosis and apoptosis is made from the morphology in the tissue, and in some cases the two coexist. Ioannidis (2005) described how a classification imposed on an ambiguous appearance can be reinforced by the expectation of the observer, and the criteria used should be stated.

Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the alternatives, and specific markers, such as the detection of fragmented DNA, can be used to confirm the form of death where the morphology is uncertain.
