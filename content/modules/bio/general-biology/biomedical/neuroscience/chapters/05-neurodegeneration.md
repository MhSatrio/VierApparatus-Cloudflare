Neurodegenerative diseases share a common feature: the progressive loss of neurons in specific regions, with the abnormal accumulation of proteins in the cells that survive. Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein's native fold is determined by its sequence and environment, and misfolding produces forms that can aggregate into insoluble fibrils. Several disorders are defined by the protein that aggregates, and the fibrils are deposited as plaques or inclusions that are visible in the tissue. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the chaperones that normally prevent such aggregation, and their failure allows the misfolded forms to accumulate.

The aggregates are not simply inert deposits. Some forms are toxic to neurons, and the soluble oligomers that precede the fibrils may be more damaging than the mature aggregates. The mechanisms by which they damage cells include disruption of membranes, impairment of the proteasome and mitochondrial dysfunction. The relation between the aggregates and the death of neurons is still debated, and the aggregates may be a marker of a process rather than its direct cause.

Neurons are lost in a pattern. The dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra, the motor neurons of the spinal cord and the cortical neurons of the hippocampus each have particular vulnerabilities, related to their size, their connectivity and their energy demands. Long axons depend on transport along microtubules, and the disruption of this transport is an early feature of several disorders.

Neuronal death can follow the apoptotic pathway described by Kerr et al. (1972), and it can also follow other forms of death that do not show the same morphology. The pathway is therefore identified by specific markers rather than by appearance alone.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a biomarker associated with a disease may reflect its progression rather than its cause, and the evidence that a protein drives neurodegeneration must be tested by interventions that change the protein and measure the outcome.

The loss of neurons is slow and cumulative, and the study of its causes depends on separating the early events from the consequences of late damage.
