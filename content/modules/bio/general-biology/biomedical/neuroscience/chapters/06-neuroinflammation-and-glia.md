The brain was once thought to contain only neurons, but the glial cells make up most of its volume and perform essential functions. Astrocytes regulate the ionic and chemical environment around synapses, take up the transmitter released during activity and supply energy substrates to neurons. Oligodendrocytes wrap axons in myelin, which increases the speed of conduction along them. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the myelin sheath and its role in saltatory conduction, and the loss of myelin is a central feature of several disorders.

Microglia are the resident immune cells of the brain. They survey the tissue continuously, remove debris and dead cells and respond to injury or infection by changing their shape and gene expression. The response is protective when it clears damage and limits infection, but a sustained activation can release inflammatory mediators and reactive oxygen species that injure neurons. The balance between these states is what determines whether the inflammation helps or harms the tissue.

Ross (1999) argued that atherosclerosis is an inflammatory disease in which the response of the vessel wall drives the progression of the lesion, and a similar argument has been made for neurodegeneration. The inflammatory response in the brain may accelerate neuronal loss, and the drugs that reduce inflammation have been tested as a means of slowing progression. The evidence from these trials is mixed, and the inflammatory process may be a consequence as well as a cause of the disease.

Measuring neuroinflammation in patients is difficult. The markers in cerebrospinal fluid reflect the state of the brain only indirectly, and imaging of microglial activation depends on tracers whose specificity is limited. Ioannidis (2005) described how a marker that changes with a disease can be taken as a cause of it, and Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the alternatives, such as whether reducing the inflammation changes the course of the disease.

The glial cells are therefore active participants in the brain's health, and their behaviour in disease must be understood in the same detail as that of the neurons.
