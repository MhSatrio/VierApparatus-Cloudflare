Neuroscience has a record of findings that did not replicate, and many of the causes are methodological. Small samples are common, because the measurements are costly or invasive, and a study with small samples has low power to detect a true effect while overestimating the size of the effects it does detect. Ioannidis (2005) described this relation between power, bias and the likelihood that a published finding is true, and the report should state the sample size and the power calculation used.

The report must describe the subjects. In animal studies this includes the species, strain, sex, age, housing and the number of animals, together with the blinding of the investigators and the randomisation of treatment. In human studies it includes the selection criteria, the recruitment, the number who withdrew and the conditions of measurement. Bliss and Lømo (1973) reported their potentiation in a small number of anaesthetised animals, and the subsequent replication of the effect in many laboratories depended on the details of the preparation being reported with care.

The analysis must be specified before the data are seen. A study that tries several analyses and reports the one that gives significance produces an inflated rate of false positives, and the prespecification of the outcome, the statistical test and the correction for multiple comparisons prevents this. Platt (1964) recommended testing a hypothesis in a way that could show it false, and a prespecified analysis is the means of making the test honest.

Data sharing allows others to check the analysis and to combine results across studies. The raw traces and images, with the code used to analyse them, are the most useful form of report, and their absence makes independent verification difficult.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biology of the systems studied, and the report should state the features of the system that could affect generalisation, such as the age or the anaesthesia of the subjects.

The report of a neuroscience result is therefore a set of decisions made transparent, so that another investigator can judge whether the finding would be expected to replicate.
