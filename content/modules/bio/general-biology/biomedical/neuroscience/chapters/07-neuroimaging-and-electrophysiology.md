Electroencephalography records the summed electrical activity of large populations of neurons through electrodes on the scalp. The signal arises mainly from the synaptic currents of the pyramidal cells of the cortex, which are aligned so that their fields add together. The impulses of single neurons are too brief and too small to be seen at the scalp, so the signal reflects the synaptic activity of populations rather than their spikes. Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) described the membrane currents that generate the spike, and the scalp signal is the sum of many synaptic events that follow it.

Evoked potentials are measured by averaging the responses to repeated stimuli, so that the activity time-locked to the stimulus emerges from the background noise. The latency and the amplitude of the averaged response are used clinically to test the integrity of sensory pathways. The method has excellent time resolution, measured in milliseconds, but its spatial resolution is limited, because the source of the signal must be inferred from the pattern on the scalp.

Functional magnetic resonance imaging measures changes in blood oxygenation that follow neural activity, and it has good spatial resolution but poor time resolution, since the haemodynamic response unfolds over seconds. The signal is an indirect measure of activity, and the relationship between the blood change and the underlying neural activity is itself an assumption that must be tested.

Reporting that a region was active or altered is an inference from these signals. A region that shows a larger signal in one condition may be engaged, or may be receiving a change in input, or may be responding to a difference in attention. Ioannidis (2005) described how the statistical treatment of imaging data offers many choices that can produce a significant result, and the analysis must be fixed and corrected for multiple comparisons.

Platt (1964) recommended comparing the alternatives that a signal could support, and a claim about a region is more secure when a second method or a manipulation of the region supports it.

The signals of the living brain are indirect, and the inference from them to function requires the same care as any other measurement.
