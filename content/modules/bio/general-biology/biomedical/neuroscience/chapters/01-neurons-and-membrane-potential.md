A neuron at rest maintains a potential difference across its membrane, typically about minus seventy millivolts inside relative to outside. The potential arises from the unequal distribution of ions, with high potassium inside and high sodium outside, and from the selective permeability of the membrane, which at rest is far more permeable to potassium. The sodium-potassium pump uses ATP to maintain the gradients. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the pump and the channels that set this potential, and the balance of the ion currents determines where the membrane sits at rest.

An action potential is a brief, large change in this potential. A depolarising stimulus opens voltage-gated sodium channels, and the rapid influx of sodium drives the membrane towards the sodium equilibrium potential. The channels then inactivate, and voltage-gated potassium channels open, so that potassium leaves the cell and the membrane repolarises. Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) measured these currents in the squid giant axon and showed that the action potential can be described by the separate conductances of sodium and potassium, each changing with voltage and time.

Their model explained propagation as well as the shape of the spike. Each region of the axon, depolarised by the spike, triggers the next region, so the impulse travels along the axon without loss of amplitude. The speed of propagation depends on the diameter of the axon and on the myelin that insulates it, which reduces the capacitance that must be charged at each step.

The equations describe the squid axon accurately, but the models of other neurons must be fitted to their own measurements. Ioannidis (2005) described how a model that fits one preparation can be taken as a general law, and the generalisation to other cells should be tested rather than assumed.

The electrical signal of the neuron is the basis of its communication, and each impulse is an event that the next chapter follows across the synapse.
