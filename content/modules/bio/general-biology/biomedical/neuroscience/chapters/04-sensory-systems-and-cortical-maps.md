A sensory neuron responds only to stimuli within a limited region of the sensory surface, called its receptive field. Hubel and Wiesel (1962) recorded from single neurons in the visual cortex of the cat while presenting bars and edges of light. They found that the cells responded best to a line of a particular orientation within the field, and that cells were arranged in columns in which orientation changed in a regular order across the cortex. They also found that the cells received inputs from both eyes, and that neighbouring cells tended to prefer the same eye, forming stripes of ocular dominance.

The organisation builds a map of the visual field, in which neighbouring locations in the world are represented by neighbouring cells in the cortex. The map is not a picture of the retina, because the magnification of the fovea is large, and the cortex devotes more area to the centre of the visual field than to its periphery. The maps of other senses have similar features, with the body surface represented across the somatosensory cortex in proportion to the density of its receptors.

The organisation depends on experience during development. Deprivation of input to one eye in early life shifts the ocular dominance of the cortex towards the open eye, and the effect is strongest during a critical period. The map is therefore an adaptation to the sensory environment as well as a fixed design, and this is why early visual deprivation can cause permanent loss of vision in one eye.

The methods used to map receptive fields in humans are indirect. Functional imaging and the recording of evoked potentials reveal activity in regions, but not the receptive fields of single cells. Ioannidis (2005) described how such indirect measures can be interpreted more precisely than they support, and Platt (1964) recommended comparing the maps with alternative explanations of the response.

The organisation of the sensory cortex is a result of both development and use, and its disruption is one of the clearest ways in which neural circuits show their dependence on experience.
