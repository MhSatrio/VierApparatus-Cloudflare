Bliss and Lømo (1973) stimulated the perforant path to the dentate gyrus of anaesthetised rabbits with brief bursts of high-frequency pulses and recorded a potentiation of the response that lasted for hours. The synapses had become stronger, so that the same input produced a larger output. This long-term potentiation has since been studied in the hippocampus and in many other regions, and it is the most widely studied model of the cellular basis of memory.

Induction depends on the pattern of activity. The strong and sustained depolarisation of the postsynaptic cell removes the magnesium that blocks the NMDA receptor, and the calcium that enters through the receptor activates kinases that strengthen the synapse. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling by which calcium activates such enzymes, and the induction is a chain of events that depends on the timing of the inputs as well as their number.

The expression of potentiation can involve an increase in the response of the receptors already present, or the insertion of more receptors into the membrane, or changes in the release of transmitter. Which mechanism operates in a given synapse and at a given time is an open question, and the answer may differ between the early and late phases of the change.

The link to memory is an inference. A synaptic change that is induced by a learning-like pattern and that lasts for a time similar to the memory is consistent with a role in memory, but it does not show that the change stores the memory. Platt (1964) argued that such a claim is strengthened by an experiment that could show it false, and the test is whether blocking the change prevents the memory without affecting other functions.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the appeal of a mechanism can lead to its acceptance before its alternatives are excluded, and the case for synaptic plasticity as the basis of memory should be read with that caution.

The strength of a synapse can change with use, and the question of whether such change is what memory is remains a matter for evidence rather than assumption.
