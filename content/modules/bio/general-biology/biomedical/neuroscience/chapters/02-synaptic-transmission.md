When an action potential reaches the terminal of an axon, it opens voltage-gated calcium channels in the membrane. Calcium enters and binds to proteins that trigger the fusion of synaptic vesicles with the membrane, releasing their neurotransmitter into the cleft between the cells. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the vesicle cycle and the proteins that dock and fuse the vesicles, and the release is rapid because the vesicles are already primed at the active zone. The amount of calcium that enters determines how many vesicles fuse, which is why the strength of a synapse depends on the calcium at its terminal.

The transmitter diffuses across the cleft and binds to receptors on the postsynaptic cell. Ionotropic receptors are ion channels that open when the transmitter binds, so the response is fast, within milliseconds. Metabotropic receptors act through G proteins and second messengers, and their responses are slower and longer lasting. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the function of a protein depends on its structure, and receptor binding and channel gating both depend on the precise folding of these proteins.

The transmitter is removed from the cleft by reuptake into the terminal or by enzymatic breakdown, and the speed of removal shapes the duration of the signal. Drugs that block reuptake or the enzyme prolong the transmitter's action, and this is the basis of many treatments in neurology and psychiatry.

The evidence for a particular transmitter in a particular synapse depends on showing that the receptor and the release are the cause of the observed response. Ioannidis (2005) described how a single pharmacological experiment can suggest a mechanism that is not confirmed by genetic or specific tests, and the mechanism should be established by more than one method.

The synapse converts an electrical signal into a chemical one and back into electricity, and the changes in this conversion are the basis of the plasticity discussed in the next chapter.
