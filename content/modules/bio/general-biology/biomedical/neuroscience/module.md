# Neuroscience

The Membrane Biophysics and Cytoskeleton topics described the physical basis of excitable membranes and the structures of the cell, and the Cell Communication module described how signals pass between cells. Neuroscience applies these principles to the nervous system, which is the most complex tissue in the body. This topic moves from the single neuron to the networks it forms, and then to the diseases that disturb them and the evidence on which their diagnosis rests.

The chapters begin with the generation of the nerve impulse, first described quantitatively by Hodgkin and Huxley (1952). Synaptic transmission and its change with experience follow, from the first demonstration of long-lasting potentiation by Bliss and Lømo (1973). Sensory systems and the organisation of cortical maps are then described, based on the receptive fields that Hubel and Wiesel (1962) mapped in the visual cortex. The later chapters address neurodegeneration, neuroinflammation and the methods by which brain function is measured in patients and in research.

The brain is studied in a field where many findings are difficult to replicate, and the animal models and small human samples often produce results that do not generalise. Ioannidis (2005) described how such problems arise, and Platt (1964) recommended experiments that discriminate between the explanations of a finding. The chapters emphasise the control of these problems in the design of studies.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell biology of neurons and glia, on which each of the systems described depends.
