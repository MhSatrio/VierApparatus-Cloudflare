# Translational Medicine

Translational medicine is the discipline that carries findings from the laboratory bench to the bedside. Much of the biology in this curriculum, from the signalling of the Cell Communication topic to the pathways of the Oncology and Cardiovascular modules, has been established in cells and animals, and its clinical value depends on crossing the gap between those systems and patients. The gap is wide, and most promising laboratory findings never become useful treatments. Translational medicine studies why this happens and how the crossing can be made more reliable.

The chapters follow the path from discovery to practice. The translational pathway is described first, with the stages at which a finding must prove itself. The loss of promising findings, which Contopoulos-Ioannidis et al. (2003) documented, is then treated as the central problem. Animal models, biomarkers, the phases of clinical trials and regulatory approval follow, and the final chapters address the relations between academia and industry and the measures by which translation is judged to have succeeded.

Contopoulos-Ioannidis et al. (2003) showed that many highly cited basic findings were not followed by clinical application, and that the reasons include the reliance on small and early studies. Ioannidis (2005) described how the statistical and design problems of early research propagate into translation, and Platt (1964) recommended the experiments that test a hypothesis against its alternatives at each stage.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biology that a translational finding rests upon, and a finding is only as secure as the mechanism that connects the molecule to the disease.
