Animal models are used to test an intervention before it is given to people, because they allow the effect to be measured in a living system and at doses and durations that would not be ethical in humans. The model is a representation of the disease, and its value depends on how closely it reproduces the features of the human condition that the treatment must affect. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the physiology that is shared between species, and the similarity of a pathway in two species does not guarantee that a treatment will act the same way in both.

Models differ from human disease in many ways. Many are induced by a single intervention, whereas human disease usually develops over decades with several causes. The lifespan of a mouse is short, and its immune system and metabolism differ in important respects. Hanahan and Weinberg (2000) described the complexity of cancer, which develops through many steps and in an environment that a transplanted tumour in a mouse does not reproduce. A treatment that acts on the simple model may not act on the complex disease.

The predictive value of a model is measured by its performance across many interventions. A model that correctly identifies the treatments that succeed and fail in patients has high predictive value, and one that does not has little. The measures must be made against the human outcome, and a model that is validated only against the expectations of the investigators is not validated. The failure of animal results to translate is one of the recognised causes of the translational gap.

Ioannidis (2005) described how an effect that is found in a small number of animals may not be reproducible, and the power and the randomisation of animal studies must be reported. Platt (1964) recommended experiments that could distinguish between alternatives, and the comparison of a treatment in several models of the same disease is a test of whether the effect is robust.

Animal models are useful tools, and their value depends on knowing how far their predictions have been shown to reach human patients.
