Translation depends on the cooperation of universities, which generate basic findings, and of companies, which have the resources to develop them into products. The partnership brings complementary skills, and many of the treatments in use began with academic discoveries that a company developed. The collaboration also creates risk, because a company that funds a study has a financial interest in its outcome, and that interest can influence the design, the analysis and the reporting of the study in ways that are not always deliberate.

Ioannidis (2005) described how financial and other interests can bias research, and the bias can operate through the choice of comparator, the selection of endpoints, the analysis of subgroups and the decision to publish. The translation of basic findings depends on the quality of the evidence that sponsors generate, and a sponsor's interest can lead to evidence that overstates benefit or understates harm.

The influence can be subtle. An investigator who depends on a sponsor for funding may be reluctant to report an unfavourable result, and a sponsor may influence the choice of an active comparator that makes its product appear superior. Platt (1964) recommended comparisons with clear alternatives, and the choice of a comparator that is not the best available is itself a form of bias that a reader should look for.

Safeguards reduce the risk. Financial interests must be disclosed, the sponsor's role in the design and analysis must be stated, and the investigators must retain control of the data and the decision to publish. Trials should be registered before they begin, so that the outcomes and the analysis plan are fixed and any change can be seen. The publication of all trials, including negative ones, prevents selective reporting.

The partnership is valuable when these safeguards are in place, and it is hazardous when the evidence is shaped by the interest that funds it. The judgement of a partnership's output must therefore consider who designed the study, who analysed it and who decided what to report.

Collaboration is essential to translation, and its benefits depend on transparency that allows the evidence to be judged independently of the interests that produced it.
