Regulators decide whether an intervention may be marketed by weighing its benefit against its risk, on the evidence presented by the developer. The benefit must be shown in a way that is clinically meaningful, and the risk must be characterised well enough to be judged acceptable for the intended population. Platt (1964) described the value of a comparison that can discriminate between alternatives, and the regulatory decision is strongest when it rests on randomised comparisons rather than on uncontrolled observations. The decision also depends on the severity of the disease, since a serious condition without other treatment may justify accepting greater uncertainty.

Approval is sometimes granted on incomplete evidence, for example on a surrogate endpoint, with the requirement that the benefit be confirmed later. Such conditional approval is a judgement that the expected benefit justifies early access, balanced against the risk that the benefit will not be confirmed. Early promising results have often not been sustained, and conditional approval places the burden of confirmation on the later study, which must actually be performed and reported.

Post-marketing studies follow approval. They examine the intervention in populations larger and more varied than the trials, and they detect adverse events that are too rare to appear in the trials. Ioannidis (2005) described how the evidence for a rare harm requires a large population to be detected, and the post-marketing system must be designed with the statistical power to find the harms it is meant to detect.

Post-marketing surveillance also has limits. Spontaneous reporting, the main source of signals, lacks a denominator and a comparison group, and a rise in reports may reflect changes in use or awareness rather than a change in risk. Studies that compare the treated population with a control group, using registries or linked records, are more informative.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biology of the intervention and its targets, which informs the expectation of which adverse effects are likely. The post-marketing study should test both the expected and the unexpected.

Approval is the beginning of the evidence on an intervention rather than its end, and post-marketing study is the means by which the early judgement is checked against real use.
