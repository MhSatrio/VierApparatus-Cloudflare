A biomarker is a measurable quantity that indicates a biological state, whether a disease, a response to treatment or a risk. Biomarkers are attractive in translation because they can be measured quickly and in many people, and they can show whether a drug has reached its target. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecules whose levels change with disease, and a biomarker is often a molecule of this kind. The usefulness of a biomarker depends on what it represents, and that must be established before it is used.

A biomarker that reflects the disease or the drug action shows that a process is occurring. It may be informative in a research study, but it does not show that changing the biomarker changes the outcome for the patient. A surrogate endpoint is a biomarker that is accepted as a substitute for a clinical outcome, such as survival or the prevention of symptoms, and its acceptance requires evidence that the biomarker predicts the outcome and that a treatment which changes it also changes the outcome.

Biomarkers expected to predict benefit have often failed to do so, and the translational failures in several fields have followed the use of surrogates whose link to the outcome was assumed rather than shown. Ioannidis (2005) described how a marker that correlates with an outcome in one population may not predict it in another, and the correlation must be shown in the population in which the marker will be used.

Validation of a surrogate requires that the treatment effect on the marker predicts the treatment effect on the outcome. A marker can be associated with the outcome in observational data and still fail to capture the effect of a treatment, because the treatment may act through another pathway. Platt (1964) recommended testing the alternatives, and the comparison of the treatment effect on the marker with the effect on the clinical outcome in trials is the test of whether the marker can be trusted as a surrogate.

The use of a biomarker must be justified by what it shows and by the decision it changes. A marker that is measured but does not alter treatment adds cost and noise without benefit.

A biomarker is a bridge between laboratory and clinic only if it has been shown to cross to the clinical outcome, and this must be tested rather than assumed.
