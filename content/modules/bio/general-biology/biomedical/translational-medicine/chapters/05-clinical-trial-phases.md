Clinical development proceeds through phases of increasing scale, and each phase answers a different question. Phase one studies are small, often in healthy volunteers, and they establish the safety, tolerability and pharmacokinetics of the intervention at increasing doses. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the pharmacological processes that these studies measure, and the dose that is safe in volunteers is the starting point for the study of patients. Phase two studies are larger and are performed in patients, and they explore the dose and the early evidence of effect. Their sample sizes are too small to establish benefit reliably.

Phase three trials are the decisive comparison. They are randomised, controlled and sized to detect a clinically meaningful difference, and they measure outcomes that matter to patients. Platt (1964) described the strong inference that such a comparison provides, since the trial can show the intervention to be no better than the control. The decision at the end of phase three is whether the benefit is large enough, reliable enough and safe enough to justify approval, and this decision is not made on the basis of phase two results.

Phase four, or post-marketing study, follows approval. It examines the effects of the intervention in larger and more varied populations, detects rare adverse events and tests use in groups that were excluded from the trials. Many early promising results have not been sustained, and the post-marketing phase is a means of detecting this after approval.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the decision to advance a project from an early phase can be made on the strength of a small favourable result, and the criteria for advancing should be set in advance and based on the question each phase must answer. The sample size of each phase should be justified by the effect it must detect, and not chosen for convenience.

Each phase is a test that could fail, and the sequence protects patients by stopping the project when the evidence does not support it.
