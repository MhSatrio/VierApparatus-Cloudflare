The translational pathway begins with the identification of a target, a molecule whose modification is expected to change the course of a disease. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling and metabolic networks in which such targets lie, and the choice of target depends on the evidence that it is causally involved rather than merely associated with the disease. Target validation follows, in which the effect of changing the target is tested in cells and in animal models, and this step establishes whether the target is a plausible point of intervention.

A candidate compound or intervention is then developed and tested for its properties, its safety and its effect in the models that represent the disease. Contopoulos-Ioannidis et al. (2003) documented that many findings promising in early work were not carried through the pathway. The pathway is a sequence of decisions, and each decision should rest on evidence that could have shown the project to be unpromising.

At each stage there is a point at which the project should be stopped if the evidence does not support it. Stopping is a valuable outcome, because it prevents resources and patients from being committed to an intervention that would not work. Platt (1964) recommended experiments that discriminate between alternatives, and the decision to advance is strongest when the experiment at each stage could have shown the project to fail.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the bias of early positive results can carry a project through several stages before its weakness is discovered. The safeguard is to require independent replication and a prespecified criterion for advancing, so that a decision is not made on the strength of a single favourable result.

The pathway therefore moves from the molecule to the patient through a series of tests, and the quality of each test determines whether the final intervention is justified.
