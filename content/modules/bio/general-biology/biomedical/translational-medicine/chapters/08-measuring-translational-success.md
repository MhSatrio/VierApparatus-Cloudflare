Translation is a process whose success is hard to measure, and the measures chosen shape the behaviour of the field. The simplest measure is the proportion of basic findings that reach clinical use, which Contopoulos-Ioannidis et al. (2003) used to describe how many influential findings were translated. The measure is informative, but it depends on how a translated finding is defined, and a finding that is adopted without benefit would count as success under a loose definition.

A more meaningful measure is the benefit that reaches patients. This includes the improvement in survival, in symptoms or in quality of life that a translated treatment provides, and it is measured in trials and in practice. The benefit must be weighed against the cost of the treatment and the harms it causes, and a treatment that is expensive and offers little gain is not a success merely because it reached clinic.

Counts of publications and patents are poor measures. They reward activity rather than outcome, and they can increase without any change in benefit to patients. A field that is judged by its output of papers may produce many findings that are not translated, because the incentive is to publish rather than to test. Ioannidis (2005) described how incentives that reward novelty and positive results distort the evidence, and the choice of a measure of success shapes which evidence is produced.

Evaluations of translational programmes must test whether they improve health. The evaluation compares the health of a population exposed to the programme with that of a population not exposed, or with an earlier period, and it must account for other changes. Platt (1964) recommended comparisons that could show the programme to fail, and an evaluation that can detect no effect is as informative as one that detects an effect.

The measure of translation should therefore be the benefit delivered, reliably and at acceptable cost, and the processes that produce it should be judged by how well they achieve that.

Translational success is a change in patients' health, and the measures that claim to capture it must be tested against that change.
