Carcinogenesis is a multistep process, and many chemicals contribute to it at different steps. An initiating carcinogen causes a mutation in a cell, which by itself is usually insufficient to produce a tumour. A promoting agent stimulates the division of initiated cells, and the cells that carry the mutation expand into a clone. Hanahan and Weinberg (2000) described the capabilities that a cell must acquire to become a tumour, and an initiating mutation is one step towards them, while promotion gives the initiated cell the opportunity to accumulate further changes.

Carcinogenicity is tested in long-term studies in rodents, in which the animals are exposed to a chemical for much of their lives and the incidence of tumours is compared with that in control animals. The studies are costly and slow, and they use high doses to detect effects in a limited number of animals. The dose at which the tumours arise is usually far above human exposure, and the extrapolation to people requires assumptions about the dose-response relation and about the similarity of the metabolism.

Epidemiology examines the cancer rates in people exposed to a chemical, such as workers in an industry. The evidence is directly relevant to people, but exposures are often mixed with others, and the estimates are subject to confounding and to the uncertainty of the exposure measurement. Ioannidis (2005) described how such studies can produce associations that are not confirmed, and the evidence must be weighed for its consistency across studies.

Classification of carcinogenicity draws on all the evidence, including the mutagenicity tests described in the previous chapter, the animal studies and the epidemiology. The strength of the classification depends on the consistency and quality of these lines, and a chemical may be classed as a probable carcinogen on partial evidence. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the explanations, and the mechanistic evidence, such as the demonstration that the chemical forms the adducts that cause the relevant mutations, strengthens the classification.

Extrapolation from animals to people is the largest uncertainty. Differences in metabolism, in the duration of exposure and in the immune response all affect the risk, and the assumptions made must be stated.

The classification of a carcinogen is a judgement based on several lines of evidence, and its strength depends on how well those lines agree.
