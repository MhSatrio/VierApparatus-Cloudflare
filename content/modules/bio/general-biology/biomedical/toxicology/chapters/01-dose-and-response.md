The principle that the dose makes the poison, first stated by Paracelsus in the sixteenth century, remains the foundation of toxicology. Almost any substance is harmless below some dose and harmful above it, and the relation between dose and effect is described by a dose-response curve. The curve usually rises from no effect at low dose to a maximum at high dose, and its position and steepness describe the potency of the substance and the variability of the response in a population.

The threshold model holds that there is a dose below which no effect occurs. It applies to many effects that depend on a level of exposure being exceeded before the body's defences are overwhelmed, such as irritation or the depletion of a protective molecule. The non-threshold model holds that any dose carries some probability of harm, and it is applied to effects such as cancer that arise from a single damaging event in a cell. The choice between the models determines how risk is estimated at low exposure, and it is one of the most contested decisions in the field.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular defences, such as detoxifying enzymes and DNA repair, whose capacity sets the threshold for some effects. When these are overwhelmed, the harm begins, and the presence of a threshold depends on whether the defence can be saturated.

A dose-response relation measured at high dose cannot simply be extrapolated to low dose, because the mechanism may change, and the effects that are seen at high dose may not occur at all at low dose. Ioannidis (2005) described how an effect measured in one range can be assumed to hold in another, and the extrapolation must be justified by the mechanism and the data.

Platt (1964) recommended testing the alternative models directly, and the question of whether a threshold exists is answered by experiments at doses where the models predict different outcomes.

The dose-response curve is the basic summary of toxicity, and its shape at low dose is where the decisions about exposure are made.
