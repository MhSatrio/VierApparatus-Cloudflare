People are exposed to chemicals through the air they breathe, the water and food they consume, the products they use and the work they do. The dose that reaches the body depends on the concentration of the chemical in the medium, the rate of contact, the duration and the fraction absorbed through the route of exposure. Inhalation delivers chemicals to the lung, where absorption can be efficient, while ingestion passes them through the gut and the liver, which may metabolise them before they reach the general circulation. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the membranes and the epithelia that these routes cross, and the absorption depends on the properties of those barriers.

Environmental measurement estimates the concentration in air, water, soil or food. The measurement is an estimate of exposure, and it depends on where and when the sample was taken and on the behaviour of the people in the area. Many people are exposed to a mixture of chemicals, and the measurement of each one separately may miss the combined effect. The estimate of exposure is therefore uncertain, and the uncertainty should be stated with it.

Biomonitoring measures the chemical or its metabolite in the body, in blood or urine. It captures the dose that has actually been absorbed from all routes, and it avoids some of the errors of environmental estimates. It also has limits. The measured level reflects recent exposure for chemicals that are cleared quickly, and it reflects longer-term exposure for those that persist. A single measurement may not represent the usual exposure, and the interpretation depends on the half-life of the chemical.

Occupational exposure is usually higher than environmental exposure and better documented, and the workplace limits are set from it. The protection of workers depends on controlling the concentration at the source and on the use of protective equipment, and the effectiveness of these controls is measured by the same means as the exposure.

Ioannidis (2005) described how an exposure estimate can be treated as a precise dose when it carries wide uncertainty, and Platt (1964) recommended comparing the alternatives that could explain a health effect, such as exposure against other causes. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the activity of a protein depends on its structure, and the proteins that a chemical binds may be altered in ways that the exposure measure does not capture.

The dose at the target tissue is the quantity that matters for harm, and every measure of exposure is an estimate of it.
