The liver is the principal site of drug and chemical metabolism, and it is exposed to high concentrations of chemicals absorbed from the gut before they reach the rest of the body. The enzymes that activate toxicants are concentrated there, as described in the earlier chapter, and the reactive intermediates formed in the liver injure its cells. The pattern of injury often follows the zonation of the lobule, since the central zone contains the highest concentration of the activating enzymes. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the organisation of the liver and the distribution of its metabolic functions, and the zonal pattern follows from that organisation.

The kidney is vulnerable for a different reason. It filters the blood and concentrates the chemicals that are excreted, so that the tubular cells are exposed to high levels of the toxicant in the urine. Some chemicals are taken up actively by the tubule and accumulate there, and the proximal tubule, which reabsorbs most of the filtrate, is the commonest site of injury. The injury can impair the reabsorption and secretion of the tubule, and the loss of function is a late sign of damage.

The injury produces cell death, which may take the form of necrosis or apoptosis as Kerr et al. (1972) distinguished, and the form depends on the severity and the mechanism. Injury may be detected by the release of enzymes from damaged cells into the blood, and by the measure of the function the organ performs. Ioannidis (2005) described how a marker that rises after injury can be taken as an early sign, but many markers rise only after substantial damage has occurred.

The limits of these measures matter for detecting harm early. A rise in a liver enzyme indicates that cells have been damaged, but it does not indicate how much function is lost, and a normal value does not exclude damage that has not yet been released. The kidney's reserve is large, so that a considerable loss of function can occur before the standard measures change. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could discriminate between the possibilities, and combining several markers gives a more complete picture than any one.

The liver and the kidney show injury because they handle the chemical, and the measures of their damage must be read with an understanding of their reserve.
