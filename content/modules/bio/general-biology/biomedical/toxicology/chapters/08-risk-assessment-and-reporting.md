A risk assessment combines the hazard of a chemical, which is the intrinsic capacity to cause harm established by the dose-response studies, with the exposure of people, which is the dose they receive. The product is an estimate of the probability of harm in a population. For effects with a threshold, the assessment derives a level below which no harm is expected, usually by dividing the no-effect dose in animals by safety factors that allow for differences between species and between people. For cancer, the assessment estimates the excess risk at a given exposure, using a model extrapolated from the dose range studied, as discussed in the carcinogenicity chapter.

The safety factors are conventions as much as calculations. A factor of ten for differences between species and another of ten for variation between people are commonly applied, and they reflect the uncertainty of extrapolation rather than a measured quantity. Hanahan and Weinberg (2000) described the complexity of the process by which cancer arises, which makes the extrapolation of its risk uncertain, and the factors are a means of accommodating what the evidence cannot yet resolve.

Reporting must state the assumptions. The dose-response data used, the model fitted, the exposure estimate and its uncertainty, the safety factors applied and the population to which the estimate refers must all be given. A conclusion that a level is safe without these details cannot be checked or revised when new evidence appears. Ioannidis (2005) described how a conclusion that is presented without its assumptions is accepted more readily than it should be, and the report is the place where the assumptions are exposed.

Platt (1964) recommended testing the alternatives, and a risk assessment should consider how its conclusion would change under the alternative assumptions, such as a different model or a different safety factor. A conclusion that holds under all reasonable alternatives is more secure than one that depends on a single choice.

The limit of risk assessment is that it estimates a probability rather than determining an outcome, and its value for decisions depends on the honesty with which its uncertainties are stated.
