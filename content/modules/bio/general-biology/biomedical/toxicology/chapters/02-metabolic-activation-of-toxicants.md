Many chemicals are not toxic in their original form but become so after the body metabolises them. The enzymes of the liver, principally the cytochrome P450 family, oxidise chemicals into intermediates that may be electrophilic, meaning that they seek electrons and react with the nucleophilic groups of proteins and DNA. The reactive product is short-lived and acts close to where it is formed. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the enzymes of the endoplasmic reticulum that carry out these reactions, and their broad specificity explains why many structurally unrelated chemicals are activated by the same route.

Detoxification competes with activation. The reactive intermediate can be trapped by glutathione, a small molecule that binds electrophiles and carries them out of the cell for excretion. Epoxide hydrolases and other enzymes convert the intermediates into less reactive products. The toxicity of a chemical therefore depends on the balance between the activating and detoxifying routes, which varies between people, between organs and with the presence of other chemicals.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the function of a protein depends on its structure, and the reactive intermediates can alter the structure of the proteins they modify. A modified enzyme may lose activity, and a modified structural protein may lose its function. The consequences depend on which proteins are modified and how many.

A change in the balance can alter toxicity dramatically. Depletion of glutathione, by a dose of a chemical that is detoxified through it, allows the reactive intermediate to accumulate and damage the liver. An inducer of the activating enzymes can increase the production of the intermediate, while an inhibitor can reduce it. The interaction between chemicals is therefore a common cause of unexpected toxicity.

Ioannidis (2005) described how an interaction observed in one study may not hold in another, and the mechanism of activation should be demonstrated for each claimed interaction. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the explanations, such as measuring the reactive intermediate directly or measuring the depletion of the detoxifying molecule.

The metabolic balance decides whether a dose becomes harmful, and the same chemical may be harmless in one person and toxic in another.
