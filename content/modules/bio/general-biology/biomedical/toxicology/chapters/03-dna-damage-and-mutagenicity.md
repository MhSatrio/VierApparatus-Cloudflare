DNA is the target of many toxicants because its bases are chemically reactive at certain positions. Electrophilic metabolites form covalent adducts with the bases, and these adducts can distort the double helix that Watson and Crick (1953) described, so that the replication machinery misreads the template or stalls. Other chemicals and radiation break the strands directly. The cell has repair systems for each type of lesion, and most damage is corrected before it is copied. Alberts et al. (2022) describe these repair pathways, and the outcome depends on whether the repair is faster than the replication that would fix the lesion permanently.

A mutation arises when a lesion is copied incorrectly. A damaged base may pair with the wrong partner, so that the new strand carries a change, and the mutation is then passed to every descendant of the cell. Mutations in genes that control growth are the first steps in the development of cancer, and this link is the reason mutagenicity is used as a predictor of carcinogenicity, as discussed in the next chapter.

Mutagenicity tests detect the damage in simple organisms or in cells. The bacterial reverse mutation test, which uses strains of Salmonella that cannot synthesise an amino acid, detects whether a chemical causes mutations that restore the ability to grow without it. Because most chemicals are not mutagenic until metabolised, the test is performed with a liver extract that provides the metabolic activation. The tests are inexpensive and rapid, and they identify many carcinogens, but they do not reproduce the metabolism, the distribution or the repair of the human body.

The limits of these tests must be stated. A chemical that is positive in the test may not be carcinogenic in people at the doses they receive, and a chemical that is negative may still cause cancer by a mechanism the test does not detect. Ioannidis (2005) described how a test that is widely used can be treated as a definitive predictor when its performance has not been measured against the outcome it predicts, and the sensitivity and specificity of the test should be established.

Platt (1964) recommended testing the alternatives, and the comparison of the test result with the result in animals and with the data from exposed people discriminates between a true hazard and an artefact of the test.

The detection of DNA damage is a sensitive signal of hazard, and its interpretation depends on the chain of evidence that links it to harm in people.
