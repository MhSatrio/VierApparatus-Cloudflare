A toxicant can kill a cell by several routes. The most direct is the failure of energy supply, when the toxicant disrupts the mitochondria or the membrane pumps that the cell depends on. Without ATP, the ion gradients collapse, the cell swells and its membrane ruptures, which is necrosis. Other toxicants damage the membrane itself or the proteins within it, with the same result. These routes produce a rapid loss of integrity, and the dead cell releases its contents into the surrounding tissue, provoking inflammation. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the ion pumps and the mitochondrial production of ATP whose failure starts this sequence.

Other toxicants trigger programmed death. DNA damage can activate the pathways that remove a cell whose genome is compromised, and stress signals from the endoplasmic reticulum or the mitochondria can activate caspases. The cell shrinks, its chromatin condenses and it is removed by neighbouring cells without release of its contents, as Kerr et al. (1972) described for apoptosis. The programmed route is quieter and less inflammatory, and it can be a protective response that removes damaged cells before they transmit their damage.

The two routes can occur together, and a high dose can overwhelm the programmed pathway so that necrosis follows. The balance depends on the dose, the toxicant and the cell, and the pattern in tissue shows which route dominated. Some toxicants produce mixed patterns, with apoptotic cells at the margin of necrotic zones.

The mode of death shapes the outcome. Necrosis releases damage signals that recruit inflammatory cells, and the repair that follows can leave scarring. Apoptosis removes cells without inflammation, and the tissue may regenerate more completely. The distinction therefore matters for predicting whether a poisoned organ recovers or is left with permanent damage.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a marker of cell death may not distinguish the routes, and a claim about the mechanism needs a test that can separate them. Platt (1964) recommended such a test, for example the measurement of caspase activation, which discriminates programmed from non-programmed death.

The way a cell dies in a poisoning determines what the tissue becomes afterwards, and the mechanism is therefore part of the assessment of harm.
