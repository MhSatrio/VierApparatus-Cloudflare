# Toxicology

Toxicology asks how much of a substance is harmful, how the harm arises and how exposure can be limited. The Pharmacology module described the actions of drugs, and a drug is a toxicant at a high enough dose. The same principles of absorption, metabolism and target interaction apply to chemicals in food, water, air and the workplace. Toxicology adds the question of harm at the doses people actually receive, which is often far below those that cause visible effects in the laboratory.

The chapters begin with dose and response, which is the foundation of the discipline, and then describe how the body transforms chemicals into more or less toxic forms. Damage to DNA and the testing for mutations and cancer follow, together with the injury to organs and the cell death that results. The exposures people encounter and the assessment of risk from them are then treated, and the final chapter addresses how the evidence is reported.

The evidence in toxicology is often indirect, because it is not ethical to expose people to harmful doses, and it relies on animals, cells and observational studies of exposed populations. Ioannidis (2005) described how these sources can be misleading when they are combined without care, and Platt (1964) recommended studies that could discriminate between the explanations of an effect. The chapters emphasise the design of the evidence as much as its content.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell biology of the processes that toxicants disturb, and the mechanism of toxicity links the chemical to the injury.
