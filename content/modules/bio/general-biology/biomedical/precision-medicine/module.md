# Precision Medicine

For much of medicine, treatments were chosen for a disease as a whole, on the basis of the average response across many patients. Precision medicine proposes instead to match treatment to the biology of the individual's disease, using genomic, molecular and clinical information to define groups of patients who are likely to respond to a given intervention. Collins and Varmus (2015) described the initiative that brought this approach to national attention, and its promise rests on the observation that diseases with the same name often have different molecular causes.

The chapters follow the logic from the individual to the population. The move from average to individual treatment is described first, followed by the genomic stratification of disease, which builds on the cancer genome landscapes that Vogelstein et al. (2013) described. Trial designs that test treatments in biomarker-defined groups come next, then the integration of data across sources, the predictive models that link features to outcomes and their validation. The later chapters address equity of access and the evidence standards that a precision treatment must meet before it changes care.

The promise of precision medicine is easy to overstate, and many matched treatments have been promoted on the basis of molecular rationale that did not translate into benefit. Ioannidis (2005) described how an attractive mechanism can be accepted before it is tested, and Platt (1964) recommended trials that could show a matched treatment to be no better than the alternative. The chapters treat the design of the evidence as central to the claim.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular pathways that stratification relies on, and each stratum is defined by a pathway whose role in the disease must be shown.
