Precision medicine can widen existing inequalities in health if its benefits reach only some patients. The genomic and clinical data on which the field depends have been drawn largely from populations of European ancestry, and the allele frequencies, the variants and the responses observed in those populations do not transfer uniformly to others. The pharmacogenomic variants of drug metabolism, described in the pharmacogenomics module, differ in frequency between populations, and a test that works well for one group may misclassify another. Under-representation in the data is therefore a source of unequal benefit.

The consequences are concrete. A genetic test calibrated on one population may miss variants common in another, and a predictive model developed in one group may perform poorly in a different one, as the validation chapter described. Patients who are not represented in the data may receive a less accurate test or none at all, and this difference falls on the groups already least served by health care. Collins and Varmus (2015) described the aim of a cohort that includes diverse participants, and the aim is a means of correcting the imbalance.

Cost is a second barrier. Targeted tests and treatments can be expensive, and a matched drug may cost far more than the standard it replaces. The benefit of a precision treatment reaches only those who can obtain it, and a health system that funds the test but not the treatment, or that funds neither for some populations, produces unequal access even when the science is sound. The cost-effectiveness of each precision intervention must therefore be judged in the context of the population it will serve.

Study design can extend benefit. Recruitment strategies that reach under-represented groups, the collection of data across ancestries and the reporting of results by ancestry all make the evidence more applicable. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result from a narrow population may be presented as general, and the populations in which a finding has been tested must be stated.

Platt (1964) recommended comparisons that could reveal a difference between groups, and the analysis of a precision treatment by ancestry and by socioeconomic group is such a comparison, since it shows whether the benefit is shared.

Precision medicine is only as fair as the data and the access it rests on, and its benefit must be measured across the whole population it is meant to serve.
