Precision medicine depends on combining data that were collected for different purposes: the genome and transcriptome of a tumour, the clinical history of the patient, the results of imaging, the response to earlier treatments and the outcome over time. Each source has its own format, its own quality and its own reasons for being incomplete, and the combination is valuable only when the records can be linked reliably to the same patient. Collins and Varmus (2015) described the ambition to build such linked resources, and their value rests on the accuracy of the linkage.

Standards allow records to be combined. Common vocabularies for diagnoses and treatments, shared formats for molecular results and agreed definitions of outcomes allow data from different hospitals to be compared. Without standards, the same disease is recorded in many ways, and a comparison across records reflects the recording as much as the biology. The adoption of standards is a practical step with large consequences for the reliability of any analysis.

Missing data are a serious problem. Patients are tested for some markers and not others, follow-up is incomplete and outcomes are recorded more often for some groups than others. The missingness is rarely random, and a conclusion drawn from the patients who have complete records may not apply to those who do not. Ioannidis (2005) described how selection of the patients with complete data can introduce bias into an association, and the pattern of missing data must be analysed before the results are interpreted.

Integration also creates the risk of spurious findings. With many variables and many patients, the search for associations produces apparent relationships by chance, and the analysis must be prespecified and validated in data that were not used to find the relationships. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could show the relationship to be false, and the validation of an association in an independent set of records is such a test.

Privacy and consent are central. The linkage of records increases the risk that a patient can be identified, and the uses of the data must be consistent with the consent given. Collins and Varmus (2015) recognised that the public trust on which such resources depend requires that these safeguards be strong.

The integration of data is the foundation of precision medicine, and its reliability depends on standards, complete records, careful analysis and the protection of the people whose data make it possible.
