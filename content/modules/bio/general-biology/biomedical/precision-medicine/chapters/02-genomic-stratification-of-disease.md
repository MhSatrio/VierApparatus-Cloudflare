Stratification divides a disease into groups defined by molecular features. In cancer, the features may be mutations in particular genes, changes in copy number or patterns of expression, and each divides the tumours of a tissue into subgroups that differ in biology. Vogelstein et al. (2013) described the genetic landscape from which such features are drawn, and the most useful stratifiers are the changes that are common enough to define a sizeable group and that act on a pathway that a drug can influence.

A feature becomes a stratifier only when it is shown to predict response to a treatment, not merely to be present in the tumour. A mutation may be frequent and yet not determine whether a drug works, and the stratifier must be validated in patients who received the treatment. The distinction between a feature that describes the tumour and one that predicts its response is central, and the earlier chapters on pharmacogenomics made the same distinction for genotypes.

Stratification is refined as sequencing reveals new subgroups. A class of tumours defined by one feature may be found to contain subgroups defined by another, and the refinement can make the groups more homogeneous and the predictions more accurate. Each refinement creates new groups that are smaller, and the statistical power to show that a treatment works in each falls. Ioannidis (2005) described how increasingly fine divisions of a sample produce groups in which chance effects are large, and each refinement must be justified by its validated predictive value.

The stratification must also be stable. A feature that is measured in one laboratory must be measured in the same way elsewhere, and the thresholds that define a group must not depend on the particular sample used to set them. A stratifier that changes with the method of measurement or with the tissue sampled cannot reliably guide treatment.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling pathways whose activation the stratifiers reflect, and a stratifier that corresponds to a pathway the drug acts upon has a plausible basis for predicting response. Collins and Varmus (2015) described the initiative that made such stratification a national priority, and the validation of each stratifier is the step that determines whether the priority yields benefit.

Stratification divides patients into groups that the biology defines, and each division must earn its place by predicting response.
