A biomarker-defined trial enrols patients according to a molecular feature and tests a treatment matched to that feature. The design can take several forms, but the core comparison is the same: patients who carry the biomarker receive the matched treatment or the standard, and the outcomes are compared. Platt (1964) described the strong inference that such a comparison makes possible, since the result can show the matched treatment to be no better than the standard, and the design must allow for that outcome.

The biomarker and its threshold must be prespecified. The trial defines in advance which patients are biomarker-positive, how the marker is measured and what level counts as positive. A threshold chosen after the data are seen can be adjusted to produce a favourable result, and the prespecification prevents this. Collins and Varmus (2015) emphasised the need for rigorous evidence if precision medicine is to deliver its promise, and a prespecified definition of the group is a basic requirement.

Biomarker-negative patients must also be considered. A treatment matched to a marker may be ineffective, or even harmful, in patients who lack the marker, and a trial that enrols only positive patients cannot show this. Some designs enrol both groups and test the treatment in each, which reveals whether the marker is truly predictive, meaning that it separates responders from non-responders, rather than merely prognostic, meaning that it predicts outcome regardless of treatment.

Ioannidis (2005) described how subgroups defined after the results are known can be found with apparent benefit by chance, since the number of possible subgroups is large. The claim of benefit in a subgroup is credible only when the subgroup was prespecified and the trial was powered to detect the effect within it. A subgroup finding that was not prespecified is a hypothesis for a new trial.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the pathway that the biomarker reflects, and the mechanistic rationale supports the choice of the marker, although it does not replace the trial.

The biomarker-defined trial tests whether a matched treatment works in the group the marker defines, and the group must be fixed before the test is run.
