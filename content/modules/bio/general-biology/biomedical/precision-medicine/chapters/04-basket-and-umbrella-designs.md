A basket trial tests one treatment in patients who have a common molecular feature, even though their tumours arise in different tissues. The design follows the logic of the shared genetic change, which Vogelstein et al. (2013) showed can recur across cancer types. The treatment is evaluated within each basket, and the results may differ between baskets, since the same molecular change can act differently depending on the tissue in which it occurs. The design is efficient for rare features, because many tissues contribute patients to a single trial.

An umbrella trial tests several treatments within one disease, with each patient assigned to the treatment matched to a subgroup. The subgroups are defined by their biomarkers, and the structure allows many hypotheses to be tested under one protocol. The umbrella design is efficient for diseases with many molecular subtypes, but each treatment is tested in a smaller group, and the power to detect an effect in each is limited.

The shared problem of these designs is borrowing. Evidence from one basket or subgroup is used to inform the decision in another, on the assumption that the molecular feature acts in the same way across them. The assumption may be false, and a treatment that works in one tissue may fail in another with the same feature. Platt (1964) recommended testing the alternative explanations, and the response of a feature in each tissue is the test of whether borrowing is justified.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the combination of many small groups increases the chance that one of them will show an apparent benefit by chance, and the error rates must be controlled across the baskets or subgroups. A result in a single basket with a small number of patients is a signal to be followed up, not an established benefit.

The separation of the effect of the treatment from that of the tissue requires a comparison within each tissue, and the trial must be designed to make that comparison possible.

Basket and umbrella designs extend the logic of matching across diseases and subgroups, and their claims require the same care as any other trial of a subgroup.
