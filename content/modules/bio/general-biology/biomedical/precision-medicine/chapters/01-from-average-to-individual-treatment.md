A clinical trial that reports an average treatment effect describes the mean response of a population, and the mean can conceal wide variation. A treatment that helps half of the patients substantially and does nothing for the rest may show a modest average benefit, and a treatment that harms a small group may be obscured by the benefit to the majority. The average is useful for policy but poor for an individual deciding on treatment, since the individual wants to know which group they belong to. Collins and Varmus (2015) argued that the aim of precision medicine is to find the groups in which a treatment works and to identify the people who belong to them.

The same clinical diagnosis can arise from different biological causes. A cancer named by its tissue of origin is a set of tumours with different genetic changes, as Vogelstein et al. (2013) showed, and the changes determine which therapies act on the tumour. Two patients with the same diagnosis may therefore need different treatments, and a trial that pools them will dilute the effect in the subgroup that responds. The variation within a diagnosis is the source of the opportunity that precision medicine seeks to use.

The move to individual treatment changes the question a trial must answer. A trial of an average effect asks whether the treatment works in the population. A trial of a matched treatment asks whether the treatment works in patients who carry the biomarker, and whether patients without the biomarker are better treated otherwise. Both questions require a control group and a prespecified analysis, and the subgroup must be defined before the results are seen.

Ioannidis (2005) described how subgroup analyses that are performed after the results are known can find an apparent benefit in any group chosen by chance, and the danger is greatest when the subgroups are many and small. Platt (1964) recommended a design in which the hypothesis about the subgroup could be tested against an alternative, and the prespecified subgroup trial is such a design.

Individual treatment is the aim, and the evidence needed to justify it is a comparison within the groups the biology defines.
