A precision treatment claims that a biomarker identifies the patients who will benefit from a matched therapy. The evidence for the claim has three parts, and each must be present before the treatment changes care. The first is the validation of the biomarker: it must be measured reliably, its threshold must be fixed and it must identify a group whose response differs from the rest. The second is the demonstration that the matched treatment improves outcome within the group, in a comparison that the design has prespecified. The third is the comparison with the alternatives, showing that the matched treatment is better than the standard for the patients who carry the marker.

Evans and Relling (1999) argued that a genetic test is valuable only when it changes a decision in a way that improves the outcome, and the three parts of the evidence follow from that principle. A biomarker that is measured and validated but does not change the treatment, or a treatment that is better than nothing but not better than the standard, does not justify the precision claim.

The standards are often not met, and the gaps can be identified. A claim may rest on a biomarker whose validation was performed in a single laboratory, on a trial in which the subgroup was defined after the results, on an uncontrolled series of patients or on a comparison with no treatment. Ioannidis (2005) described how such gaps allow an attractive mechanism to be accepted as an established benefit, and the judgement of a precision claim consists in finding the missing parts.

Collins and Varmus (2015) described precision medicine as a means of improving the care of patients, and the standards are the means of checking whether the improvement is real. A treatment that is precisely matched but not shown to be better is an intervention with a cost and a risk and no proven benefit.

Platt (1964) recommended a test that could show the claim to be wrong, and the trial of the matched treatment against the standard in the marker-defined group is the means of showing whether it is.

A precision treatment is justified when the marker, the group and the benefit have each been shown, and the claim must be judged by the evidence for all three.
