Open science is the practice of making research transparent at every stage, so that its claims can be checked, reused and built upon. It covers the publication of results without barriers, the sharing of data, code and materials, the registration of plans before data are collected and the visibility of the review that evaluates the work. Munafò et al. (2017) set out these practices as a manifesto for reproducible science, and they argued that openness is the means by which a scientific community corrects its own errors.

The sharing of data and code is central to open science. Data are shared with persistent identifiers and licences that state the conditions of reuse, and code is deposited in a form that others can run. Wilkinson et al. (2016) described the principles that make such sharing useful, and Peng (2011) argued that the availability of the computational steps is what makes a result reproducible rather than merely described.

Pre-registration and the registered report separate the planning of a study from the interpretation of its results. Preprints make findings available before formal review, so that they can be scrutinised early, and open peer review makes the evaluation visible so that readers can see how a claim was judged. Ioannidis (2005) described how the hidden selection of results distorts the literature, and these practices reduce the scope for such selection.

Openness has legitimate limits. Consent, privacy and safety may restrict what can be released, and the restriction must be justified and as narrow as possible. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that restriction is an exception to be explained, not a reason to withhold the methods, and the limits should be stated openly so that readers understand what has been withheld and why. Open science is strengthened when incentives reward it, and institutions that value transparency encourage researchers to practise it.
