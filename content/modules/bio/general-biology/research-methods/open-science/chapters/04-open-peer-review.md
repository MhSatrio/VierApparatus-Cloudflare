Peer review is the evaluation of research before publication, and it is usually hidden: the reviewers' identities and their comments are known only to the editor and the authors. Open peer review makes some or all of this process visible. The reviewers' reports may be published with the article, their names may be attached to them, or the review may continue after publication, so that readers can see how the claim was judged and how the authors responded.

Open identities make reviewers accountable for their comments, since a reviewer who signs a report must stand behind it. Open reports allow readers to see the strengths and weaknesses that the reviewers identified, and to judge whether the concerns were adequately addressed. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that a public record of evaluation improves trust in published work, because the basis of acceptance can be examined.

Named reviewers may be less candid, particularly when the review concerns a senior colleague or a competitor. Protections such as anonymity of the report with identity known to the editor, or the separation of technical comments from general opinion, address this risk. Ioannidis (2005) described how the social dynamics of review can shape which findings are accepted, and the design of the review should limit these influences.

The registered report, discussed elsewhere in this module, is a form of open review in which the journal publishes its decision on the question and the method before the results exist. This separates the judgement of the design from the judgement of the findings. Cumming (2014) argues that a review should attend to the precision of the claims and the adequacy of their intervals, and open reports allow readers to see whether this attention was given.

Post-publication review continues evaluation after an article appears, through public comments, replication attempts and reanalysis. It recognises that a single review cannot establish the reliability of a finding, and the evaluation of a claim accumulates over time. Reviewers need incentives to take part in open review, since the work is often unrewarded, and institutions that recognise it encourage the practice.
