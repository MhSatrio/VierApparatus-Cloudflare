Open data are the most visible part of open science, and they are useful only when they can be found, understood and reused. A dataset deposited with a persistent identifier and a licence can be cited and retrieved, and the metadata and data dictionary explain what each value means. Wilkinson et al. (2016) described how these features make data findable and reusable, and a dataset without them is effectively closed, however freely it is posted.

Biological materials, such as strains, cell lines, plasmids and antibodies, are shared through repositories and collections that verify their identity and distribute them to researchers. A material described only in a paper cannot easily be obtained, and the loss of a strain can end the ability to repeat a study. Peng (2011) argued that the means of reproducing a result must be available to those who wish to check it, and repositories are the practical channel for this.

Detailed protocols should be deposited with identifiers so that a method can be followed exactly. A protocol that gives only a summary leaves gaps that each reader fills differently, and the differences can change the result. The deposited protocol should include the reagents, their lot numbers, the equipment settings and the steps in order, with any deviation from the published method stated.

The transfer of materials between institutions is governed by agreements that protect the interests of the creators and the recipients. These agreements may restrict commercial use or require acknowledgement, and they should be stated when the material is shared. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that openness is compatible with such agreements, but they should be designed to permit verification and reuse, not to prevent them. Openness must also be balanced against consent, privacy and safety, as described in the other chapters.
