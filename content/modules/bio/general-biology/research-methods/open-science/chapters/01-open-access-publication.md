Open access makes a research article available to any reader without a subscription or a payment. The gold route publishes the article openly in the journal itself, usually with a fee paid by the author or the funder. The green route allows the author to deposit a version of the article in a repository, often after an embargo set by the publisher. Both routes make the work available, but they differ in cost, in the version that is available and in the conditions under which readers may reuse it.

The licence of an article determines what readers may do with it. An open licence that permits reuse with attribution allows the article to be copied, analysed and built upon, whereas an article with no licence can be read but not reused freely. Wilkinson et al. (2016) argued that the reusability of research outputs depends on clear conditions of reuse, and the licence is the means of stating them.

Fee models determine who bears the cost of publication. Where the author or the funder pays, researchers without funding may be excluded from publishing openly, and the cost may influence the choice of venue. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the costs of openness should not fall unfairly on some researchers, and institutions should consider how these costs are shared.

Restricted access excludes readers who cannot afford a subscription, including clinicians, teachers, scientists in lower-income countries and the public who funded the work. Ioannidis (2005) described how the failure of evidence to reach those who could use it weakens the field, and open access is one remedy for this failure. Funder and institutional mandates increasingly require open access, and authors should retain the rights needed to deposit and reuse their own work.
