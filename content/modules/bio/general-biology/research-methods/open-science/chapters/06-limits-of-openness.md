Openness is a strong default, but it is not an absolute rule. Participants who consented to a study under particular terms are owed those terms, and the privacy of individuals must be protected even when the scientific value of their data is high. Some methods could be misused to cause harm, and some collaborations involve commercial interests that legitimately constrain release. The principle of openness must be applied with these limits in view, rather than in ignorance of them.

Consent is the first limit. The terms under which participants agreed to take part define the purposes for which their data may be used and the audiences to which they may be shown. Data shared beyond those terms breach the trust on which the research depends, and the sharing must be limited to what the consent permits. Wilkinson et al. (2016) described the reusability of data as a goal, but it is subordinate to the terms of consent.

Privacy requires that identifying information be removed or aggregated before release, and the risk of re-identification must be assessed rather than assumed to be absent. Safety requires that methods which could be used for serious harm be handled with care, and the decision to withhold some detail should be made by people with relevant expertise. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that restriction is an exception to be justified, and it must be as narrow as the justification allows.

Where restriction is necessary, the least restrictive means should be used. An embargo delays release for a stated period, a restricted access procedure releases data to approved users who meet stated criteria, and aggregated or synthetic data may give the same scientific information without the sensitive detail. Peng (2011) argued that the means of verification should be preserved even when the data cannot be released, and an approved procedure can often do this.

The most important safeguard against misuse of the limits of openness is transparency about them. A publication that states what has been withheld, why it has been withheld and how a qualified person could gain access makes the restriction visible and accountable. Ioannidis (2005) described how unexplained gaps in the evidence undermine confidence in a field, and an open statement of restriction keeps the limit from becoming a silent barrier to verification.
