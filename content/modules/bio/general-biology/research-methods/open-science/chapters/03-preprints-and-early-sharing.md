A preprint is a manuscript posted to a public server before it has passed formal peer review. It establishes the date on which a finding was made public, and it makes the work available to the community for scrutiny without waiting for a journal decision. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that early sharing supports the self-correcting character of science, because errors are more likely to be found when many readers can examine the work.

The value of early scrutiny depends on readers who examine the work carefully. A preprint may receive comments that identify a flawed analysis, an unreported exclusion or a claim that the data cannot support. Ioannidis (2005) described how errors that survive the review of a few people can persist in the literature for years, and open comment on a preprint offers a route to finding them sooner.

The risk of preprints is that they are read as established findings. A preprint that has not been reviewed may contain errors that a journal would have caught, and the press or a practitioner may act on it as though it were settled. Each preprint should state clearly that it is not peer reviewed, and readers should weigh its claims accordingly. Cumming (2014) argues that the precision of each estimate should be given in the preprint, so that its uncertainty is visible even before review.

A preprint can be updated with new versions, and each version should record what changed and why. The preprint should be linked to the published version when one appears, so that readers can move between them. Early sharing also makes negative and null results visible sooner, which reduces the delay in the field and the duplication of effort that comes from unpublished work. Journals have policies on preprints, and authors should check them before posting.
