Open science practices require effort, and effort goes where it is rewarded. When evaluation counts only articles, the preparation of data for release and the writing of documented code are costs that bring no credit, and researchers reasonably give them less attention. Ioannidis (2005) described how the incentives of the field shape its findings, and the incentives for openness are the lever that changes the practice.

Recognition of data, code and materials as scholarly outputs is the most direct change. When a dataset or a software tool is cited, credited and counted in evaluation, the work of making it reusable becomes worthwhile. Wilkinson et al. (2016) argued that the persistent identifier and the citation make such outputs visible, and visibility is what evaluation depends on.

Journals and funders can signal and require transparent practice. Badges that recognise shared data and pre-registration, policies that require a data availability statement, and mandates that require a data management plan each raise the expected standard. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that such standards are most effective when they are consistent across venues, because a standard adopted by a few journals changes little.

Repositories and training reduce the cost of openness, which is often the barrier that matters most. A repository that accepts a dataset with little effort, and a course that teaches the steps, make openness practical for a researcher with limited time. Institutions can support this by changing hiring and promotion criteria so that transparency is counted alongside productivity. The evidence that open practice improves the quality and uptake of research strengthens the case for these changes, and Peng (2011) argued that the value of research depends on whether it can be used, which openness makes possible.
