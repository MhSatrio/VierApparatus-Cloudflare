Reviewing is a skill that is rarely taught, and most researchers learn it by receiving reviews of their own work. This is a poor route, because the reviewer's own experience is narrow and the standards of review vary widely between fields and between journals. Training makes the standards explicit, and it allows early-career researchers to take part in review with the confidence that a careful judgement requires. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the quality of review is a determinant of the quality of the record, and training improves it.

A practical route begins with reading published reviews and the decisions that followed them. A researcher can learn which concerns were decisive, which were ignored and how the authors responded. Reading the reviews alongside the revised papers shows the effect of a good review on the final work. Ioannidis (2005) described how the literature is shaped by the judgements made in review, and understanding those judgements is the first step towards making them well.

Supervised review follows, in which a junior researcher drafts a report on a manuscript and a senior colleague comments on the draft. The senior colleague points out missed concerns, unclear requests and judgements that go beyond the evidence. The junior researcher learns to separate major from minor concerns and to state each concern specifically. Cumming (2014) argues that a reviewer should ask for the precision of each main claim, and supervised practice teaches how to make that request clearly.

Training should include the statistical checks and the reporting guidelines that structure a judgement. Reviewers who know the guidelines for their design can check whether the report contains what it should. Recognition is also needed: review is time-consuming and largely unrewarded, and institutions that count review as a scholarly contribution encourage researchers to do it well. Editors can help by giving feedback on reports, so that reviewers improve and the system of review becomes more reliable.
