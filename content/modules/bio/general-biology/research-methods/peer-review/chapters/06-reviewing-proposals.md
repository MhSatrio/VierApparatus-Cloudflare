Grant reviewers assess proposals that have not yet been carried out, so their judgement rests on the design, the preliminary evidence and the team. Each criterion, such as significance, approach, feasibility and team, is scored separately, and each score must be justified in writing. A score without a justification gives the applicant and the panel nothing to act on, and it cannot be defended if the outcome is challenged.

The reviewer distinguishes the science from the fit with the funder's remit. The science is judged on its merits, whereas the fit is the funder's concern and may be stated separately. A proposal may be scientifically excellent but outside a scheme's remit, and the reviewer should say so without penalising the science. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that a proposal should be judged on the rigour of its methods, and the reviewer's role is to apply that standard.

The approach is judged on whether it can answer the aims. The sample size should be justified by a stated effect and precision, and the analysis should be planned in advance. Cumming (2014) argues that a proposal that states its expected precision gives the reviewer a clear basis for judgement, and one that omits it leaves the reviewer to guess.

A reviewer should not penalise a sound approach because it differs from the one the reviewer would have chosen. The test is whether the approach is adequate to the aims, not whether it matches the reviewer's preference. Ioannidis (2005) described how the imposition of a single preferred method can narrow the range of research that is funded, and a fair review distinguishes a sound alternative from one that is genuinely inadequate. The proposal is confidential, and the reviewer declares any conflict of interest in the same way as for a manuscript.
