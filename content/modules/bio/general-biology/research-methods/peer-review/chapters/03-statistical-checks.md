A reviewer without access to the raw data can still check whether the reported numbers are consistent with one another. A test statistic, its degrees of freedom and its p-value are linked by a known mathematical relation, and a reported combination that does not satisfy it indicates an error in transcription or in analysis. Ioannidis (2005) described how errors of this kind are more common than is generally admitted, and simple consistency checks are an inexpensive way to find them.

An interval should be consistent with the estimate it accompanies and with the sample size reported. An interval that is too narrow for the stated sample, or that is not centred on the estimate, suggests a reporting error or an analysis that does not match the description. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval is the most informative element of a report, and a reviewer who checks it gains a useful measure of the reliability of the numbers.

Where the summary statistics are reported, a reviewer can recalculate a simple effect size and compare it with the one stated. Agreement increases confidence in the report, while disagreement calls for an explanation. Peng (2011) argued that the reproducibility of a result depends on the consistency of its reported parts, and this check tests that consistency directly.

Results that are implausibly precise for their sample size deserve scrutiny. A very small study that reports a very narrow interval may have pseudoreplicated its data, counted measurements as independent units, or made an error in calculation. The reviewer should ask for the unit of replication and the analysis to be stated. The sample sizes in the text should also match those in the tables and figures, and multiple comparisons without correction should be noted.

These checks have limits. They detect some errors but cannot establish that a result is correct, and a consistent report may still be wrong in its design or its data. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the reviewer's checks complement, rather than replace, the transparency of the authors, and the reviewer should state what was checked and what was not.
