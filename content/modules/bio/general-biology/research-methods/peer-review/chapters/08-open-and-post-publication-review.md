Open and post-publication review extend the evaluation of research beyond the moment of acceptance. When the review reports of an accepted article are published, readers can see the concerns that were raised and how the authors responded, and they can judge the strength of the acceptance for themselves. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that such visibility improves trust, because the basis of a claim is open to examination rather than hidden behind a decision.

Open identities make reviewers accountable, since a named reviewer must stand behind the comments. The protections that keep reviewers candid, such as the separation of the identity from the report or the privacy of confidential comments to the editor, must be preserved. Ioannidis (2005) described how the social dynamics of review can shape which findings survive, and the design of open review should balance accountability with the freedom to criticise.

Post-publication commenting allows the evaluation of a claim to continue after the article appears. Readers can comment on a paper's methods, point to errors, report failures to replicate and raise questions that the review did not consider. Peng (2011) argued that the reproducibility of a result is tested over time, and a public forum for comment makes that testing visible and ongoing.

Replication and reanalysis are the most powerful forms of post-publication review, because they test the claim directly. A reanalysis of deposited data may reveal an error or an alternative conclusion, and a replication may show whether the effect holds. Commentators on published work have responsibilities: they should declare their interests, be courteous, focus on the work rather than the authors and state the evidence for each criticism. Platforms that host discussion should moderate it, so that legitimate criticism is heard and abuse is not tolerated.
