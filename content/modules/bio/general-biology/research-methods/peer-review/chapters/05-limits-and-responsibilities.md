Peer review is a useful safeguard, but it has limits that every reviewer should understand. Reviewers usually see the manuscript and not the raw data, and they cannot detect fraud or error that is hidden in the data or the analysis. Ioannidis (2005) described how review has repeatedly failed to prevent the publication of findings that do not replicate, and a reviewer who believes that review guarantees truth overestimates what it can achieve.

Because review cannot see everything, it depends on the honesty of the authors and on the transparency of the methods. A manuscript that describes its methods, deposits its data and reports every planned outcome gives the reviewer the means to check it. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the evaluation of research is strengthened by openness, and a reviewer can ask for the data and code where they are not supplied. Peng (2011) argued that the reproducibility of a result is the strongest guarantee, and review can test whether this condition is met.

The reviewer's responsibilities follow from these limits. The reviewer owes the authors a timely and fair review, which evaluates the work on its merits and does not delay publication for personal reasons. The reviewer owes the editor a return within the agreed time, with a clear recommendation, and this allows the editor to make a decision. Cumming (2014) argues that a reviewer should flag claims whose precision is not reported, since these are the claims a reader is least able to judge.

The reviewer also owes the field the recognition that review is a shared and imperfect safeguard. Errors that pass review can be corrected later through replication, post-publication comment and reanalysis, and the scientific community depends on all of these together. Ioannidis (2005) described how the correction of the literature is slow, and reviewers who take their responsibility seriously contribute to making that correction faster and more reliable.
