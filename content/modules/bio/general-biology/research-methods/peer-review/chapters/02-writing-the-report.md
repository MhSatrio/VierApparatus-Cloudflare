A review report is read by the editor, who uses it to decide, and by the authors, who use it to revise. It should begin with a short summary of the work in the reviewer's own words, which shows that the paper has been understood and allows the editor to check the reviewer's grasp of it. The summary should be neutral, and it should state the main claim as the reviewer understands it, so that any misreading is visible early.

The report should separate major concerns from minor ones. A major concern affects whether the conclusions are supported, such as an analysis that does not match the design or an interpretation that goes beyond the data. A minor concern affects presentation, such as an unclear label or a missing unit. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the reviewer should focus attention on the concerns that affect the validity of the work, rather than on matters of style that do not change the findings.

Each concern should state what is wrong, why it matters and what change would address it. A concern that says the analysis is inadequate gives the authors nothing to act on, whereas one that says the primary outcome should be analysed with a model that accounts for the clustering of animals gives a clear task. Cumming (2014) argues that the reviewer should ask for the interval of each main estimate, since this is a specific and checkable request.

The report must be free of personal remarks, and its judgements should be about the work, not the authors. A reviewer distinguishes a request for additional analysis, which may require new data, from a request for clarification, which requires only a better explanation. The recommendation to the editor, such as accept, revise or reject, should be stated with its reasons. Confidential comments to the editor, such as concerns about misconduct, are kept separate from the report that goes to the authors. Ioannidis (2005) described how fair and specific review improves the reliability of the literature that it filters.
