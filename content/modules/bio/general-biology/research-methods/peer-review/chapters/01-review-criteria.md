The criteria of review are the standards against which a manuscript is judged, and a reviewer should apply them consistently. The first criterion is the question: whether it is clearly stated, whether it addresses a gap in the evidence and whether the answer would matter. A study of a question that is already settled, or of one that does not matter to anyone, is unlikely to add to the record, however well it is conducted. Ioannidis (2005) described how questions chosen without regard to the existing evidence contribute to the flood of unreplicated findings.

The second criterion is the design. The reviewer asks whether the design can answer the question, whether the controls are adequate and whether the sample is large enough to estimate the effect with a useful precision. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the design should be judged on the basis of whether it protects against bias, and the presence of randomisation, blinding and pre-specification is evidence of that protection.

The third criterion is the analysis. The reviewer checks whether the analysis matches the design, whether the primary outcome was pre-specified and whether the tests are appropriate to the data. Cumming (2014) argues that the review should ask for estimates with intervals in place of a reliance on p-values, since the interval conveys the precision that a reader needs.

The fourth criterion is the conclusion. It must follow from the results, and a conclusion that goes beyond them, particularly in its causal or practical claims, should be identified and a change proposed. The reviewer weighs these criteria together to reach a judgement proportionate to the strength of the evidence, rather than to the apparent importance of the topic.
