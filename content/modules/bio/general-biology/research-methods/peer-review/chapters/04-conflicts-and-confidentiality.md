A conflict of interest exists when a reviewer's own interests could influence the judgement of the work. The conflicts include collaboration with the authors in the recent past, competition for the same funding or the same result, financial interests in the outcome and personal relationships. Some conflicts can be declared and the review proceeds, while others make a fair review impossible and the reviewer should decline. Ioannidis (2005) described how the interests of those who evaluate research can shape what is accepted, and the disclosure of interests is the first safeguard against this.

Declaration is not sufficient where the conflict is severe. A reviewer who is directly competing with the authors, and who could benefit from a delay in publication, cannot give a neutral judgement, however honest the intention. The reviewer should decline the review and inform the editor, rather than attempt to proceed. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the integrity of review depends on its independence, and the reviewer's duty is to protect it.

The manuscript is confidential, and its contents, including unpublished data and methods, must not be used by the reviewer for any purpose. A reviewer who learns of a finding from a manuscript and uses it in their own work has committed a serious breach. Peng (2011) argued that the trust placed in the reviewer depends on the reliability of this confidentiality, and an unpublished result is the property of its authors until it is published.

The manuscript must not be shared with others without the editor's permission, and a reviewer should not seek the advice of a colleague without disclosing that they are doing so. Authors sometimes ask to know the identity of the reviewer, and the editor decides whether to disclose it, not the reviewer. Concerns about misconduct, such as fabricated data, should be reported to the editor through the confidential channel rather than discussed with the authors or others, since an unfounded accusation can damage a researcher without cause. Cumming (2014) argues that the evaluation of the data should be done by the editor and the authors together, and the reviewer's role is to report what the manuscript shows.
