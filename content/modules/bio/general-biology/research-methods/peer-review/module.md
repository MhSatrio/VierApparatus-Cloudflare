Peer review is the evaluation of research by qualified colleagues before it is published or funded. Its purposes are to detect errors, to judge whether the question is significant and to advise the editor or funder on what should be done. Ioannidis (2005) described how the system of review has limits, since reviewers rarely have access to the raw data and cannot detect every error, and a reviewer should understand what review can and cannot achieve.

A reviewer judges the question, the design, the analysis and the reporting against stated criteria. The question should be clear and important, the design should be able to answer it, the analysis should match the design and the reporting should give the estimates and intervals the reader needs. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the reviewer should check whether the methods are described well enough to repeat, since a review that accepts an unrepeatable method cannot assure the field.

A review report should be specific, constructive and organised. It separates major concerns, which affect the conclusions, from minor concerns, which affect the presentation. Each concern should state what is wrong and what would address it. Cumming (2014) argues that a reviewer should ask for the precision of each claim to be reported, since many claims that look strong have wide intervals that the text ignores.

Reviewers must declare conflicts of interest and decline work they cannot judge fairly. They must also keep the manuscript confidential and not use its content before publication. Peng (2011) argued that the reproducibility of review depends on its transparency, and the reviewer's responsibility is to the authors, the editor and the wider field that will rely on the result. The following chapters examine the criteria, the report, the statistical checks and the limits of review in turn.
