A direct replication repeats a study with the same methods and a new sample, and it tests whether the original effect is found again. Its value depends on its power. A replication with the same small sample as the original will often fail even when the effect is real, and a replication should be planned with a sample large enough to detect the original effect with high probability. Ioannidis (2005) described how the first reports of effects are often inflated, so the replication sample should be based on a realistic, and usually smaller, estimate of the effect.

The comparison between the original and the replication estimate should be made with their intervals. Two studies whose intervals overlap substantially are consistent with a common effect, whereas non-overlapping intervals suggest a real difference in their conditions or in the original estimate. Cumming (2014) argues that the question of replication is one of estimation, and the intervals show how well the replication pins down the effect.

A failed replication is evidence, but it is not proof that the original finding was false. The replication may differ in population, equipment or skill, and each difference is a possible explanation. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that a failed replication should prompt an examination of these differences, and the original and replication estimates should be pooled where the methods are judged comparable.

A conceptual replication tests the same hypothesis with different methods, and it establishes whether the effect is general or specific to one approach. When conceptual replications succeed, the finding is more secure, because it does not depend on one method. Ioannidis (2005) emphasised that replication with independent methods and independent samples is among the strongest protections against error, and the replication results should be published whatever they show.
