Research is shaped by the rewards it offers. Careers are advanced by publications, and publications in prominent journals are most often given to novel results that are positive, so the incentives favour the findings most likely to be inflated. Ioannidis (2005) described how these incentives, combined with the small samples and flexible analysis common in the field, make a large share of published findings unlikely to be true, and the incentives are therefore as much a part of the problem as the methods.

Pressures of career progression encourage flexible analysis and selective reporting, even among researchers who wish to act honestly. A young investigator who needs a significant result for a grant may choose an analysis that produces one, without any intention to deceive. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the remedy lies in changing the criteria by which research is evaluated, so that rigour and transparency become the basis of advancement.

Journals, funders and institutions can change these criteria. Badges that recognise the sharing of data and code, policies that require the registration of trials, and evaluation schemes that assess the quality of methods rather than the number of papers each have a measurable effect. Peng (2011) argued that such standards are most effective when they are applied consistently, since a standard that is adopted by some journals and ignored by others changes little.

Mentoring is the means by which reproducible practice is taught, and it is most effective at the beginning of a career. A student who learns to script analyses, to record decisions and to report null results will carry these habits forward. Training in the statistical reasoning behind replication, and in the interpretation of intervals, supports this aim. Evaluation of research should reward the rigour of its methods and the completeness of its reporting, rather than only the novelty of its results.
