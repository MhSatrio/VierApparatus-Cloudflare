A single replication does not settle whether an effect is real, and the evidence is strongest when the original study and its replications are considered together. The pooled estimate combines all the studies, weighting each by its precision, and the heterogeneity among them shows whether they agree. Higgins & Thompson (2002) described how this heterogeneity can be quantified, and the same methods apply when the studies are direct replications of one another.

The original study is often the most inflated estimate in a series, because the first report of an effect is frequently selected for being large. Ioannidis (2005) described how this winner's curse produces original estimates that shrink when the effect is tested again. Comparing the original estimate with the distribution of replication estimates reveals this inflation, and the pooled estimate that includes the replications is usually the more reliable guide.

Replications can be pooled only when they are similar enough in population, method and outcome for a common effect to be meaningful. Where they differ substantially, the reasons should be examined, and the synthesis may describe the range of effects rather than a single average. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that a synthesis should be explicit about the criteria by which replications were judged comparable.

Bayesian methods offer a different way of combining evidence, by updating a prior belief with the new data to give a posterior distribution of the effect. The result is expressed as a probability distribution, and its width conveys the remaining uncertainty. Cumming (2014) argues that whichever method is used, the combined estimate should be reported with its interval, so that the field can judge how much confidence the finding deserves.
