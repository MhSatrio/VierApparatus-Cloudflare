Pre-registration records the hypotheses, the design and the analysis plan in a public registry before the data are collected. The registry entry is time-stamped, so that it can be shown that the plan preceded the results. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that this makes the flexibility of analysis visible, since any departure from the plan can be seen and justified. The registration does not prevent exploration, but it marks clearly which findings were planned and which were discovered.

The registered report goes further by separating the decision to publish from the outcome of the study. The study is reviewed on the importance of its question and the soundness of its method before the data are collected, and an accepted report is published whatever its results. Ioannidis (2005) described how the publication of only positive results inflates the literature, and the registered report removes the incentive that produces this bias.

Null results become publishable under this format, which corrects the imbalance in the record. A well-designed study that finds no effect contributes information, and its estimate with its interval is a valuable input to any later synthesis. Cumming (2014) argues that the estimate and its interval should be reported in every case, so that the null result can be judged by its precision as well as its direction.

Deviations from the registered plan are permitted, but each must be reported with its date and reason. The confirmatory tests are those specified in the plan, and they carry the weight of the study's main claim. Exploratory analyses that were not planned may suggest hypotheses for future work, and they should be labelled as such in the report. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that this labelling is essential to the honest interpretation of a study.
