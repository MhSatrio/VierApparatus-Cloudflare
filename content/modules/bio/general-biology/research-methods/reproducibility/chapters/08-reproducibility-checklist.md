A checklist turns the principles of reproducibility into items that can be checked before a study is submitted. Each item asks a concrete question about the design, the analysis, the materials or the reporting. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the habit of checking is as important as the knowledge of what to check, because the errors that undermine reproducibility are usually small and easily missed by an investigator who has been close to the work for a long time.

The design items ask whether the hypotheses, the sample size and the analysis were specified before data collection, whether the randomisation, blinding and controls were used, and whether the unit of replication was identified. Cumming (2014) argues that the design should state the precision the study aims to achieve, and the checklist asks for this to be recorded with the sample size.

The materials items ask whether every reagent, lot number, software version and strain is recorded, and whether the methods are complete enough for another laboratory to repeat them. The analysis items ask whether every step from raw data to figure is scripted, whether random seeds are fixed and whether the code and data are deposited with identifiers. Sandve et al. (2013) provided the rules that underlie these items.

The reporting items ask whether all planned outcomes are reported, including those with null results, whether every exclusion and deviation is stated with its reason, and whether the effect estimates are given with their intervals. Ioannidis (2005) described how the omission of such details makes a published finding impossible to evaluate. The checklist is completed by a second person where possible, since an independent reader finds errors the author cannot see.
