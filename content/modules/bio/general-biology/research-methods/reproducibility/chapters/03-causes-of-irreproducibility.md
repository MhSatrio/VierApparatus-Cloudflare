Irreproducibility has causes that can be identified and, in most cases, prevented. Low statistical power is among the most important. A study with too few participants can detect only the largest chance deviations, and the effect estimates it produces are inflated when they reach significance. Ioannidis (2005) described how such inflated estimates accumulate in a literature, and each replication then appears to fail when it is compared with the overstated originals.

Selective reporting distorts the record in a different way. When only the favourable outcomes, analyses or studies reach publication, the published effect is larger than the true effect, and the unreported results remain invisible. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that this is one of the most serious threats to the reliability of science, and the solution lies in reporting all planned outcomes and in publishing null results.

Flexible analysis allows an investigator to try many analyses, exclusions and transformations until one gives a significant result. Each choice is individually reasonable, but their combination makes a false positive highly likely. Cumming (2014) argues that the analysis should be specified in advance and that estimates should be reported with their intervals, so that the flexibility is limited and the uncertainty is visible.

Insufficient description of methods prevents others from repeating the work, and it hides the sources of variation that matter. Reagent lots, cell lines, animal strains and the conditions of measurement all influence results, and their omission makes a finding difficult to reproduce. Ioannidis (2005) noted that many failures to replicate reflect such unrecorded differences rather than false original findings, and complete reporting is the remedy.
