Reproducibility is the property that allows a finding to be confirmed by others. The word covers several activities that differ in what they establish. Computational reproducibility shows that the same data and code produce the same result. Direct replication repeats the study with new data and the same methods, and conceptual replication tests the same idea with different methods. Each is valuable, and a finding that survives all three is far more secure than one that has been checked only once.

Irreproducibility has several causes. Low statistical power produces unreliable estimates, selective reporting of favourable results distorts the literature, and flexible analysis allows many routes to a significant result. Ioannidis (2005) described how these causes combine to make many published findings false, and the remedies address each cause directly. Munafò et al. (2017) set out the practices that the research community can adopt to reduce them.

The practices that reduce irreproducibility include the pre-registration of hypotheses and analyses, complete reporting of methods, and the public sharing of data and code. Version control of analysis pipelines keeps a record of every change, so that an earlier result can be regenerated. Peng (2011) argued that the computational steps behind a result are part of the result, and Sandve et al. (2013) provided practical rules for making them reproducible.

Replication studies establish which findings are robust, and they are essential because the first report of a result is often the most inflated. Cumming (2014) argues that replication should be judged by the agreement of effect estimates and their intervals, rather than by whether each study crosses a threshold of significance. Institutions that reward novelty over replication discourage this work, and the incentives that shape research are part of the problem that reproducibility must solve.
