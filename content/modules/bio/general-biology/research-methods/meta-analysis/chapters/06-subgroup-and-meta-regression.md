Subgroup analysis and meta-regression are the principal tools for explaining heterogeneity. A subgroup analysis divides the studies into groups, such as those with human or animal populations, and compares the pooled estimates of the groups. A meta-regression relates the effect of each study to a study-level covariate, such as the dose, the duration of follow-up or the mean age of participants. Higgins & Thompson (2002) described how the variation that remains after such explanation can be assessed, and Page et al. (2021) required the planned subgroups to be reported.

The subgroups and the covariates should be specified before the analysis, with a reason for each, since a search through many possible divisions will find differences by chance. Ioannidis (2005) described how such multiple comparisons produce spurious findings that appear strong, and the number of comparisons made must be reported. A difference between subgroup estimates is tested directly, and an apparent difference in significance between two subgroups is not itself evidence of a difference between them.

Meta-regression uses the study as the unit of analysis, so its covariate describes a property of the study rather than of each participant. A relationship between a study-level covariate and the effect does not establish that the same relationship holds for individuals, which is the ecological fallacy. Studies that differ in dose may also differ in population or in quality, so the covariate may be confounded with other differences among the studies.

Meta-regression also has low power when there are few studies, and a model with several covariates will over-fit the data. Cumming (2014) argues that the estimated slope should be reported with its interval, so that the uncertainty of the relationship is visible. Subgroup and meta-regression results should be labelled as exploratory unless they were pre-specified, and their conclusions should be treated as hypotheses for future studies.
