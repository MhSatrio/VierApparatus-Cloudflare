Publication bias occurs when the studies that are published differ systematically from those that are not, most often because studies with significant or favourable results are more likely to appear in journals. A meta-analysis that includes only published studies can then overstate the effect, because the missing null results are absent from the pool. Ioannidis (2005) described how this selection can make a field appear to have consistent positive findings, and Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the reproducibility of science depends on the publication of all results.

The funnel plot displays each study's effect on the horizontal axis against its precision on the vertical axis. In the absence of bias the points form a symmetrical funnel, because small studies scatter widely and large studies cluster near the true value. An asymmetrical funnel, with a gap where small studies with null or negative results would be expected, suggests that such studies are missing. Asymmetry can also arise from true differences among studies, heterogeneity, or chance, so it is a warning rather than a proof.

Regression tests quantify funnel plot asymmetry by relating the effect to its standard error. These tests have low power when the number of studies is small, and they can give false reassurance when there are few studies. The trim-and-fill method estimates the number of missing studies and imputes them to produce an adjusted pooled estimate, and its result should be reported as a sensitivity analysis because its assumptions may not hold.

The most effective defence against publication bias is a comprehensive search that includes trial registries, theses, conference abstracts and grey literature, as described in the chapter on searching. Page et al. (2021) required reviews to report the methods used to assess publication bias and the reasons for any limitations. Cumming (2014) argues that the bias analysis should be reported with the pooled estimate and its interval, not as a separate conclusion.
