A meta-analysis combines the results of several studies into a single estimate of an effect, with an interval that expresses its precision. It is a statistical step within a systematic review, and it is undertaken only when the studies are similar enough in their question and their outcome for a combined estimate to be meaningful. Page et al. (2021) required the method, the model and the measures of heterogeneity to be reported, since each choice affects the pooled result.

The pooled estimate depends on the model. A fixed-effect model assumes that all studies estimate one true effect, and a random-effects model allows the true effect to differ among studies, which is usually the more realistic assumption. Higgins & Thompson (2002) showed how the amount of variation among studies can be quantified, and DerSimonian & Laird (1986) gave the method that is still most widely used to estimate it for random-effects pooling. The later chapters examine these methods in turn.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a pooled estimate can be inflated by biased studies or by studies that were never published, and the chapters on publication bias and sensitivity analysis address these problems. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval of the pooled estimate must be reported and interpreted, since a precise average can conceal large differences between studies.

Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the models, the software and the decisions should be recorded, so that a meta-analysis can be reproduced and checked. The final chapter sets out how to interpret and report a pooled estimate with its certainty.
