A research interest is usually broad, and a project must narrow it to a question that can be answered with the methods at hand. The narrowing begins with a survey of what is already known and what remains uncertain. A question that has been answered many times adds little, and a question that cannot be answered by any available method is not useful. The gap between what is known and what is worth knowing is where a good question lies.

A well-formed question names the population to be studied, the exposure or intervention, the comparison and the outcome. Munafò et al. (2017) emphasised that a question stated in these terms can be tested, because each element defines what data are needed. A question that omits the comparison, for example, cannot be answered, since an outcome means little without a standard against which to judge it.

Questions differ in kind. A descriptive question asks how common a condition is or what a population is like, an associational question asks whether two variables are related, and a causal question asks whether changing one produces a change in the other. Each kind requires a different design, and a study that answers an associational question cannot support a causal claim without further evidence. The kind of question should be stated, so that the conclusions drawn match the design.

A question must be neither too vague nor too narrow. A vague question such as whether a treatment is good cannot be tested, and a narrow question that concerns a trivial detail may not matter to anyone. A useful test is to ask what result would change the investigator's view, since a question for which no result could change the view is not a test. Ioannidis (2005) described how a question chosen because it will produce a positive result is a source of unreliable findings, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the question and the reasons for it.
