A question may be important and still be impossible to answer with the resources available. Feasibility is the test of whether the study can be completed, and it must be assessed before any work begins. The data needed to answer the question must exist or be collectable, the time and money must suffice, and the skills and equipment must be available. A feasibility assessment that is honest about these constraints prevents a project from failing after years of effort.

The sample size is the most important feasibility constraint, and it follows from the effect of interest. A small effect requires a large sample to detect with adequate power, and the required size may exceed what can be recruited. Munafò et al. (2017) noted that underpowered studies are a common source of unreliable findings, and the feasibility of the sample must be checked against the power needed for the question.

The risk of failure must also be assessed. Participants may drop out, samples may be lost, instruments may fail and the planned data may not be obtained. Each risk reduces the effective sample or the quality of the data, and a plan should allow for the expected losses. Cumming (2014) argues that a study should be designed so that its estimates will be precise enough to be useful even if the sample is smaller than planned.

The scope bounds the question so that it can be completed. A question can often be narrowed to a population, a setting or an outcome that the project can actually address. If a question cannot be made feasible by narrowing, it should be redesigned or abandoned. Ioannidis (2005) described how studies that are started without a realistic assessment of their power often produce uninterpretable results, and the assessment should be recorded with the question.
