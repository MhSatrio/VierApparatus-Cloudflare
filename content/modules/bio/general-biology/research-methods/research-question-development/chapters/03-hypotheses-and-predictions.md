A hypothesis is a statement about how the world works, and a prediction is a statement about what the data will show if the hypothesis is true. The prediction is what the study tests. A hypothesis that predicts nothing that could be observed cannot be tested, and a prediction that would be observed whether or not the hypothesis were true tells the investigator nothing. A good prediction therefore states a data pattern that would be surprising if the hypothesis were false.

The null hypothesis states that there is no effect or no difference, and the alternative states that there is one. The study is designed to assess the data against the null, and the direction and the size of the expected effect should be stated in advance. A hypothesis that says only that an effect exists, without its direction or size, is weak, because almost any data can be read as consistent with it.

A hypothesis written after the results are known is a serious problem. The investigator who sees the data may formulate a hypothesis that the data support, and then present it as if it had been the aim of the study. Ioannidis (2005) described how this practice, often called hypothesising after the results are known, produces findings that do not replicate, and the defence is to state the hypothesis before the data are examined.

Risky predictions, which could easily prove false, are the most valuable, because a prediction that survives a severe test gives more support than one that is easily met. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the scientific culture should reward such tests, and Cumming (2014) argues that the predicted effect should be stated with its size and its interval, so that the result can be compared with the prediction.
