A pilot study is a small preliminary study that tests the feasibility of the main study and refines its design. It can show whether participants can be recruited and retained, whether the measurements work in practice and how variable the outcome is. These are valuable questions, and a pilot that answers them saves the main study from avoidable failure. The pilot is not designed to answer the research question itself, and it must not be treated as if it did.

The variability of the outcome estimated in a pilot is needed for the power calculation of the main study, and it is often the most useful product of a pilot. The effect size estimated in a pilot, however, is unreliable, because pilots are small and the estimates of effect from small samples are imprecise and often exaggerated. Ioannidis (2005) described how an effect estimated in a small study tends to be overstated, and a power calculation based on a pilot effect may plan a study too small for the true effect.

The pilot tests whether the measurement procedures work, whether instruments are sensitive enough and whether participants understand the tasks. It also reveals practical problems such as missing data, scheduling difficulties and the time each procedure takes. These findings improve the main study, and they should be reported so that others can avoid the same problems.

Pilot results must be reported with their purpose and their limits. A pilot that is presented as evidence for an effect misleads the reader, and its estimates should be given with their wide intervals. Cumming (2014) argues that the imprecision of small-sample estimates should be shown, so that the reader does not mistake a pilot for a conclusive result.
