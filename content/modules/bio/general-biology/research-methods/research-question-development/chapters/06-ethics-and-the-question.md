A research question must be justified before a study is designed, and the justification is ethical as well as scientific. Research imposes a burden on the people or animals who take part, and that burden is justified only if the answer is likely to be of value. The question should therefore be assessed for its potential benefit to participants, to future patients or to the wider public, and for the cost that it imposes on those who take part.

The principle of proportionality requires that a study use no more participants or animals than are needed to answer the question. An underpowered study uses participants without producing a useful answer, and an oversized study uses more than necessary. Munafò et al. (2017) noted that poor research practice wastes the resources and the goodwill of participants, and the sample size must be justified by the question.

Consent must be informed, and vulnerable groups require additional protection. The question should not be designed to exploit those who cannot easily refuse, and the benefit should not fall only to those who are already advantaged. The protection of participants is part of the justification of the question, and the study must be designed to respect it.

Research should answer questions whose answers matter, rather than repeating settled ones or pursuing trivial details. The duty extends to reporting results whatever they show. A negative result that is not published means that the participants' contribution is lost to the field, and other investigators may repeat a study that has already failed. Ioannidis (2005) described the consequences of publication bias, and the ethical duty to report is part of responsible research. Cumming (2014) argues that a result should be reported with its interval whether or not it reaches significance.
