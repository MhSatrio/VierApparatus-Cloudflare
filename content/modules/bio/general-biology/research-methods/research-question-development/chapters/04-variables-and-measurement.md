A research question names its variables, and each must be defined and measured before the study begins. The outcome is the quantity the question is about, and the exposure or intervention is the factor whose effect is of interest. Confounders are factors that affect both and must be controlled, and mediators lie on the path between them and change the meaning of an estimate if adjusted for. The roles of the variables should be specified in the question, since they determine the analysis.

A concept such as stress or wellbeing must be made measurable through an operational definition, which states the procedure by which the concept is quantified. Different operational definitions of the same concept may give different results, and the choice must be justified. Cumming (2014) argues that the measurement should be described in enough detail that its precision can be judged.

Validity is the degree to which a measure captures what it is intended to capture, and reliability is the degree to which it gives consistent results on repetition. A measure may be reliable and invalid, giving the same wrong answer each time, or valid on average but unreliable for each individual. Both must be assessed, and the assessment should be reported.

The scale of measurement, whether nominal, ordinal, interval or ratio, determines which statistics are appropriate, and treating an ordinal scale as interval can produce misleading results. Each variable should be reported with its scale, its definition and its distribution, so that the reader can judge the analysis. Ioannidis (2005) described how measurements that are chosen after the results are seen may be selected to produce a result, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the definitions and the measurement procedures.
