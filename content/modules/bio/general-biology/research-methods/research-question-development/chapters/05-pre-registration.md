Pre-registration deposits the question, the hypotheses, the design and the analysis plan in a public registry before the data are collected or examined. The registry records the date, so that the plan can be shown to have existed before the results. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that pre-registration is one of the most effective means of protecting research against the flexibility that produces false positives, since it makes visible the decisions that would otherwise be made after the data are seen.

A registration should state the primary outcome, the sample size and its justification, the exclusion criteria, the statistical test and the decision rule. The more fully these are specified, the less room remains for choices that favour a result. The registration need not anticipate every analysis, but it should make clear which analyses are planned and which are added later.

The registration distinguishes confirmatory analyses, which test the pre-specified hypotheses, from exploratory analyses, which generate new hypotheses from the data. Both are valuable, but they have different evidential status, and a report that presents an exploratory finding as confirmatory misleads the reader. Ioannidis (2005) described how the blurring of these two kinds of analysis is a central cause of findings that do not replicate.

Registered reports go further, in that the design and the analysis are reviewed before the data are collected, and the publication is decided on the quality of the question rather than on the result. Deviations from the registered plan are allowed, but they must be reported and justified. Peng (2011) argued that the means of reproducing a result must be available, and the registration provides a record of the plan against which the study can be judged. Cumming (2014) argues that the planned estimates and their intervals should be stated in the registration.
