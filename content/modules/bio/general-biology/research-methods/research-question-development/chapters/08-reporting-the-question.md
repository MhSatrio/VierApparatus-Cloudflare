A research question is reported so that the reader can see what the study set out to answer and why. The statement should name the population, the exposure or intervention, the comparison and the outcome, and it should be followed by a rationale that connects the question to what is already known. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that a clear statement of the question is the first requirement of reproducible science, since the question defines what the study can and cannot show.

The hypotheses and predictions should be stated before the data are described, and the design and analysis plan should follow from them. A reader who sees a plan that matches the question can judge whether the analysis is appropriate, and a plan that is written after the results are known cannot be trusted in the same way. Peng (2011) argued that the means of producing a result must be available, and the plan is among those means.

Any change to the question during the study must be recorded with the reason for it. Changes are sometimes necessary, because a pilot reveals a problem or because the data are not available as expected, and the change is legitimate when it is reported openly. A change that is hidden, so that the reported question differs from the original, misleads the reader about the study's aim. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording every change so that the history of the study can be followed.

Where a registration exists, the report should link to it, and the ethical and practical justification of the study should be given. Cumming (2014) argues that the estimates expected from the question should be stated with their precision, so that the eventual results can be compared with what was anticipated. The question, the plan and the record of changes together give the reader what is needed to judge the study.
