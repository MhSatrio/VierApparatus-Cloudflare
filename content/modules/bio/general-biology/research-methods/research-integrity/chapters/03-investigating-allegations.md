An allegation of misconduct must be taken seriously and investigated fairly, because both the complainant and the accused have interests that the process must protect. The first stage is an assessment of whether the allegation, if true, would constitute misconduct and whether it is specific enough to investigate. A vague or unfounded allegation may be closed at this stage, but a credible one proceeds to a formal inquiry, and the decision should be recorded with its reasons.

Evidence must be preserved before the investigation begins, because records can be altered or lost once the investigation is known. Raw data, laboratory notebooks, analysis files, version histories and correspondence should be secured. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the openness of data makes such preservation more effective, since an independent copy of the raw records is hard to alter. Peng (2011) argued that a reproducible analysis allows the investigators to rerun the work and test whether the reported results follow from the data.

The accused must be informed of the allegation and given the opportunity to respond. This right protects the innocent from unjust damage, and a finding reached without hearing the accused is open to challenge. The investigating panel should be independent of anyone with a conflict, and its members should be able to examine the evidence without pressure from the institution or from those involved.

The findings must state the evidence for each conclusion and the standard of proof applied. A finding that is supported by the raw data, with the calculations shown, is more credible than one that asserts a conclusion. Ioannidis (2005) described how the reliability of the record depends on the willingness to examine errors carefully, and the written findings are the public account of that examination. Sanctions should be proportionate to the findings, and the notification of funders and journals allows the record to be corrected, including retraction where the published work is unreliable.
