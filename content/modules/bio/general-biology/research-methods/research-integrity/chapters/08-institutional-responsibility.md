Research integrity is not only the responsibility of individual researchers. Institutions shape the environment in which research is done, and their policies, procedures and incentives determine how easy it is to behave well. An institution that rewards only the number of publications and the prestige of their journals creates pressure toward questionable practice, whatever its written values. Ioannidis (2005) described how such incentives shape the findings a field produces, and institutional change is the lever that alters them.

Policies must define expected conduct clearly, and training must make them known. A policy that exists only on a website has little effect, while one that is taught and discussed shapes behaviour. Procedures for handling allegations must be fair, timely and confidential, and they must protect those who raise concerns from retaliation. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that an institution that protects its whistleblowers is one that can be trusted to correct its own errors.

Criteria for appointment, promotion and funding should reward rigour and transparency alongside productivity. An institution that counts data sharing, pre-registration and replication as scholarly achievements gives researchers a reason to practise them. Peng (2011) argued that the value of research depends on its reproducibility, and evaluation should reflect this. Cumming (2014) argues that the evaluation should consider whether a researcher reports precision and the full range of results, rather than only whether a result was significant.

Institutions can provide the resources that make good practice achievable, including data management support, statistical advice and secure storage. They can audit their research for patterns that signal problems, such as an unusual concentration of retractions or implausible results, and they should report publicly how misconduct is handled. Trust in an institution is earned by its openness about failures as well as by its successes.
