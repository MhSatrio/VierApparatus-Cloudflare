Trainees learn integrity mainly by watching their supervisors. A leader who reports a null result as readily as a positive one, who discusses an error openly and who insists that records be complete teaches a norm that trainees carry into their own work. A leader who rewards only striking results, or who is impatient with failed experiments, teaches the opposite, and the lesson is learned quickly by those who depend on the leader for their careers. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the culture of a laboratory is a major determinant of the reliability of its output.

Open discussion of errors and failed experiments is a sign of a healthy laboratory. When such failures are treated as normal parts of research, trainees are not tempted to conceal them. Ioannidis (2005) described how the pressure to produce positive results drives questionable practice, and a leader who makes it safe to report negative outcomes reduces that pressure at its source.

The power imbalance between supervisor and trainee is a particular risk. A trainee who depends on the supervisor for funding, references and a future post may feel unable to refuse a request to drop an inconvenient result or to adopt an analysis that favours the hypothesis. Protection requires clear procedures for raising concerns, and the trainee must know that raising a concern will not damage their career. Cumming (2014) argues that the reporting of all estimates with their intervals limits the room for such pressure, since the full evidence is visible.

Integrity training should be part of research education rather than a separate event. Trainees should learn how to keep complete records, how to report null results, how to recognise questionable practice and how to raise a concern. Laboratories and departments should be evaluated on the quality of their practice as well as the volume of their output, so that the incentives reward the behaviour that the integrity of research requires.
