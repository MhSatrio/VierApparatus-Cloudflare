Credit is the currency of science, and its fair distribution is part of research integrity. Authorship confers credit and responsibility, and it should go to those who contributed substantially and who can answer for the work. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the attribution of responsibility is part of the transparency on which the record depends, and misattributed credit distorts the record as surely as misreported data.

Those who do not meet the criteria for authorship, such as technicians, students who provided data and collaborators who supplied a reagent, should be acknowledged. Their contribution is real and should be visible, but their inclusion as authors without the required contribution misrepresents responsibility. Ioannidis (2005) described how the structure of credit influences what is reported, and an honest acknowledgement section reflects the true distribution of work.

Ideas, data and methods must be attributed to their origin through citation. A claim that is not cited may appear to be the author's own, and the reader cannot trace its evidence. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended that the software and methods used in a study be cited as part of the record, and the same principle applies to ideas and data. Cumming (2014) argues that each estimate should be traceable to the study that produced it, which is the practical meaning of attribution in quantitative work.

Data and software are outputs in their own right, and their creators deserve credit when others reuse them. Citing a dataset or a software package with its identifier and version recognises the work of its creators and makes its use traceable. Disputes about credit should be resolved through the procedures of the institution, and the evaluation of research should count these outputs so that the incentive to attribute them fairly is strengthened.
