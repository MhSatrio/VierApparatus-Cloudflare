Questionable research practices are not fraud, and many who engage in them believe they are behaving reasonably. They nonetheless distort the evidence in the same direction as misconduct, and their cumulative effect on a literature can be large. Ioannidis (2005) described how such practices, widely used, make a large share of published findings unreliable, and Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the research community must recognise them as a problem of practice rather than of a few bad actors.

Selective reporting of outcomes is the most common of these practices. A study that measures many outcomes and reports only those that reached significance gives a false impression of the effect of the treatment. The planned outcomes should all be reported, including those that showed no effect, and the omission of a planned outcome should be explained. Cumming (2014) argues that reporting the estimate and interval for every outcome makes the selection visible, since a reader can see the full range of results.

Flexible analysis, in which the investigator tries several exclusions, transformations and models until one gives a significant result, inflates the false positive rate substantially. Each choice may seem sensible when taken alone, but the combination is a search for the desired result. Pre-specification of the analysis is the remedy, because a single planned analysis cannot be selected after the fact.

Hypothesising after the results are known is the presentation of an exploratory finding as though it had been predicted. The result appears confirmatory, and the reader is misled about how much support the hypothesis has. Stopping data collection when a result becomes significant, and reporting the result as though the sample size had been fixed in advance, is a related practice that inflates the apparent evidence. Ioannidis (2005) described how such choices make the findings look stronger than the data support, and the labelling of exploratory work as such is the correction.
