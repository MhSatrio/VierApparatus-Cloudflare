Fabrication is the invention of data, results or observations that were never obtained. It is the most direct form of misconduct, and it corrupts the record more thoroughly than any other because the false result looks exactly like a real one. A fabricated dataset may show a precise and convincing pattern, and the defence against it is the retention of the raw records and an audit trail that links each published value to its source. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the openness of data is a deterrent as well as a benefit.

Falsification is the manipulation of materials, equipment, processes or data so that the record misrepresents what occurred. Its common forms include the removal of points that do not fit a pattern, the adjustment of a value without a stated reason and the alteration of images to emphasise a feature. An image adjusted to remove background that obscures a result is a falsification even when the underlying finding is real, since the reader is shown something other than the original. Ioannidis (2005) described how the distortion of results at any stage can produce a false finding, and the record of each change is essential.

Plagiarism is the use of another person's words, ideas or results without appropriate acknowledgement. It includes the copying of text from another paper and the presentation of another researcher's unpublished result as one's own. Plagiarism is detected by comparison with existing texts and by the knowledge of those who work in the same area, and it is prevented by the habit of citing every source. Cumming (2014) argues that each estimate should be traced to the work that produced it, and citation is the means by which this tracing is made possible.

The distinction between honest error and deliberate misconduct depends on intent, and an investigation must establish it from the evidence. An error that is corrected when discovered is different in kind from a fabrication that is concealed. A reliable audit trail, which records who made each change and when, is the most important means of establishing what happened. Peng (2011) argued that the reproducibility of a computational result depends on the same trail, and a researcher who keeps one protects both the work and themselves.
