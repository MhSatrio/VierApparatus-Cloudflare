A record is the evidence of what was done, and its completeness determines whether a result can be defended. The laboratory notebook should record each procedure, observation and decision with its date, so that the work can be reconstructed. Peng (2011) argued that a result is only as trustworthy as the record that supports it, and a notebook that is written after the event, or not at all, leaves the result unsupported.

The original data should be preserved in their raw form before any processing. Electronic instruments often produce files that are overwritten by later analysis, and the loss of the original is a serious problem. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended that raw data be kept unchanged and that every derived value be traceable to them, and this separation is the core of the audit trail.

The version history of analysis files records each change, who made it and why. A history of this kind shows how an analysis developed and allows an earlier state to be recovered. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that such transparency is a basic safeguard of research integrity, since changes that are recorded cannot be concealed.

Records must be retained for the period required by the institution, the funder and the law, and they must be protected against alteration. Secure storage with timestamps makes it difficult to change a record without leaving a trace, and an investigation can rely on the timestamps to establish the sequence of events. Deviations from protocol must be recorded with their reasons, since an undocumented deviation may later appear as misconduct even when it was innocent. Ioannidis (2005) described how the reliability of a field depends on the ability to check its work, and these records make that checking possible.
