Errors are found in published work, and the way they are handled reveals the integrity of a field. An error that is discovered and corrected openly strengthens the record, while one that is concealed corrupts every study that relies on it. Ioannidis (2005) described how the literature accumulates unreliable findings when corrections are slow or absent, and Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the readiness to correct is a sign of a self-correcting science.

The authors have the first responsibility. When they discover an error in their own published work, they should acknowledge it promptly and state what was wrong and what the correct value is. The correction should be proportionate to the error, and it should reach the readers who used the original result. Cumming (2014) argues that the correction should report the corrected estimate and its interval, so that readers can see the effect of the error on the conclusion.

A correction is appropriate when the error changes a detail but not the main conclusion. A retraction is appropriate when the findings are unreliable and cannot be corrected, for example when the data were fabricated or when the analysis that supports the conclusion is shown to be invalid. An expression of concern flags a paper whose reliability is in doubt while an investigation proceeds, so that readers are warned before the matter is resolved.

Those who used the affected results should be notified, since they may have built on an error. Peng (2011) argued that the reproducibility of a result allows such errors to be detected, because the analysis can be rerun on the original data. Journals and authors should make the correction visible, and the recognition that correcting the record is a sign of a healthy science, rather than a failure, should guide how researchers respond when they find that they have been wrong.
