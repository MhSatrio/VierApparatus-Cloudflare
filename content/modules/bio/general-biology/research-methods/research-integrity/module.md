Research integrity is the commitment to honest, rigorous and accountable scientific practice. It rests on a small set of values that every researcher should know: honesty in reporting what was done and found, rigour in the design and analysis, transparency in sharing methods and data, and accountability for the work that is published under one's name. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that these values are the foundation of a reliable literature, and their violation undermines every study that depends on the record.

Misconduct includes fabrication, which is the invention of data or results, falsification, which is the manipulation of data or of their presentation, and plagiarism, which is the appropriation of another's words or ideas. These acts are rare, but their consequences are severe, because a fabricated result can mislead many later studies. Ioannidis (2005) described how the literature is vulnerable to error that is not detected, and the procedures that guard against misconduct protect the field as a whole.

Questionable research practices fall short of misconduct but distort the evidence in similar ways. Selective reporting of favourable outcomes, flexible analysis that tries many routes until one is significant, and undisclosed exclusions of inconvenient observations all produce findings that are more favourable than the truth. Cumming (2014) argues that these practices are best countered by reporting the full set of estimates with their intervals, since the selection that produces an inflated result is then visible.

Institutions must have procedures for investigating allegations that protect both the accused and the complainant. An allegation that is investigated fairly and confidentially, with a stated process and a right to respond, protects the innocent from unjust damage and encourages honest reports. Peng (2011) argued that the reproducibility of research is a safeguard of integrity, and a process that asks for the data and code to be examined can establish the facts quickly. Supervisors and laboratory leaders have a particular responsibility to model and teach these values, since trainees learn integrity from the behaviour of those around them.
