An adaptive design allows an experiment to change in response to the data it generates, and a sequential design examines the data at planned points. These approaches can save animals, participants and money, because an experiment can stop early when a clear effect is seen or when a result is unlikely to emerge. The price is that every change must be planned in advance, because the act of looking at accumulating data affects the error rate of the final conclusion.

Each planned interim analysis has a stopping boundary, which is the strength of evidence required to stop. Boundaries are set so that the overall probability of a false positive remains at the chosen level, however many times the data are examined. Ioannidis (2005) described how repeated looks at data, with a stop whenever significance appears, can make nearly any hypothesis appear supported, and the boundaries prevent this.

Sample size re-estimation allows the number of units to be increased if the observed variability is larger than assumed. This protects the power of the study against a poor initial assumption. The rule for re-estimation must be specified in advance, and the final analysis must account for the change in sample size, since it alters the properties of the test.

An experiment that stops early for a clear effect tends to overestimate the size of that effect, because the chance fluctuations that led to the stop are retained in the estimate. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval should be reported for the final sample, and the bias of early stopping should be acknowledged. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that every interim look and its decision must be reported, so that readers can see how the final estimate was reached.
