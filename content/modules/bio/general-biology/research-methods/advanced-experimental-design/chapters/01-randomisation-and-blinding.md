Randomisation assigns experimental units to groups by chance, so that the groups differ only by chance in the factors that were not measured. Its value depends on the allocation being unpredictable. If the investigator can foresee the next assignment, the investigator may consciously or unconsciously place favourable units in one group, and the randomisation then protects nothing. The sequence should therefore be generated by a method that is not under the control of those who enrol the units.

Concealment is the step that keeps the next allocation hidden until the unit is committed to the study. Sealed envelopes or a central allocation service are common methods, and each should be described in the report. Ioannidis (2005) described how the failure to conceal allocation can exaggerate apparent effects, which is why concealment is treated as a distinct requirement from randomisation itself.

Stratified randomisation balances known prognostic factors across groups, which is useful when the number of units is small and an imbalance by chance would be consequential. Restricted randomisation, which ensures that groups are of equal size at regular points, can be used for the same reason. Cumming (2014) argues that balance improves the precision of the comparison, since it reduces the variation that would otherwise be attributed to the factor under study.

Blinding prevents the expectations of investigators and assessors from influencing measurements. When the intervention itself cannot be hidden, the outcome assessor can often still be blinded, and this is frequently the more important safeguard. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that blinding should be reported with the number of people who were blinded and an assessment of whether the blinding worked, since an unsuccessful blind provides little protection.
