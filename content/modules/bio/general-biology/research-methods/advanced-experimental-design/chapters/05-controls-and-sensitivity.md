A control establishes that the experimental system is working. A positive control is a treatment with a known effect, and its failure to produce that effect shows that the assay could not have detected the change under study. A negative control receives everything except the treatment, and it shows the level of background signal. Without these controls, a null result cannot be distinguished from an assay that was never capable of detecting the effect, and Ioannidis (2005) described this as a common cause of false negatives in the literature.

The vehicle control receives the solvent or carrier in which the treatment was delivered, with no active agent. It isolates the effect of the treatment from the effect of its vehicle, which may itself alter the system. Many apparent effects of compounds have been traced to the solvent, and the vehicle control is the only way to exclude this explanation. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that controls of this kind should be described in full in the methods.

An assay has a dynamic range within which it responds proportionally to the quantity measured, and a limit of detection below which it cannot distinguish signal from noise. A null result is informative only if the expected effect is above the limit of detection. Cumming (2014) argues that the precision of the assay should be reported alongside the effect, since a precise measurement can still be too imprecise to detect a small change.

The signal-to-noise ratio determines whether a small effect can be separated from the background variation of the system. It should be estimated from the control data and reported, so that a reader can judge whether the experiment could have detected an effect of the size the proposal expects. Control results deserve the same reporting detail as the experimental results, since they are the evidence that the experiment was capable of answering its question.
