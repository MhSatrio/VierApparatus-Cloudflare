A report of an experiment should allow a reader to judge what was done and to repeat it. The methods must state the question, the design, the experimental units, the randomisation, the blinding and the controls. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the methods are the means by which a result is checked, and an omission in them is an obstacle to every later use of the finding.

The number of units analysed must be reported at each stage, from those allocated to those analysed, together with the number excluded and the reason for each exclusion. A flow of units through the study makes exclusions visible, and an exclusion that changes the result is a serious concern. Cumming (2014) argues that the effect estimates should be reported with their intervals, since a p-value alone does not show the size or the precision of the effect.

The statistical model, its assumptions and the checks made on those assumptions must be described, so that an independent analyst can reproduce the result. The raw data and the analysis code should be deposited with identifiers, and their location stated in the report. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only when the computations that produced it are available, and this requirement applies to every experiment that is reported.

The discussion should acknowledge the limitations of the design that bear on the interpretation, including the limits of the population, the duration and the sensitivity of the assays. Ioannidis (2005) described how overconfident conclusions from small or narrow experiments mislead the field, and an honest account of limitations protects readers from that error. Reporting every planned analysis, including those that gave null results, completes the account.
