A grant application is a competitive argument for funding, and it is judged by reviewers who read many applications and have limited time. The application must make its case quickly: what the question is, why it matters, how it will be answered and whether the team can do it. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the quality of the methods is what reviewers should weigh most heavily, and an application that makes its rigour visible has an advantage over one that relies on the importance of its topic alone.

Choosing the right funder is the first decision. Each funder has a remit, a set of review criteria and a typical scale of award, and an application that does not match these is unlikely to succeed however well written. The applicant should read the criteria closely and frame the aims in the terms the funder uses, while remaining honest about what the programme can achieve. Ioannidis (2005) described how the overstatement of expected findings damages credibility, so the aims should be realistic.

The approach is the core of the application, and it must specify the design, the sample size, the analysis and the controls with enough detail for reviewers to judge their adequacy. Cumming (2014) argues that a grant should state the precision the study will achieve, since this links the design to the question. Preliminary data that establish the feasibility of the key methods are among the most persuasive additions an applicant can make.

The application should present its risks and limitations, together with the contingencies that protect the main aim. A reviewer is more confident in an applicant who identifies a weakness and explains how it will be managed than in one who presents the project as free of risk. The application is then revised in response to peer feedback, and each change should be explained clearly in any resubmission.
