The budget justification explains each cost in the application, and it is the place where reviewers check whether the requested resources match the work described. Each line should be tied to an aim and to a task, and the calculation should show how the cost follows from the number of samples, replicates, personnel months or units of equipment time. A figure that cannot be traced to the design raises the question of whether the approach has been planned in enough detail.

Funders define directly incurred costs and indirect costs in different ways, and the applicant must follow the definitions in the call. Mixing them can lead to an application that is ineligible or that is reduced without explanation. The justification should use the funder's categories and state clearly which costs are requested and which are provided by the institution or by other sources.

Equipment requires particular justification. A reviewer will ask whether the equipment is necessary for the aims or whether it is convenient, and an application that buys a new instrument when access to an existing one would suffice will be marked down. Where equipment is shared, the application should describe the arrangement and its cost. Cumming (2014) argues that the precision of the measurements depends on the instruments used, so the justification should link the equipment to the precision the aims require.

Inflation and contingency should be stated transparently, with the rate and the reason for each allowance. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that openness about resources makes a programme easier to assess, and hidden contingencies undermine trust. Ioannidis (2005) described how the presentation of a research programme as more certain than it is weakens the case for it, and an honest budget that acknowledges uncertainty is more credible than one that does not.
