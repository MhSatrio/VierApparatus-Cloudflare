Significance is the argument that the answer matters. It rests on a precise statement of the gap in knowledge, supported by the best available evidence. A gap that is described only as an absence of work is weak, whereas one that identifies a conflict in the results, an untested population or a measurement that has never been validated gives reviewers a clear reason to fund the work. The argument should explain what the field will gain and for whom, so that the benefit of the answer is visible.

The specific aims follow from the gap, and they should be few. Two or three aims is a common and sensible number, since each aim must be achieved within the funding period and each must be assessable at the end. An aim that is too broad, such as to understand the mechanism of a disease, cannot be judged, while an aim that names a measurable outcome and a population can be. Ioannidis (2005) described how overambitious programmes produce findings that are incomplete, and a narrower set of aims protects against this.

An aim describes what will be done or delivered, whereas a hypothesis states an expected result. Both are useful, but they serve different purposes. Some schemes require hypotheses, and others prefer aims that are framed around the estimation of an effect. Cumming (2014) argues that an aim to estimate an effect with a stated precision is often more informative than a hypothesis that asks only whether an effect exists.

Each aim should be linked to the approach that will address it, so that a reviewer can see how the work will answer the question. The link allows the reviewer to judge whether the approach is adequate for each aim, and it exposes any aim that the approach does not address. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the connection between the question and the method is the basis of a credible plan, and the aims section should make that connection explicit.
