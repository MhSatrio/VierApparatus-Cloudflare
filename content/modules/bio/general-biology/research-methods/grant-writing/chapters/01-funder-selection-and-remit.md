Every funder states a remit, which describes the kinds of research it supports, and a set of criteria by which applications are assessed. The remit and the criteria are not the same thing. A funder may have a broad remit in biology but assess applications primarily on the quality of their methods and the track record of the team, and an applicant must know which of these governs the decision. Reading the call closely is therefore the first step of any application, and the applicant should note each criterion explicitly.

The scale and duration of the programme must match the typical award. A programme designed for five years and a large team will not be funded by a scheme that awards small grants for one year, however well the science is argued. Published success rates and the experience of previous applicants indicate how competitive a scheme is, and the applicant should judge whether the programme can be made strong enough to succeed. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that funding decisions should rest on the rigour of the proposed work, and applicants should look for schemes that assess this directly.

Partnership and co-funding can extend a programme beyond the reach of a single award. Where another funder shares an interest, the arrangements should be agreed in advance and described in the application, since reviewers will ask how the programmes are coordinated. A shared programme can also be more vulnerable to delay, and the risk should be stated.

Deciding not to apply is a legitimate outcome of this assessment. A weak fit consumes time that could be spent preparing a better-matched application, and a rejected application may not be reconsidered for some time. Ioannidis (2005) described how the overstatement of findings to fit an expected narrative damages a field, and a similar overstatement to fit a funder's remit should be avoided. Cumming (2014) argues that the precision expected from the study should be stated honestly, so that the funder can judge the value of the investment.
