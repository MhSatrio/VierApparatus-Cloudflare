Impact is the difference a programme makes beyond its own publications. It may be academic, changing how other researchers think or work, practical, changing a clinical guideline or an agricultural practice, or public, changing what citizens know or how they engage with science. A credible impact statement names the kind of impact expected, identifies the people or institutions who would act on it and explains the route by which the findings would reach them.

Claims of impact must be realistic. An application that promises to transform a field within three years invites scepticism, while one that identifies the specific advances the work can achieve, and the evidence that would show them, is more persuasive. Ioannidis (2005) described how exaggerated claims of importance damage the credibility of research, and a sober statement of expected impact protects the applicant as well as the funder.

The dissemination plan should commit to publishing all results, including null and unexpected findings, since a plan that publishes only the favourable results distorts the evidence. Open access publication, the deposition of data and code and the sharing of materials should be described with their routes and their costs. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that such commitments are the core of responsible dissemination, and a funder is right to expect them.

Engagement with users and stakeholders can make dissemination effective, because findings that are discussed with those who will use them are more likely to be applied. The plan should name the stakeholders, describe how they will be involved and state the outputs intended for them. Cumming (2014) argues that the findings should be communicated with their estimates and intervals, so that users can judge how much confidence the evidence deserves.
