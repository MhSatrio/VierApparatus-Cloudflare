The approach section describes how each aim will be achieved, and it is the part of the application that reviewers scrutinise most closely. For each aim the section should state the design, the sample size and its justification, the controls, the analysis and the precision that the study is expected to achieve. A reviewer who can reconstruct the study from this description is in a position to judge it, and one who cannot will usually score it down.

Preliminary data show that the key methods work and that the effect is large enough to be detected. A pilot study with a small sample can establish feasibility, provided its limitations are stated and its sample size is reported. Cumming (2014) argues that an estimate from a small pilot has a wide interval, and the application should say so, since a reviewer will otherwise assume a precision the pilot cannot support.

Preliminary data must be presented honestly. Results selected to show only success, or presented without the number of observations on which they rest, mislead the reviewer and undermine the applicant's credibility when the full study is run. Ioannidis (2005) described how selective presentation inflates the apparent strength of a finding, and the same risk applies to a grant that relies on pilot results.

The approach should state the analysis that will be used for each aim, and where possible the analysis plan should be registered before the main data are collected. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that pre-specification protects the value of a study against flexible analysis, and a reviewer who sees the plan fixed in advance can trust the eventual result more. The approach section is complete when every aim is linked to a design and an analysis that a reviewer can assess.
