Reviewers assess the team as well as the science, because a programme is delivered by people and their expertise determines whether the work can be done. The team description should show how each member's skills map onto the aims, so that every task has a named person with the expertise to carry it out. A gap in expertise, such as no one trained in the statistical analysis, is a risk that the application must acknowledge and address, perhaps through a collaborator or a consultant.

Management and governance describe how decisions are made and how progress is tracked. The application should state who is responsible for each work package, how the members communicate, how disagreements are resolved and who makes the final decision. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that clear responsibilities are part of the transparency on which a credible programme depends, and an undefined governance structure invites difficulty when problems arise.

Milestones allow the funder and the team to see progress and to detect delay early. Each milestone should be verifiable, such as the completion of a validated method or the collection of a stated number of observations, and it should be linked to the aims. Ioannidis (2005) described how studies that drift from their plans without notice produce uncertain results, and milestones with dates reduce that drift.

Training and career development are part of a responsible team plan, particularly for early-career researchers who may carry much of the practical work. The plan should describe the training each member will receive and the opportunities for their development. The application should also state the facilities and collaborations on which the programme depends, and the arrangements for data management, ethics and safety. Cumming (2014) argues that the precision expected from the work should be linked to the resources available to achieve it.
