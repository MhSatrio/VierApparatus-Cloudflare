A reviewer reads the summary first, and often decides on the application from it. The summary must state the problem, the aim, the approach and the expected outcome in about a page, using plain language and the terms the funder uses. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that clarity in the statement of methods supports the transparency that funders seek, and the summary is where that clarity is first tested.

The opening paragraph of the main text has a particular role. It establishes why the question matters, and it should do so in the first few sentences, since reviewers form an impression quickly. A claim of importance that is supported by the state of the evidence, rather than by general enthusiasm, is more convincing. Ioannidis (2005) described how claims of importance that exceed the evidence weaken a proposal, and the opening should be measured.

The sections should follow the order that reviewers expect, with headings that correspond to the criteria. A reviewer scoring the approach should find it under a clear heading, and a reviewer checking the feasibility should find the timetable and the team without searching. Consistency matters: a sample size stated as one number in the summary and another in the approach will undermine confidence in the whole application, so every figure should be checked across sections.

The application must be checked against the funder's template and page limits before submission. An application that exceeds a limit may be rejected without review, and one that omits a required section is incomplete. Cumming (2014) argues that the precision of each estimate should be stated where it is first used, so that the reader can follow the logic from the question to the expected interval.
