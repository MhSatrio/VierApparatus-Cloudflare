Peer feedback on an unsuccessful application is among the most valuable information an applicant can receive, and the resubmission is the chance to act on it. The first task is to read the comments for their substance. Some concerns appear in several reviews, and these usually point to the most important weaknesses. Others come from a single reviewer, and they may reflect a particular expertise or a misunderstanding that clarity can resolve.

Each comment must be classified. A comment that questions the design, the sample size or the analysis requires a change to the application, and the change should be made in the approach itself rather than only described in the response. A comment that asks for clarification requires a rewritten passage that answers the question directly. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that a weakness in the methods is the most serious criticism a proposal can receive, and it should be corrected in substance.

The response to reviewers is a structured document that addresses each comment in turn. For each one it states whether the application was changed, where the change appears and what it was, or why no change was made. A reviewer who sees that every comment has been addressed, even where the applicant disagrees, is more inclined to recommend the revised application. Cumming (2014) argues that precision should be stated where it has been changed, so the reviewer can see the effect of the revision on the expected interval.

Where a concern is not accepted, the applicant should explain why with evidence, not with dismissal. New preliminary data or a pilot study can strengthen the application where a reviewer doubts feasibility. Ioannidis (2005) described how an application that overstates its findings invites doubt, and a resubmission that tempers its claims in response to criticism is usually stronger. The funder's rules on resubmission and page limits must be followed, since a resubmission that breaks them is rejected before review.
