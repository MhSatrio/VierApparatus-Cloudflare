Robustness is the capacity of a method to give the same result when its conditions vary slightly, as they inevitably do in routine use. Temperature, the pH of a buffer, the time of an incubation and the lot of a reagent can each shift the result, and a method that is sensitive to them will fail in other laboratories or on other days. The robustness study identifies these factors before the method is adopted, so that the sensitive ones can be controlled and written into the procedure.

The study varies the factors within small ranges around their nominal values, using a designed experiment that estimates the effect of each factor and some of their interactions efficiently. The effects are compared with the precision of the method, since an effect smaller than the random variation is unimportant. Cumming (2014) argues that each effect should be reported with its interval, so that a reader can judge whether a small effect is distinguishable from noise.

System suitability tests are short checks run with each batch of samples to confirm that the method is performing as it did during validation. A reference standard is measured, and its response must fall within the criteria before the batch results are accepted. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that such routine checks are a practical form of transparency, because they show that the method was working when each result was produced.

Revalidation is triggered by changes in the equipment, the reagents, the analyst or the procedure. The extent of revalidation depends on the size of the change, and a full validation may be needed for a new method while a partial check suffices for a new reagent lot. Ioannidis (2005) described how methods that are changed without revalidation can drift away from their validated performance, and the record should link every result to the version of the method that produced it.
