Accuracy is established by comparison with a material whose true value is known. A certified reference material carries a value and an uncertainty determined by an accepted standard, and a method that reproduces that value within its uncertainty is shown to be accurate for that material. The reference must be traceable, because a reference whose value is itself uncertain cannot establish the accuracy of a new method. Ioannidis (2005) described how the failure to check measurements against a trusted standard allows systematic errors to persist unnoticed.

The recovery experiment adds a known quantity of analyte to a sample and measures how much is recovered. A recovery near one hundred per cent indicates that the sample matrix does not remove or mask the analyte. Recovery is reported at several levels across the range, with the number of replicates at each level, and the average recovery is given with its confidence interval. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval conveys the uncertainty of the recovery better than a single average.

Comparison with an established method uses paired samples measured by both methods, and the agreement is assessed by a difference plot or by regression. A regression that shows a slope different from one or an intercept different from zero reveals proportional or constant bias. The difference between methods should be estimated with its interval, since a visual impression of agreement can conceal a consistent offset.

Matrix effects arise when the composition of a sample alters the response of the method, so that a method accurate for one sample type is biased for another. Testing a new sample type requires its own recovery or comparison experiment. Peng (2011) argued that the conditions under which a method was shown to be accurate must be described, so that a reader does not extend the conclusion to samples for which it was never tested.
