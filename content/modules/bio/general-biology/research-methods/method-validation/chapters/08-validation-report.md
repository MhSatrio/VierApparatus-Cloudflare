A validation report is the evidence on which a method is adopted, and it must allow a reader to judge the conclusion without repeating the work. It begins with the intended use and the acceptance criteria, which were set in the protocol before testing. The description of the method must be complete enough that a competent analyst could perform it, including the reagents, the instrument settings and the calculation. Peng (2011) argued that reproducibility depends on this completeness, and a method that cannot be repeated cannot be validated in any meaningful sense.

Each performance characteristic is presented with its data, its estimate and its interval. A table of the precision results at each level, a plot of the calibration with its residuals and a summary of the recovery experiments are the usual forms. Cumming (2014) argues that the intervals should appear alongside the estimates, so that the uncertainty of each characteristic is visible at a glance.

Each result is compared with the acceptance criterion set in advance, and the report states whether it was met. Any deviation from the protocol must be listed with its justification. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that deviations are a normal part of practice, but they must be disclosed, since an undisclosed deviation can change the conclusion of the validation.

The report states the scope of the validation: the range, the limits, the sample types and the conditions under which the method has been shown to perform. The conclusion that the method is fit for use is limited to this scope. Ioannidis (2005) described how conclusions are often extended beyond the evidence that supports them, and a clearly bounded scope is the safeguard against that error. The conclusion is signed and dated, so that it can be linked to the version of the method it describes.
