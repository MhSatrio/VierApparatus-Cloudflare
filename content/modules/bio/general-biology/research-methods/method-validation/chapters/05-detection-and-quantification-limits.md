The limit of detection is the smallest amount of analyte that can be distinguished from a blank with a stated level of confidence. Measurements of the blank vary, and a signal must exceed that variation before it can be said to indicate the presence of the analyte. The limit is commonly estimated from the standard deviation of replicate blank measurements, multiplied by a factor that controls the risk of false detection. Peng (2011) argued that the factor and the method of estimation should be stated, so that the limit can be reproduced and compared.

The limit of quantification is higher. It is the smallest amount that can be measured with acceptable precision and accuracy, and it is the lowest value that should be reported as a number. Between the two limits lies a region in which the analyte can be detected but not measured reliably. A value in this region should be reported as detected but below the limit of quantification, rather than as a precise concentration.

The limits can also be estimated from the calibration curve, using the standard error of the regression and the slope. This approach uses information from the whole curve, but it assumes that the variation is constant across the range, which may not hold. Cumming (2014) argues that the uncertainty of each limit should be reported with its interval, since a limit is itself an estimate.

The estimated limits must be confirmed by replicate measurements of a sample at the limit. If the measured precision at that level does not meet the acceptance criterion, the limit of quantification must be raised. Ioannidis (2005) described how the reporting of values below the reliable range produces a literature of apparently precise results that cannot be reproduced, and the stated limits protect against this.
