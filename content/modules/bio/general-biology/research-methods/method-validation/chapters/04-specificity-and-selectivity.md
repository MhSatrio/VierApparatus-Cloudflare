Specificity is the ability of a method to measure the intended analyte when other substances are present. A method that responds to a related compound will report a value that is partly the analyte and partly the interferent, and the result will be biased upward. Specificity is therefore demonstrated, not assumed, and the validation must state which substances were tested and what effect each had. Ioannidis (2005) described how unrecognised interference can produce findings that are systematically wrong, and specificity testing guards against this.

The test set includes structurally related compounds, metabolites and degradation products, since these are the most likely to interfere. Each is added to a blank matrix, and the response is compared with the response of the analyte alone. A difference beyond the acceptance criterion shows that the method is not specific for the analyte in that matrix. Cumming (2014) argues that the magnitude of any interference should be estimated with its interval, so that its practical consequence can be judged.

Spiking a real sample with potential interferents tests the method under realistic conditions. The bias caused by the interferent is measured at the concentrations that the sample is likely to contain, which may differ from those tested in a clean solvent. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the conditions of the test should be described in full, so that the scope of the specificity claim is clear.

A second method that works on a different principle, an orthogonal method, provides independent confirmation of identity. Agreement between two methods that do not share an interference is strong evidence that the measurement is specific. A single method cannot establish specificity for every substance, and the claim should be limited to the substances that were tested, as Peng (2011) argued for reproducible reporting.
