The intended use of a method is the decision it must support. A method that will classify a sample as above or below a safety threshold needs its accuracy near that threshold, whereas a method that tracks a trend needs precision across the range of interest. Stating the decision first determines which errors matter, and it prevents a validation from testing characteristics that make no difference to the outcome.

From the intended use follow the numerical acceptance criteria. The range that must be covered, the largest tolerable bias and the largest tolerable imprecision are set in advance, and each is expressed as a number that the validation can meet or fail. Cumming (2014) argues that the criteria should be stated as intervals, so that the uncertainty of each characteristic is judged against what the decision requires.

The validation protocol records the intended use, the criteria and the design of the tests before any testing is done. The protocol is signed or dated so that its timing can be shown. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that pre-specification protects against the flexibility that allows a method to be judged by criteria chosen to suit its results.

Setting criteria after the results are known invites the choice of a standard that the method happens to pass. Ioannidis (2005) described how such post hoc choices produce findings that look reliable but are not, and the same logic applies to measurement methods. Any change to the criteria after testing must be recorded with its reason and reported, and the original criteria must remain visible in the record.
