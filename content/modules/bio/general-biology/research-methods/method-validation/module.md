A method is validated to show that it produces results fit for a stated purpose. Validation begins with a clear statement of that purpose, since a method adequate for screening samples may be inadequate for quantifying them. The performance characteristics that matter, such as accuracy, precision and sensitivity, are then identified, and the method is tested against criteria set before the testing begins. Cumming (2014) argues that a validation should report the uncertainty of each characteristic, not only whether it met a threshold.

Accuracy is the closeness of a result to the true value, and it is assessed against reference materials of known composition. Precision is the closeness of repeated results to one another, and it is assessed through repeatability within a run and intermediate precision across days, analysts and instruments. A method can be precise and inaccurate, so both must be assessed. Ioannidis (2005) described how the apparent consistency of a measurement can conceal a systematic error that only a reference material reveals.

Specificity shows that the method measures the intended analyte and not something that resembles it. The limits of detection and quantification define the smallest amount that can be reliably detected and reliably measured. Peng (2011) argued that such limits must be stated so that a reader can judge whether a result near the limit is meaningful, and reporting a value below the limit as a precise measurement is an error.

Linearity and the range over which the response is linear are assessed with a series of standards. Robustness tests small deliberate changes in conditions, such as temperature or pH, to find which conditions must be controlled. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the protocol and its acceptance criteria should be recorded before testing, and that the validation should be repeated when the method, the reagents or the instrument change.
