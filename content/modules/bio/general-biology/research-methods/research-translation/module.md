Research translation is the movement of findings from the laboratory or the study into practice, policy and products. It is a long and uncertain process, and many promising findings never reach the people they were intended to help. Ioannidis (2005) described how the instability of early results is a principal reason for this failure, since findings that are acted upon before they are replicated often prove smaller or false, and the cost of acting on them is borne by those who trusted them.

The translation of a finding passes through stages, from discovery to validation, early application and adoption at scale. Each stage requires different evidence. Discovery requires a plausible and tested hypothesis, validation requires replication in conditions close to the intended use, and early application requires an estimate of the benefit and of the harm with their uncertainty. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval of each estimate should be part of the evidence at every stage, since a benefit reported without its precision cannot be weighed against its cost.

Higgins & Thompson (2002) showed how the heterogeneity among studies can make a pooled estimate misleading when the conditions of application differ from those of the studies. Translation therefore requires attention to whether the evidence applies to the population, setting and conditions in which the finding will be used. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that a translated finding should be transparent about the conditions under which it was established, so that users can judge its relevance.

Regulatory and ethical frameworks govern clinical and environmental applications, and they exist to protect those who would bear the risk. Engagement with users, practitioners and communities shapes an application so that it serves them, and it provides an evaluation that is grounded in use. The chapters that follow examine the stages of translation, the evidence each requires, the frameworks that govern it and the monitoring that continues after adoption.
