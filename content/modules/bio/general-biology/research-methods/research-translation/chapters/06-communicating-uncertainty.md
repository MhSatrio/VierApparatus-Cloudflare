Decision-makers and the public rely on research findings, and the way those findings are communicated determines whether they are used well. A finding that is described as certain when it is uncertain may lead to a decision that the evidence does not support, while one that is described as hopeless when it is merely uncertain may lead to a missed opportunity. Communication must therefore convey both the finding and the degree of confidence it deserves, and Cumming (2014) argues that the interval is the means of conveying that confidence.

The communicator should distinguish what is established, what is probable and what is unknown. An established finding has been replicated and its estimate is precise. A probable finding has supporting evidence but remains uncertain. An unknown is a question that the evidence does not yet address. Ioannidis (2005) described how the failure to make these distinctions leads to claims that outrun the data, and the public discussion of research often blurs them.

Estimates should be presented with their intervals in forms that non-specialists can understand. A range of likely values, shown visually or in plain words, conveys more than a single number or a statement of significance. The language should avoid implying more certainty than the evidence supports, such as describing a tentative result as a proof. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that transparency about the limits of evidence is necessary for the trust that research requires.

The choice of measure can mislead. A relative risk reduction of fifty per cent sounds large, but it may correspond to a fall from two cases in a thousand to one in a thousand, which is a small absolute change. Both the relative and the absolute measure should be reported, so that the audience can judge the size of the effect. Disagreement among studies should be acknowledged, and when the evidence changes, earlier public claims should be corrected. Researchers who speak to the media have a responsibility to represent the state of the evidence accurately, even when a simpler and more striking account would attract more attention.
