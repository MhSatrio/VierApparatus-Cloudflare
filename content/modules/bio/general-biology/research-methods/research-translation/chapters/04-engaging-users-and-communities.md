An application of research is judged by the people who use it or who are affected by it, and their judgement may differ from that of the researchers. Engagement brings these people into the process from the design stage, so that the application addresses problems they recognise and measures outcomes they value. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that research serves its purpose when it is open to those it affects, and engagement makes that openness practical.

Outcomes should be co-designed with users. A researcher may measure a biomarker that is easy to record, while a user cares about a symptom or a yield that the biomarker does not capture. Asking users which outcomes matter, and measuring them, produces evidence that bears on the decision the users actually face. Ioannidis (2005) described how the choice of outcomes that are easy to measure can distort the evidence, and the involvement of users corrects this distortion.

Local knowledge and the priorities of communities deserve respect. Communities often understand their own circumstances in ways that outside researchers do not, and an application that ignores this knowledge may fail in practice even when it succeeds in a trial. The engagement must be genuine, with decisions shared where appropriate, rather than a formality that follows a decision already made.

The evidence must be communicated in terms that users can understand and act upon. A statement of benefit without its uncertainty, or a risk described in technical language that the affected people cannot follow, does not inform the decision. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval of each estimate should be communicated in a form that is accessible, since users who know the range of likely outcomes can decide more wisely than those given a single number.

Expectations must be managed honestly, so that an application is not presented as more certain or more beneficial than the evidence allows. Users should be able to report problems, and the mechanisms by which they can do so should influence later decisions. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that openness about the limits of evidence is part of the trust on which engagement depends.
