Translation is justified by its benefit, and that benefit must be evaluated rather than assumed. The first requirement is that the intended outcomes be defined at the outset, in terms that matter to users, so that the evaluation has a clear target. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that pre-specified outcomes protect an evaluation against the flexibility that can make an application appear successful, and this principle applies to the evaluation of impact as it applies to research.

A before-and-after comparison is weak, because outcomes change for many reasons besides the application. A comparative design estimates the benefit against a counterfactual, which is what would have happened without the application. Cumming (2014) argues that the difference between the application and the counterfactual should be reported with its interval, since the benefit is an estimate subject to uncertainty, not a fixed quantity.

A stepped or staggered introduction, in which the application is introduced to different groups at different times, allows the comparison of early and late adopters. Such designs can separate the effect of the application from the effect of the passage of time, and they can be ethically acceptable when the application is expected to help and the order of introduction is determined by fair criteria. Ioannidis (2005) described how the failure to compare with a proper counterfactual has led many apparent successes to be overstated.

The evaluation must measure unintended consequences as well as intended ones, and the cost of the application as well as its benefit. It should also examine whether the benefit is distributed equitably across groups, since an application that helps some people while harming others may not be justified overall. The results should be used to decide whether to continue, modify or withdraw the application, and the decision should be published so that others can learn from it.
