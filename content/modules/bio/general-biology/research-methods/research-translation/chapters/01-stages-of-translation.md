Translation begins with discovery, in which a finding suggests that an intervention or a process may be useful. At this stage the finding rests on a small number of studies, often with exploratory analyses, and its estimate is likely to be inflated. Ioannidis (2005) described how the first report of a finding is typically the largest, and a finding should not be treated as established on the strength of its discovery alone. The stage ends when the finding is specific enough to be tested under defined conditions.

Validation tests the finding by independent replication in conditions close to those of intended use. A replication that is pre-registered, adequately powered and conducted by a team unconnected with the discovery is strong evidence, and a failure to replicate is equally informative. Cumming (2014) argues that the replication should be judged by whether its estimate agrees with the original within the uncertainty of both, rather than by whether it reaches significance on its own.

Early application places the finding in a limited setting, such as a single clinic, farm or site, and evaluates both its benefit and its harm. The evaluation must measure the outcomes that matter to users, not only those that are easiest to record, and it must report the uncertainty of each estimate. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the pre-specification of outcomes and of the criteria for success protects this evaluation from the flexibility that can make an application appear to work.

The decision to adopt at scale requires evidence of consistent benefit across the settings in which the finding will be used. A benefit observed in one population may not appear in another, and the variation across settings must be measured. Higgins & Thompson (2002) described how heterogeneity among studies can be quantified, and the same logic applies to heterogeneity among settings. Adoption at scale should be governed by criteria stated in advance, so that a finding does not advance on enthusiasm alone.
