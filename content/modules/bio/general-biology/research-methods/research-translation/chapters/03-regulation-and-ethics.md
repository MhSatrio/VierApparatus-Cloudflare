Applications of research are governed by regulatory frameworks that exist to protect those who will bear their risks. Approval processes assess safety and efficacy before a product, practice or intervention is used, and the evidence required is set out in guidelines that reflect the consequences of error. A process that is too slow delays benefit to those who need it, and a process that is too fast exposes them to harm. The balance between the speed of access and the certainty of safety is a central judgement of every framework.

The evidence that supports an approval must be complete. Trials that are conducted for approval should be registered before they begin, and their results should be reported whatever they show. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the registration of trials prevents the selective publication of favourable results, and a regulator that approves a product on the basis of only the favourable trials has been misled. Ioannidis (2005) described how the suppression of negative results inflates the apparent benefit of interventions, and the completeness of the evidence is an ethical requirement as well as a regulatory one.

Standards of good practice, such as good laboratory, manufacturing and clinical practice, govern the quality of the evidence that is produced. They require records, validated methods, trained staff and independent oversight, and they exist so that the data on which an approval rests can be trusted. Cumming (2014) argues that the precision of the estimates is part of that quality, since an approval based on imprecise data carries a risk that the framework must weigh.

Developers and regulators have a duty to communicate the risks of an application clearly to those who will bear them. Consent, labelling and public information should give the people affected an accurate account of what is known and what is uncertain. The obligation extends to the monitoring of an application after approval, since rare or delayed harms may appear only in wide use. The regulatory and ethical frameworks are instruments for protecting people, and their effectiveness depends on the completeness and honesty of the evidence presented to them.
