Adoption is not the end of the evidence. The trials and early studies that supported an application were limited in size and duration, and rare or delayed harms may appear only when the application is used widely over time. Monitoring after adoption is the means of detecting such harms, and it is an obligation on those who introduce an application. Ioannidis (2005) described how the early evidence for a finding can be incomplete, and monitoring extends the evidence as use grows.

Outcome data can be collected in routine use, provided that privacy and consent are protected. The data should be collected in a structured form that allows comparison with the expectations set by the original evidence. Cumming (2014) argues that the observed rate of an outcome should be reported with its interval, since a small number of events in routine use may be too few to distinguish a real change from chance.

A divergence between observed outcomes and the expected ones should trigger an investigation. The investigation asks whether the difference is real, whether it is due to changed conditions, to differences in the population or to chance, and what action is needed. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the pre-specification of expectations allows divergence to be detected objectively, rather than explained away after it is noticed.

Benefit can decline as conditions change, for example when a pest develops resistance or a population changes its characteristics. Monitoring should detect such declines, and the application should be modified or withdrawn when its risks outweigh its benefits. The results of monitoring should be reported publicly, so that the evidence is updated and other users can make informed decisions. Monitoring is the continuation of the evidence process after the decision to adopt, and it protects those who use the application from harm that the original evidence could not foresee.
