Before a finding is applied, the evidence for it should be judged against standards set in advance. The most important standard is replication: a finding established by one study, however carefully conducted, is not yet reliable enough for widespread use. Ioannidis (2005) described how many findings fail on replication, and the application of an unreplicated finding exposes users to the risk that it will not hold.

The estimate of benefit and of harm should be reported with its interval, and a significance test alone is insufficient. A test that reaches significance may describe an effect too small to matter, and a test that fails to reach significance may describe an effect that is uncertain but potentially large. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval conveys both the size and the precision of the effect, and decisions depend on both.

Where an existing standard of care or practice exists, the intervention should be compared with that standard rather than with no treatment. A comparison with no treatment can show an effect that is irrelevant to the choice users face, since the existing standard may already produce the same benefit. The comparison must be designed so that it answers the practical question.

The effect must be large enough to matter to the people who would use it. Clinicians and practitioners often express benefit as the number needed to treat, which is the number of people who must receive the intervention for one to benefit, and the equivalent measure in other fields should be considered. Higgins & Thompson (2002) described how the variation among studies can make an average benefit misleading for some subgroups, and the attention to subgroups for whom the benefit is absent or the harm is greater is part of the evidence.

The explicit statement of what remains uncertain allows decisions to be made with the uncertainty in view. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that transparency about the limits of the evidence is a condition of its responsible use, and users should be told what is known, what is not known and how confident the evidence allows them to be.
