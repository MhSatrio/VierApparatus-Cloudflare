A critical literature review asks what the published research establishes, how firmly it does so and where the evidence is missing. It is more than a summary, since it judges the studies that it describes, and its judgements must be reasoned and transparent. Page et al. (2021) set out the standards for reporting reviews in the PRISMA statement, and those standards describe the transparency that a critical review should also meet, even when it is not a formal systematic review.

The topic begins with the search, which must be designed to find the relevant studies without omission and without selection that favours a conclusion. The chapters that follow examine the appraisal of each study's design and quality, the recognition of bias in the literature, the evaluation of statistical claims, the synthesis of disagreeing findings, the identification of gaps, the citation of sources and the reporting of the review.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the published literature is shaped by the pressures of publication, and a review that takes the literature at face value inherits its biases. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that a reviewer must judge the evidence rather than count the studies, and Cumming (2014) argues that the review should weigh the size and the precision of the effects rather than the number of significant results.

Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the search, the criteria and the judgements so that the review can be checked and updated.
