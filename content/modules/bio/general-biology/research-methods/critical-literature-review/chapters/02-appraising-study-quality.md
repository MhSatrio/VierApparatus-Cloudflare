Not all studies contribute equally to an answer, and a review must appraise each study for its design and quality before weighing its result. Randomised experiments support causal inference more strongly than observational studies, because randomisation balances unmeasured factors between groups, and the design of each study sets the ceiling on what it can show. Ioannidis (2005) described how weak designs can produce strong-looking results, and the appraisal is the means of recognising them.

Several features of a study's conduct determine its quality. Randomisation of allocation, blinding of observers and participants where possible, the completeness of follow-up and the handling of missing data all affect the reliability of the result. Checklists structure the appraisal of these features, so that each study is judged on the same criteria. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that such standards are needed to make the literature trustworthy.

The quality of a study's conduct should be distinguished from the quality of its reporting. A well-conducted study may be reported so poorly that its methods cannot be judged, and a poorly conducted study may be reported in a way that conceals its faults. Page et al. (2021) set the standards for reporting, and a reviewer must note whether a study reports enough to be appraised at all.

Sample size and precision determine how much a study can contribute to the review. A large study with a precise estimate carries more weight than a small one with an imprecise estimate, and the weight should follow the precision rather than the statistical significance. Cumming (2014) argues that the reviewer should weigh studies by the information they contain, and a checklist is a support for this judgement rather than a substitute for it.
