A critical review identifies the gaps in the evidence, and these gaps justify new research. A gap may be a question that has not been asked, a population or setting in which the question has not been studied, an outcome that has not been measured, or an inconsistency among studies that remains unresolved. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the literature should be used to direct research to the questions that matter, rather than to repeat the questions already answered many times.

A gap matters when its answer would change a decision, a theory or a practice. A gap that exists only because no one has studied a trivial detail does not justify new research, and the reviewer should state why the gap matters. The importance of a gap can be judged by the consequences of the answer, and the reviewer should make this judgement explicit.

Inconsistencies among studies can reveal gaps, because the disagreement may point to an unmeasured factor or to a population that has been poorly studied. A reviewer who explains a disagreement by a difference in design or population has identified a question that a new study could resolve. Cumming (2014) argues that the uncertainty that remains should be shown, since the width of an interval often reveals where more evidence is needed.

A gap should be framed as a specific question that new research could answer, with a population, an exposure, a comparison and an outcome. A vague statement that more research is needed does not guide anyone, while a precise question can be tested. Ioannidis (2005) described how a gap framed in a way that the new research can easily satisfy may not be worth filling, and the framing should be honest about what a study could achieve.
