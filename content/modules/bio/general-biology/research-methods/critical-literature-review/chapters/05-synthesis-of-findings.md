A critical review must bring the findings of many studies together into a coherent account, and this synthesis is the most demanding part of the work. The studies are first organised by the question they address, their design and their result, so that comparable studies can be seen together. A table or a structured narrative allows the reader to see the pattern across the studies, and the organisation should follow the question rather than the order in which the studies were found.

Studies that address the same question with comparable measures can be compared directly, and the pattern of their results indicates the direction and the consistency of the evidence. Where the measures differ, the reviewer must judge whether the studies are comparable enough to be combined, and the judgement should be explained. Cumming (2014) argues that the effects should be compared on a common scale with their intervals, so that the reader sees the size of the effects as well as their direction.

Disagreement among studies is common, and it is informative. Differences in the population studied, the design, the measurement or the context may explain why some studies find an effect and others do not. The reviewer should examine these differences rather than simply count the studies on each side, and the explanation offered should be tested against the data where possible.

Studies should be weighted by their precision and their quality, not by their number or their statistical significance. A single large and well-conducted study may be more informative than a dozen small and poorly designed ones, and the reviewer must make this judgement explicit. The overall direction and strength of the evidence should be stated, along with the confidence the reviewer places in it, and the limits of what the studies can show together should be acknowledged. Peng (2011) argued that the synthesis is reproducible only if the reviewer records how the studies were combined and weighted.
