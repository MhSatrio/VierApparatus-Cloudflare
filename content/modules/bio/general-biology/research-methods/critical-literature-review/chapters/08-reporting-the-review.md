A critical literature review makes judgements about the evidence, and its readers must be able to check those judgements. The question and the scope of the review should be stated first, along with the reason the review was undertaken. Page et al. (2021) set out the items that a review should report, and their intent is that the reader can see what was done and reproduce the selection of studies. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and for a review those means include the search and the criteria.

The search should be described with the databases, the terms and the dates, and the criteria by which studies were included and excluded should be given. The appraisal of each study and the basis of the judgement should be reported, so that a reader can see why a study was weighted as it was. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording these steps, since the judgements of a review are only as trustworthy as the record of how they were made.

The synthesis and the weight given to each finding should be described, along with the conclusions and the confidence the reviewer places in them. The gaps that remain should be stated, and the uncertainty of the conclusions should be shown. Cumming (2014) argues that the conclusions should be stated with their intervals, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a review that does not acknowledge its limits may give a false impression of certainty.

The reviewer should declare any interests that could affect the judgement, and the search record and the extraction table should be deposited so that they can be checked and updated.
