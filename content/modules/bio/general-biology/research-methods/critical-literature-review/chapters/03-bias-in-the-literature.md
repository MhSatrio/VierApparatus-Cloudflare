The published literature is not a random sample of the research that has been done. Studies with positive results are more likely to be published than those with negative results, and this publication bias makes the apparent evidence on a question more favourable than the true evidence. Ioannidis (2005) described publication bias as one of the principal reasons that many published findings do not hold, and a critical review must assume that some results are missing.

Selective reporting of outcomes occurs when a study measures several outcomes and reports only those that reach significance. The reader sees a favourable result that the study did not establish as its primary aim, and the unreported outcomes are lost. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that registration of outcomes in advance is the principal remedy, and a reviewer can compare the outcomes registered with those reported.

Time-lag bias arises because early studies of a new finding are often more extreme than later ones, and the effect estimated in the first studies tends to shrink as more data accumulate. A review that weights the early studies heavily will overstate the effect, and the dates of the studies should be considered in the synthesis.

Citation bias occurs when a claim is repeated in many papers, each citing the one before, until it appears to be established, although the original evidence is weak. A reviewer should trace claims to their primary sources, and check whether the source supports the claim as stated. Cumming (2014) argues that the strength of the evidence should be judged from the data and not from the number of citations, and funnel plots and comparisons of registered outcomes help to detect the bias that remains.
