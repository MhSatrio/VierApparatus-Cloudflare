A critical review must read the statistics of the studies it describes as carefully as their designs. The p-value is the probability of data at least as extreme as those observed if the null hypothesis were true. It is not the probability that the hypothesis is true, and a p-value of 0.04 does not mean that there is a 96 per cent chance the effect is real. Cumming (2014) argues that this misreading is widespread, and it leads readers to overrate findings that barely reach a threshold.

Statistical significance is not practical importance. A large study may find an effect that is statistically significant but too small to matter, and a small study may find a large effect that is not significant because it is imprecise. The reviewer should ask about the size of the effect and its interval, not only whether it passed a threshold. The dichotomy of significant and non-significant hides the size of the effect, and a result near the threshold is not qualitatively different from one just beyond it.

Multiple comparisons inflate the chance of a false positive, and a study that tests many outcomes or many subgroups will find some significant results by chance. Ioannidis (2005) described how such flexibility, combined with the pressure to find significance, produces results that do not replicate. The reviewer should check whether the number of tests was reported and whether a correction was applied.

Some statistics can be checked by recalculation from the summary values that a study reports, such as means, standard deviations and sample sizes. A recalculation that disagrees with the reported statistic reveals an error or an undisclosed choice. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that such checks are part of the ongoing verification of the literature, and the reviewer should report any discrepancy.
