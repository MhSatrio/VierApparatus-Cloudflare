A citation is a promise that the source supports the claim it is attached to, and a critical review must keep that promise. Each claim should be traced to the primary study that reports the finding, rather than to a secondary summary, since the summary may have altered the claim. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended that the sources be recorded precisely so that the chain from claim to evidence can be followed by the reader.

A source may be cited for a claim it does not support. The study may have found a weaker result than the citing author states, may have studied a different population, or may have been retracted or corrected. The reviewer should read the cited source and check that it says what is attributed to it, since errors of this kind are common and they propagate.

A chain of citations can make a weak claim appear established. Each paper cites the one before, and the claim gains apparent authority with each repetition, although the original evidence may be thin. Ioannidis (2005) described how such chains produce a false sense of consensus, and tracing each claim to its origin is the means of exposing the chain.

Findings should be attributed with their scope and limits. A result found in one population or under one condition should not be presented as a general truth, and the attribution should state the conditions. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with its conditions, and the citation should carry them. References should be given with persistent identifiers such as DOIs, so that every reference can be located and checked by the reader.
