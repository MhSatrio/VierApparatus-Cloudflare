A literature search is the foundation of a review, and a review can be no more complete than its search. The search begins with the concepts of the question, each of which is translated into terms that the databases recognise. Each concept is expressed through its synonyms and through the controlled vocabulary of the database, and the terms are combined with the operators the database supports. A search string that is too narrow misses relevant studies, and one that is too broad floods the reviewer with irrelevant ones.

No single database covers a field completely, and the choice of databases must reflect the discipline and the kind of studies sought. Page et al. (2021) required reviews to report the databases searched and the dates, since the coverage of each database differs. Searching several databases reduces the chance that relevant studies are missed, and the overlap among them should be checked.

Citation tracking finds studies that cite or are cited by key papers, and it can reveal work that the search terms did not capture. It is a useful complement to the database search, since the terms used by authors may differ from those a reviewer would choose. The tracking should be recorded, so that its contribution to the final set can be seen.

A search that finds only published positive studies gives a biased picture of the field, because negative results are less often published. Trial registries, preprint servers and the reports of regulators reveal unpublished or ongoing work, and searching them reduces this bias. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended that the search be recorded with its date and its exact terms so that it can be repeated.
