A systematic review answers a precise question by finding, judging and combining all the studies that meet criteria set in advance. It differs from a narrative review in that every step is planned, recorded and open to checking, so that the result does not depend on the reviewer's selection of what to read. Page et al. (2021) set out the PRISMA standard for reporting these reviews, and the standard is the framework of the topic.

The review begins with a protocol that states the question, the criteria for inclusion, the search and the planned analysis, and the protocol is registered before the search. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that registration of this kind protects a review from the flexibility that produces biased conclusions. The later chapters examine the search and screening, the extraction of data, the assessment of risk of bias, the synthesis, the grading of certainty, the updating of reviews and their reporting.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a review that is shaped after its results are known can give a misleading answer, and the pre-specification of every step is the defence. Cumming (2014) argues that the review should report the size and the precision of the effects, not only their direction.

Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended that every decision, every data extraction and every calculation be recorded so that the review can be reproduced.
