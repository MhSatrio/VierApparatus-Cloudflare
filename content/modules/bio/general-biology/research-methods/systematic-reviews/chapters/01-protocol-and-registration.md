The protocol of a systematic review states, before the review begins, what it will ask and how it will answer. The question is framed by its population, its intervention or exposure, its comparator and its outcome, and these four elements define which studies are relevant. The criteria for inclusion and exclusion follow from them, and they should be specific about design, population, setting and outcome, so that two reviewers applying them would select the same studies.

The protocol also states the planned search, including the databases and the terms, the data to be extracted, the risk of bias assessment and the synthesis. Page et al. (2021) required these items to be reported, and the protocol makes the review's plan visible to others. Registration of the protocol in a public registry records its date, so that the plan can be shown to have existed before the results.

Protocols are sometimes amended, because a search reveals an unexpected problem or a criterion proves impossible to apply. The amendment is legitimate when it is recorded with its date and its reason and when it is reported. An undisclosed change to the criteria, made after the studies have been seen, undermines the purpose of the protocol. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that openness about changes is the basis of trust in a review.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the criteria of a review that are chosen after the results are known can select the studies that favour a conclusion, and Cumming (2014) argues that the planned analysis should state the estimates to be reported with their intervals. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended that the protocol and its version history be kept together.
