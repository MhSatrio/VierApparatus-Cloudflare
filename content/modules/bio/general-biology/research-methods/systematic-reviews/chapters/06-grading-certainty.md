The certainty of a body of evidence is the degree to which the reviewer is confident that the estimate of an effect is correct. It is distinct from the size of the effect, and a large effect may be uncertain and a small one may be certain. Grading provides a structured way of judging certainty, and Page et al. (2021) required reviews to make the certainty of their findings clear. The certainty begins at a level set by the design, with randomised evidence starting higher than observational evidence, and is then adjusted by the judgements below.

Certainty is reduced by risk of bias in the studies, by inconsistency when the studies disagree in their results beyond what chance would produce, and by indirectness when the evidence concerns a different population, intervention or outcome from the question. It is reduced by imprecision when the interval of the estimate is wide enough to include effects that would change the decision, and by publication bias when the missing studies are likely to change the picture. Ioannidis (2005) described how each of these can make an apparent finding unreliable.

Certainty may be raised for observational evidence when the effect is large, when a dose-response relation is present or when all plausible residual confounding would reduce the apparent effect. These judgements must be made with care, since they are easily misapplied. Cumming (2014) argues that the precision of each estimate should be shown with its interval, which is the basis of the judgement about imprecision.

The grades are summarised in a table that presents the effect for each outcome with its certainty, and the table allows a decision-maker to see the confidence that the evidence allows. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that such summaries make the strength of the evidence transparent, and the reasons for each grade should be reported with the table.
