The search must be comprehensive enough that eligible studies are unlikely to be missed, and it must be recorded so that it can be repeated. Several bibliographic databases are searched with the terms and the dates given, and the terms are built from the components of the question. Trial registries and the grey literature, such as reports and theses, are searched because much relevant work is not published in journals. Page et al. (2021) required the sources and the dates to be reported for each search.

The records found are de-duplicated, since the same study appears in several databases, and then screened. Screening first examines titles and abstracts against the criteria, and records that pass are examined in full text. Two reviewers should screen independently, since a single reviewer may miss an eligible study, and the agreement between them should be measured. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording each decision so that the screening can be checked.

Disagreements between the reviewers are resolved by discussion or by a third reviewer, and the resolution should be recorded. The number of records at each stage, from those found to those included, should be shown in a flow diagram, which allows the reader to see how many studies were excluded and for what reason. The reasons for exclusion of full-text studies should be given with their categories.

Cumming (2014) argues that the number of studies and the agreement between reviewers should be reported, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a screen that relies on the judgement of one person may admit or exclude studies in ways that favour a conclusion. Peng (2011) argued that the screening is reproducible only if its decisions are recorded.
