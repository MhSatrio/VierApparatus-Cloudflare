Risk of bias is the likelihood that a study's result is systematically distorted, and it differs from the question of whether the study is precise. A large study may have a high risk of bias if its randomisation was not concealed or if its outcomes were assessed by people who knew the treatment, and a small study may have a low risk if its design protects against bias. Page et al. (2021) required reviews to assess the risk of bias in each included study, since the results of a review depend on the quality of the studies it combines.

The assessment considers the domains of bias that matter for the design. For a randomised study these include the randomisation process, deviations from the intended interventions, missing outcome data, the measurement of the outcome and the selection of the reported result. Each domain is judged with signalling questions that direct the reviewer to the information that matters, and the answers determine the judgement of low, some concerns or high risk.

The overall judgement for each study combines the domains, and the reviewer must state the reasons for each judgement. A study with high risk in one domain is not necessarily worthless, but the concern must be carried into the synthesis. Ioannidis (2005) described how biased studies can produce strong results, and the reviewer should not allow the strength of a result to override the judgement of its risk.

The risk of bias assessments are used in the synthesis, often through a sensitivity analysis that excludes or downweights the high-risk studies. If the conclusion changes when the high-risk studies are removed, the conclusion depends on biased evidence, and this must be reported. Cumming (2014) argues that the effect of the assessments on the estimates should be shown with their intervals, and Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the transparency of such judgements is essential to trust in the review.
