A systematic review is reported according to the PRISMA standard, which lists the items that a review should describe so that a reader can see every decision. The title should identify the review as systematic, and the abstract should be structured so that the question, the methods, the results and the conclusions can be found quickly. Page et al. (2021) set out these items in full, and the report should follow them closely.

The rationale and the objectives state the question in the structured form of population, intervention, comparator and outcome. The methods describe the protocol and its registration, the search strategies with their databases and dates, the study selection with the flow diagram, the data extraction, the risk of bias assessment, the synthesis and the certainty of the evidence. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended that these methods be described in enough detail for the review to be repeated.

The results report the characteristics of the included studies and the findings for each outcome, with their intervals. The results should be given for all the pre-specified outcomes, including those with null or negative findings, since selective reporting of outcomes is itself a source of bias. Cumming (2014) argues that the intervals must be shown alongside the estimates, and Peng (2011) argued that the data behind the results should be available.

The discussion must acknowledge the limitations of the review, including the limits of the studies, the bias that may remain and the gaps in the evidence. The conclusions should be stated with the confidence the evidence allows, and the data and the code should be deposited with identifiers. Ioannidis (2005) described how a review that does not acknowledge its limitations may give a false impression of the certainty of its conclusions.
