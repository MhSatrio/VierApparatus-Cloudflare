Research accumulates, and a systematic review that was accurate when published can become out of date within a few years. Updating keeps the review current, and a living review is kept under continuous surveillance of the literature so that its conclusions reflect the latest evidence. Page et al. (2021) required reviews to state their date of search, so that readers can see how current the conclusions are, and the update is the means of keeping them current.

New studies are incorporated using the same criteria, search and methods as the original review, so that the update is comparable to it. Changing the methods in an update makes the new conclusions incomparable with the old, and any change must be recorded and justified. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with its methods, and the update should follow them.

A cumulative meta-analysis shows how the pooled estimate changed as each study was added, in order of publication or of precision. It reveals whether the evidence reached a stable conclusion early, and whether later studies changed it, and it can help decide when further studies are unlikely to alter the decision. Cumming (2014) argues that the cumulative estimate should be shown with its interval, since the change may be within the uncertainty.

Version control records what changed in each update and why. Ioannidis (2005) described how a conclusion that is revised silently may mislead readers who relied on the earlier version, and Munafò et al. (2017) argued that openness about changes is the basis of trust. Each version should be dated, and its search and inclusions should be recorded.
