Not every systematic review can pool its studies. The studies may differ too much in their populations, interventions or outcomes for a single estimate to be meaningful, or the data may be too sparse for a pooled estimate to be reliable. In these cases the review synthesises the studies in a structured narrative, which organises them by population, intervention and outcome and presents their results in tables and displays. Page et al. (2021) required a reason to be given whenever a meta-analysis is not undertaken.

A structured synthesis presents the effect in each study with its interval, so that the reader can see the size and the precision of each result together, without a pooled value. The grouping of studies should follow the question, and the display should make the direction and the magnitude of the effects visible. Cumming (2014) argues that the intervals convey the information that a single significance decision conceals, and they are the core of a structured synthesis.

Vote-counting, which tallies the studies that found a significant effect against those that did not, is misleading. It ignores the size and the precision of the studies, and a large study with a small effect and a small study with a large effect may be counted in opposite ways. The number of significant results depends on the sample sizes, so a count can favour an effect that is trivial. Ioannidis (2005) described how such counting can mislead about the evidence, and it should not be used as the basis of a conclusion.

Some descriptive methods, such as the plot of the direction of effects and the examination of the distribution of p-values, can describe a body of studies without pooling them. Heterogeneity among studies may make pooling inappropriate, and the reason for the choice must be stated. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that a review should be transparent about the methods it uses and the reasons it did not use others.
