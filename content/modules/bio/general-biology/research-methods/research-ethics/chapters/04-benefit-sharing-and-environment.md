Research that collects biological material from the environment, from communities or from genetic resources takes something that belongs to others, and it creates obligations in return. The providers of material and the communities whose knowledge or resources are used must give their prior informed consent, and the benefits that arise from the use of their material should be shared fairly. These obligations are as much part of research ethics as the protection of human participants, and they are often overlooked in studies that treat the environment as an open resource.

Access and benefit sharing agreements set out the terms on which material may be collected and used, and the benefits that will return to the providers. The agreement should be made before collection, and its terms should be honoured throughout the study. Peng (2011) argued that the availability of data supports verification, and the benefit sharing obligations do not prevent the open publication of results, provided the providers are acknowledged and their interests protected.

Traditional knowledge and the interests of indigenous communities require particular care. Knowledge that a community holds may be used in a study only with its consent, and the study should not present that knowledge as the investigators' own discovery. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the openness of research must not come at the expense of the people who make it possible, and attribution is part of the respect owed to them.

Sampling itself can harm the environment, through the removal of organisms, the disturbance of habitats or the spread of invasive species. The sampling should be planned to minimise these effects, using the smallest number of samples that answers the question, and the harm should be assessed before the work begins. Wilkinson et al. (2016) described the principle that data should be findable and reusable, and the providers should be given the results that matter to them, with the same care that the principles of open data require.
