Every study imposes some risk on those who take part, and the ethical question is whether that risk is justified. Risks may be physical, such as the side effects of an intervention, psychological, such as distress from a questionnaire, social, such as the disclosure of a sensitive attribute, or legal and economic. Each should be identified in the protocol, and the design should minimise it, for example by using the smallest sample that answers the question and the least invasive measurement that provides the data.

The justification of any remaining risk rests on the scientific value of the question and on the adequacy of the design to answer it. A study that is unlikely to produce an informative result cannot justify exposing participants to risk, because the risk buys nothing. Cumming (2014) argues that a study should be designed to estimate its effect with a stated precision, and the expected precision is part of the justification, since a study that cannot distinguish the effects that matter is not worth the risk.

Ioannidis (2005) described how many studies are underpowered and their results unreliable, and this has an ethical dimension, since participants bear risk for findings that cannot be trusted. The review committee should examine the power and the design of a study as part of its judgement of risk and benefit. An adequately powered and well-designed study makes the best use of the participants who take part.

Ethical review committees are composed to include people with scientific, medical, ethical and lay expertise, and they should be independent of the investigators. They judge the protocol against its criteria and may approve, approve with conditions, request changes or reject. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that openness in review is part of the integrity of research, and the decisions of committees should be recorded with their reasons.

Approved studies must be monitored, and adverse events must be reported to the committee. Amendments to the protocol must be submitted for review before they are implemented, since a change made without approval can alter the risk to participants without anyone having judged it. The investigators are responsible for keeping the committee informed throughout the life of the study.
