Some people are vulnerable to exploitation or harm in research, and the ethical protections for them must be stronger. Children cannot give fully informed consent and depend on others for decisions. People with impaired capacity may not understand what they are agreeing to. People in dependent relationships, such as students or patients of the investigator, may feel unable to refuse. Prisoners, people in economic hardship and communities that may be stigmatised all face particular pressures, and the protections must be designed for the vulnerability that each group faces.

Research with a vulnerable group is justified only when the question requires that group and cannot be answered with less vulnerable people. A study of a condition that affects only children must include children, but a study whose question could be answered with adults should not put children at risk for convenience. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the justification for including a group should be stated clearly, and the review committee should test it.

Safeguards should be proportionate to the vulnerability. For children, these include the consent of parents, the assent of the child and the limitation of procedures to those that are needed. For dependent participants, the consent must be obtained by someone other than the investigator, so that refusal carries no penalty. Ioannidis (2005) described how underpowered studies in vulnerable groups expose participants to risk for results that may not be reliable, and the adequacy of the design is part of the protection.

Payment to people in economic hardship can become coercive when it is large relative to their needs, and the amount must be set so that it does not override the judgement of the participant. Research may also harm a community through findings that stigmatise it, and the investigators should consider how results will be presented and who will be affected. Cumming (2014) argues that findings about groups should report the variation within them as well as the differences between them, which reduces the risk of stereotyping.
