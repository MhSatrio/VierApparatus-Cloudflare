The use of animals in research is justified only when the question cannot be answered by other means and when the animals' welfare is protected as far as the science allows. The principles of replacement, reduction and refinement, often called the 3Rs, guide this judgement. Replacement asks whether cell cultures, computational models or studies of humans can answer the question. Where they can, the use of animals is not justified, and the protocol must state why each alternative was rejected.

Reduction requires that the number of animals be the smallest that allows the question to be answered. Cumming (2014) argues that the sample size should be calculated to estimate the effect with a stated precision, and this calculation is an ethical as well as a scientific obligation. Within-subject designs, shared controls and blocking can reduce the number of animals without losing information, and each should be considered in the design.

Ioannidis (2005) described how underpowered studies produce unreliable results, and in animal research this means that animals are used for findings that cannot be trusted. An underpowered study therefore wastes the animals it uses, and the review committee should examine the power of the design as part of its judgement of welfare. A study that cannot detect the effect it aims to measure does not justify the harm it causes.

Refinement reduces the pain, distress and suffering that animals experience. It includes the use of appropriate anaesthesia and analgesia, the establishment of humane endpoints at which an animal is removed from the study before severe suffering, and the provision of enrichment that supports natural behaviour. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the welfare of animals should be monitored throughout a study, and the findings of that monitoring should be reported. The number of animals used and the welfare measures applied should be reported in the publication, as the review committee and the journal require.
