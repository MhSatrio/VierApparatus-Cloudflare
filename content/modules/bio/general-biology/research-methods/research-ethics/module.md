Research ethics asks whether a study is justified and whether it is conducted in a way that respects those it affects. Its principles are respect for persons, which requires informed and voluntary participation; beneficence and non-maleficence, which require that the benefits of research justify its risks; and justice, which requires that the burdens and benefits of research are distributed fairly. These principles apply to human participants, to animals and to the environment from which biological material is taken, and each has its own requirements.

Ethical review is the process by which a committee evaluates a protocol before data are collected. The committee judges whether the risks are minimised, whether the potential benefits justify them, whether the consent process is adequate and whether the study is scientifically valid, since an invalid study cannot justify exposing participants to risk. Ioannidis (2005) described how studies with small samples and weak design waste resources and expose participants without producing reliable knowledge, and the scientific quality of a study is therefore an ethical matter.

Research with animals is governed by the principles of replacement, reduction and refinement. Replacement asks whether the question can be answered without animals, reduction asks whether the number of animals can be reduced without loss of information, and refinement asks whether the procedures can be made less harmful. Cumming (2014) argues that the calculation of sample size is an ethical as well as a statistical obligation in animal research, since an underpowered study uses animals without a reasonable chance of an informative result.

Research on biological material from the environment or from communities carries obligations of benefit sharing and respect for the sources. Peng (2011) argued that the availability of data supports the verification of results, and the sharing of benefits from research with the communities that provided material is a parallel obligation. Ethical duties continue throughout a study: the investigators must monitor risks, stop the study when the risks outweigh the benefits and report adverse events and deviations. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that honesty about the conduct of a study is part of its ethics, and the following chapters examine consent, review, animal welfare and benefit sharing in turn.
