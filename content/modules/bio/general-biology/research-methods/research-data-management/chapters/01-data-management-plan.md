A data management plan is a document that describes how a project's data will be handled, and it is most useful when it is written before the data are collected. It begins with a description of the data: what will be generated or reused, how much of it is expected, and in which formats. A plan that estimates volume allows storage and costs to be planned, and a plan that names formats allows the data to be read in the future. Wilkinson et al. (2016) described the principles that make such formats useful, and the plan should apply them.

Each data type should be matched to a standard, whether a community format, a controlled vocabulary or an established schema. The standard makes the data interoperable with other work, and it reduces the effort needed to combine datasets later. Peng (2011) argued that the meaning of the data must be recoverable, and a standard that is documented allows a future user to understand the variables without the original team.

Metadata describe the data: who collected them, when, how, with which instrument and under what conditions. Metadata are as important as the data they describe, because a dataset without its context cannot be reused reliably. The plan should state which metadata will be recorded and where they will be kept, so that they are captured during the work rather than reconstructed afterwards.

Responsibilities must be assigned, because data management that belongs to everyone is often done by no one. The plan should name the person responsible for each task, from the recording of metadata to the deposition of the final dataset, and it should set a schedule for reviewing the plan as the project changes. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the openness of research depends on such organisation, and a plan that is reviewed regularly stays aligned with what is actually being done.
