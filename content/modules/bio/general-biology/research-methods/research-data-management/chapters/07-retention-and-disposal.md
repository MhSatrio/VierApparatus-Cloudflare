Retention is the decision to keep data for a stated period, and disposal is the decision to destroy them. Both must be made deliberately, because data kept without reason accumulate risk and cost, while data destroyed too early may be impossible to recover. The retention period is set by the requirements of funders, journals, institutions and the law, and the team should identify the requirement that applies to each dataset and state it in the plan.

Not all data deserve long-term preservation. Those that are the basis of published results, that cannot be regenerated and that have value for future research should be preserved. Wilkinson et al. (2016) described how preserved data remain findable and reusable only if they are maintained, and the appraisal should identify the datasets whose loss would matter. Peng (2011) argued that the data behind a result must remain available as long as the result is relied upon.

Preservation requires a repository that will maintain the data beyond the life of the project, with a persistent identifier and a commitment to keep the files readable. A repository that is discontinued leaves its datasets without a home, so the choice of preservation service should consider its continuity. The documentation accompanying the data must also be kept, since a preserved file without its dictionary and metadata is of little use to a future user.

Data that must not be kept, such as personal information no longer needed for its purpose, must be destroyed securely. Deleting a file often leaves recoverable copies in backups and caches, so destruction must reach every copy. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the record of what was kept and what was destroyed is itself part of transparent research, and it should state the authority for each decision and the date on which it was made.
