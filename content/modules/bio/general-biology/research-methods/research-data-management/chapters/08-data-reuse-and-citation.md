Reusing existing data can answer questions that a new study could not afford to ask, but a dataset is only as useful as the reuser's understanding of it. Before reuse, the analyst should examine the provenance of the data: who collected them, by what methods, for what purpose and with what known limitations. Wilkinson et al. (2016) argued that a dataset whose provenance is clear is far more reusable than one whose origin must be guessed, and the examination is the first step in judging fitness for a new purpose.

The integrity of the data should be verified against a checksum or the original source before analysis begins. A dataset that has been copied or transformed since its creation may differ from the version that its creators described, and the differences can change the result. The version used must be recorded, since datasets are often revised, and an analysis of a superseded version cannot be repeated from the current one.

Creators of data deserve credit, and a dataset should be cited in the reference list with its persistent identifier, its version and the name of its creators. A citation of this kind allows the original work to be traced, and it encourages the sharing of data that others will reuse. Peng (2011) argued that the chain of credit and evidence depends on such citations being complete.

Combining datasets that use different standards requires harmonisation, in which variables are mapped to a common definition. The mapping must be documented, and its limitations stated, since a variable that appears the same in two studies may differ in its measurement. Cumming (2014) argues that the uncertainty introduced by harmonisation should be considered alongside the sampling uncertainty, so that the combined estimate is not presented as more precise than the evidence allows.
