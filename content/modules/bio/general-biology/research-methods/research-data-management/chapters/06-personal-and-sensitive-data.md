Personal and sensitive data require the most careful management, because they concern people who have trusted the investigators with information about themselves. The consent under which the data were collected defines the purposes for which they may be used, and any use beyond those purposes requires fresh consent or a legal basis. The data management plan must state these limits, and the team must respect them even when a broader use would be scientifically attractive.

Data are identifiable when they can be linked to a person, pseudonymised when identifiers are replaced by codes that can be reversed with a separate key, and anonymised when no link can reasonably be made. Removing names does not always remove the risk of re-identification, because a combination of age, postcode and diagnosis can single out an individual. Wilkinson et al. (2016) described the principle of reusability, but for personal data reuse must be balanced against the protection of the people described.

Data should be minimised to what the research requires, since data that are not collected cannot be lost or misused. A high-risk processing activity, such as the linkage of many sources or the collection of genetic information, may require a formal assessment of its risks before it begins. Genetic and health information is especially sensitive, and its disclosure can affect families as well as the individual, so the safeguards should be stronger.

Agreements with collaborators should define who is responsible for the data, who may access them and how they may be used. Such agreements prevent disputes about ownership and reduce the risk that data are shared outside the terms of consent. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that openness must be pursued within these limits, and Ioannidis (2005) described how the withholding of data can impede the correction of error, so the publication should state clearly how privacy was protected and why any restriction was needed.
