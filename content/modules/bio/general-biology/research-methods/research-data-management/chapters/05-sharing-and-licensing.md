Sharing data allows others to check a result, to combine it with other evidence and to build on it. A dataset should be deposited in a repository that preserves it for the long term and assigns a persistent identifier, so that the citation remains valid when the project's own web pages change. Wilkinson et al. (2016) described the identifier as the first step towards findable data, and a repository that offers one is preferable to an informal link on a personal page.

Not every dataset can be shared openly. Consent given by participants, the protection of privacy and the legitimate interests of collaborators may restrict what is released. Where restriction is needed, the reasons should be stated, and the data should be made available through a controlled procedure in which qualified applicants are approved. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that openness is the default that science should aim for, and restrictions should be exceptions that are justified.

A licence states the conditions under which the data may be reused. An open licence that permits reuse with attribution is the common choice, because it removes doubt about whether others may use the data. Without a licence, the legal position of a dataset is unclear, and many users will not use it. The licence should be chosen before deposition and stated in the repository record.

An embargo holds a dataset back for a fixed period, usually until the main publication appears, so that the investigators have time to analyse their data first. An embargo should be stated in the plan and in the repository record, with a date for release. The publication should cite the dataset with its identifier and state where the data can be found and under what conditions they may be used, so that a reader can locate them. Peng (2011) argued that such statements complete the chain between the claim and the evidence.
