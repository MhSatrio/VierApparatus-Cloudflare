Research data are the foundation of every result, and their management determines whether a finding can be checked, reused and trusted. A data management plan written at the start of a project states what data will be collected, in what form, where they will be stored, who may access them and how they will be shared. Peng (2011) argued that the data are as much a part of the evidence as the analysis, and the plan makes their stewardship deliberate rather than accidental.

The FAIR principles, set out by Wilkinson et al. (2016), describe data that are findable, accessible, interoperable and reusable. A dataset is findable when it carries a persistent identifier and rich metadata, accessible when its identifier leads to a retrieval procedure, interoperable when it uses shared formats and vocabularies, and reusable when its licence and provenance are clear. These principles guide the choices made throughout the project, from file formats to the description of each variable.

Raw data are kept unchanged, and every processed or derived dataset is produced from them by a recorded procedure. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended this separation, since it allows any derived value to be traced to its origin. Storage should include backups in a second location, and the integrity of the files should be checked, since data lost or corrupted during a project cannot be recovered from the published result.

Personal and sensitive data are governed by the consent under which they were collected and by the law that applies to them. The plan must state how such data will be anonymised, stored securely and shared only in forms that respect the consent given. At the end of the project, the plan should specify how long the data will be retained and how they will be disposed of, so that retention is a decision rather than a default.
