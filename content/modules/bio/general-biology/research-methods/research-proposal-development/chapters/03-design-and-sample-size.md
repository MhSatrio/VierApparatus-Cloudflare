The design of a proposal must match its aims. A controlled experiment can establish cause, a cohort study can follow an exposure over time, and a comparative study can describe differences among groups. The choice is constrained by what can be done ethically and practically, and the proposal must justify it in terms of the question. A design that cannot answer the aim, however carefully executed, will not produce a useful result.

The sample size is calculated from the effect that matters, the precision required and the variability of the outcome. The effect should be one of practical or scientific importance, not merely the smallest effect that a test could detect. Cumming (2014) argues that the calculation should target an interval of a stated width, since this addresses the question of how precisely the effect is known. The assumptions about variability should be taken from the existing evidence, with their uncertainty acknowledged.

An underpowered study is a common source of misleading results. When a study has too few participants, only the largest chance deviations reach significance, and the significant estimates therefore overstate the true effect. Ioannidis (2005) described this as one of the principal reasons that published findings are unreliable, and a proposal that does not calculate its sample size invites this error.

The calculation must allow for attrition, for missing data and for any clustering in the data, such as several measurements from one animal or several animals from one tank. Peng (2011) argued that reproducible research requires every assumption to be stated, and the assumptions behind the sample size should be reported in the proposal so that a reviewer can test them. A sensitivity table showing how the sample size changes with the assumed effect is a helpful way to present this.
