The review of evidence in a proposal establishes what is already known, so that the new work is shown to be needed. It is not a general survey of the field. A proposal that claims a gap because little has been written does not persuade, whereas one that identifies a result that conflicts with another, or a population in which the effect has never been measured, gives the reviewer a specific reason to fund the work. The review should be focused on the question the aims will address.

The quality of the existing studies must be judged, not merely counted. A large number of weak studies may provide less reliable evidence than a few well-designed ones, and the review should state how the studies were assessed. Ioannidis (2005) described how the apparent consensus of a field can rest on a small number of biased or unreplicated studies, and a review that does not examine quality may build a false gap on a false consensus.

Published effect sizes, with their intervals, are the most useful information for a proposal. They allow the expected magnitude of the effect to be estimated and the sample size to be calculated. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval conveys the uncertainty of the existing estimate, and a proposal that uses only a point estimate may plan a study that is too small to detect the effect that matters.

The review must also acknowledge the limits of what is known. Existing studies may have used different methods, populations or outcomes, and these differences should be stated. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that transparent reporting of limitations is a mark of a credible research programme, and the proposal should show how its own design will address the most important limitation identified.
