Every research project meets risks, and a proposal that ignores them is less credible than one that names them and plans for them. Risks may be technical, such as the failure of an assay; biological, such as the death of a culture or the absence of a response; logistical, such as delayed equipment; or personnel-related, such as the departure of a researcher. Each should be assessed by how likely it is and by how far it would threaten the primary aim, since a risk that cannot affect the aim needs little attention.

For each serious risk the proposal should state a contingency, which is the action taken if the risk occurs. A contingency might be a second assay, a larger pilot to absorb losses or a change of model organism. Contingencies protect the primary aim, and the proposal should show that the most important aim survives the failure of any single component. Ioannidis (2005) described how studies that cannot withstand such failures often produce incomplete or biased results.

The timetable is organised around milestones that mark the completion of each aim. A milestone should be verifiable, such as the collection of a stated number of samples or the completion of a validated assay, so that delay can be seen early. Tasks that depend on one another form a critical path, which determines the earliest completion date, and the proposal should identify it.

The timetable must include the time needed for ethical approvals, for training in new methods and for the acquisition of materials, since these are often underestimated. Peng (2011) argued that the steps of research should be recorded so that progress can be checked, and a timetable with dated milestones serves this purpose. Cumming (2014) argues that the timing of data collection should allow enough time for the analysis, so that the interval of each estimate can be reported before the study ends.
