The aims of a proposal state what the work will accomplish. A primary aim should be a single sentence that names the question, the population or system and the outcome, and secondary aims should follow from it without introducing new questions. Too many aims dilute the proposal and make it impossible to assess whether it has succeeded. Reviewers look first at the primary aim, because it defines what the study is for.

An aim differs from a hypothesis. An aim states the activity, such as to estimate the effect of a treatment on growth, whereas a hypothesis states the expected result and the direction in which it will be found. Hypotheses are useful when the design is able to test them decisively, but they should not be stated as certainties. Cumming (2014) argues that many proposals are better served by an aim to estimate an effect with a stated precision than by a hypothesis to detect a difference.

The significance of the work depends on the gap it fills. The gap is identified by reviewing the evidence, and it must be stated precisely: a result that is missing, a result that conflicts with another, or a question that has not been asked in the population of interest. Ioannidis (2005) described how many published findings are never replicated, and a proposal that addresses a replication need can therefore be strongly justified.

Significance should be argued without exaggeration. The proposal should state what the answer will change, whether for understanding, for practice or for a further study, and it should not promise outcomes that the design cannot deliver. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that overstated claims damage the credibility of research, and a sober statement of significance is more persuasive to a careful reviewer.
