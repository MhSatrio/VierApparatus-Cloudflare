The budget translates the design into costs, and a reviewer uses it to test whether the proposed work is feasible. Each cost should follow from a quantity the design requires, such as the number of samples multiplied by the number of replicates and the number of time points. A budget built on round numbers rather than on the design signals that the work has not been planned in detail, and reviewers treat such budgets with suspicion.

Each item should be justified in terms of an aim. Personnel are needed for specific tasks, equipment for specific measurements and consumables for specific assays, and the justification should state which aim each item serves. Cumming (2014) argues that the precision of the main estimate depends on the sample size, which in turn depends on cost, so the budget and the sample size must be considered together.

The proposal should distinguish the resources it requests from those already available, such as an existing instrument or a shared facility. Including resources already in place shows that the work is supported, and it reveals how much new funding is required. The allowances for inflation and contingency should be stated and justified, since unexpected costs are common in laboratory work.

Peng (2011) argued that reproducible research depends on access to the data and materials, and the budget should include the cost of storing and sharing them. Munafò et al. (2017) noted that transparent reporting of resources makes a research programme easier to assess, and a budget that states its assumptions clearly is more useful to reviewers and to the investigators who must later account for it.
