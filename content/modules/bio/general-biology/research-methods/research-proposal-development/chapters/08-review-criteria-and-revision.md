Reviewers assess a proposal against a small set of criteria: whether the aims are significant and original, whether the design and sample size are sound, whether the timetable and budget are feasible, and whether the ethical and governance statements are adequate. A proposal can be excellent in its science and still fail if any of these is weak, so the investigator should check each criterion deliberately before submission, rather than assume that the topic will carry the proposal.

The sample size and the design are judged most closely, because a reviewer who finds the study underpowered will doubt the value of any result it produces. Cumming (2014) argues that a proposal should show how the planned precision answers the aim, and a reviewer who sees this link is more likely to accept the design. Ioannidis (2005) described how underpowered designs produce unreliable findings, which is why reviewers attend to this point.

Revision is a response to review, and each comment should be answered in a structured reply. The reply should state, for each comment, whether the proposal was changed and where, or why it was not changed. Comments that call for a change to the design require action, whereas comments that ask for clarification require only an explanation. A reply that addresses every comment, even those it does not accept, shows that the review was taken seriously.

A resubmission is stronger when it includes preliminary data or a pilot study that tests the feasibility of the key measurement. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that preliminary work that shows the methods work is among the most persuasive additions to a proposal. The pilot should be reported honestly, with its limitations, so that the reviewer can judge the evidence it provides.
