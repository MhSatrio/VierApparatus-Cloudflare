Pre-registration commits the investigator to a primary outcome, a model and the rules for handling the data before any data are collected. The record is time-stamped in a public registry, so that it can be shown that the plan preceded the results. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that this reduces the room for choices that favour a conclusion after the data have been seen, and it is one of the most effective safeguards available to a research programme.

The analysis plan should name one primary outcome and one confirmatory analysis, since these carry the weight of the study's main claim. Additional analyses may be planned as exploratory, and their results should be reported as hypotheses for further work. Ioannidis (2005) described how the multiplicity of analyses, each tested until one reaches significance, is a major source of false findings, and the distinction between confirmatory and exploratory work protects against it.

The rules for missing data, outliers and exclusions must be fixed in advance. A rule chosen after the data are seen, such as dropping a participant whose result is unexpected, can change the conclusion without any disclosure. The plan should state the criteria for each exclusion and the method for handling missing values, and it should state how many observations are expected to be affected.

Deviations from the plan are sometimes necessary, because a measurement fails or a participant withdraws. Each deviation should be recorded with its date and its reason, and the results should be reported both with and without it where this is informative. Cumming (2014) argues that the estimate and its interval should be reported for the planned analysis, since the uncertainty of the main finding is the most important information a reader can have.
