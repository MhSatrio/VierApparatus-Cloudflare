Every proposal must address its ethical obligations, and reviewers expect a clear statement of them. The ethical issues depend on the work: studies with animals raise questions of welfare and the justification of harm, studies with people raise questions of consent and privacy, and studies with environmental samples may raise questions of access and benefit sharing. The proposal should name the issues that apply and state how each will be handled, rather than offering a general assurance of good practice.

Approval from the relevant committee must be obtained before data are collected, and the proposal should state when and from whom approval will be sought. Work that begins before approval invalidates its data and may invalidate the wider programme. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the integrity of research depends on the procedures that govern it being visible, and a clear statement of approvals serves this purpose.

Data governance covers how data are stored, secured, shared and eventually destroyed. The plan must respect the consent under which the data were collected and the legal requirements that apply to them. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended that the data and the code of computational research be stored in a way that allows them to be checked, and this recommendation must be balanced against the privacy of participants when data are sensitive.

The proposal should commit to reporting results whatever they show, including null or unexpected findings, since selective reporting is a serious ethical failure. Ioannidis (2005) described how unreported negative results distort the literature, and the commitment to publish them should be explicit. Any conflicts of interest and sources of funding should also be disclosed in the proposal.
