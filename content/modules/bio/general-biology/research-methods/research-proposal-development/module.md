A research proposal is an argument that a question is worth asking and that the proposed study can answer it. It must establish the gap in the evidence, state the aims, describe a design that can address them and show that the work is feasible within the time and resources available. Reviewers judge the proposal on these points, and a proposal that is vague about any of them is rarely funded, however interesting its topic. The chapters that follow examine each part of the proposal in turn.

The design must be specified closely enough to be tested. The effect of interest should be stated with the size of change that would matter, and the sample size should be calculated to estimate that effect with a stated precision. Cumming (2014) argues that a proposal should aim for an interval narrow enough to inform a decision, rather than for a test that merely crosses a threshold. Ioannidis (2005) described how underpowered studies inflate the apparent size of effects that reach significance, so the calculation protects the proposal against this problem.

Pre-registration records the hypotheses and the analysis plan before the data are collected, and it makes the analysis that was planned visible to reviewers and later readers. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that such registration reduces the flexibility that allows an investigator to choose the analysis that gives the most favourable result. The proposal should state which analyses are confirmatory and which are exploratory, since the two carry different weight.

A proposal is also a plan for the management of risk. Each risk, such as the failure of a reagent, the loss of samples or the late delivery of equipment, should be identified with a contingency. The budget and the timetable should show how the work will be done, and the justification for each item should be given in terms of the aims. The following chapters describe how the review of evidence, the design and the analysis are constructed to meet these standards.
