Authorship is a statement of responsibility as well as a form of credit. An author should have made a substantial contribution to the design, the data or the analysis, should have helped to draft or revise the manuscript and should have approved the final version. Each author should also be accountable for the accuracy of the work, which means that a person who cannot answer for a particular result should not be listed as its author. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that clear accountability is part of the integrity of science, and authorship that does not follow it weakens the record.

Contributors who do not meet the criteria, such as those who provided technical help or a reagent, should be acknowledged rather than listed as authors. Honorary authorship, in which a senior figure is added without contribution, and ghost authorship, in which a person who did the work is omitted, both misrepresent responsibility. Ioannidis (2005) described how the structure of credit in science shapes what is reported, and the honest allocation of authorship is part of the same discipline.

The order of authors and the basis for it should be agreed before the work begins, and the agreement should be revisited as the work changes. Disputes about authorship are common, and a written agreement at the start prevents many of them. Where a dispute arises, the journal or the institution may provide a procedure, and the authors should use it rather than resolve the matter by withholding data.

A contribution statement records what each author did, such as the design of the study, the collection of the data, the analysis or the writing. The statement makes responsibility visible, and it helps readers to know whom to ask about a particular aspect of the work. Peng (2011) argued that the record of who did what is part of the reproducibility of a study, and the contribution statement makes that record available to the reader.
