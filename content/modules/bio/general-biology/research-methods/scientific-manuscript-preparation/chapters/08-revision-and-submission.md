Before submission, the manuscript must be checked against the reporting guideline for its design, and every number in the text, the tables and the figures must be compared with the analysis output. Errors of transcription are common, and a single mismatched value can undermine confidence in the whole report. Peng (2011) argued that the link between the output of the analysis and the published numbers should be direct, and a check that compares each number with its source is the way to ensure it.

The cover letter states the contribution of the work and its fit with the journal, and it should do so without overstatement. An editor who reads a letter claiming that the work will change a field is more sceptical than one who reads a clear statement of what was found and why it matters. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that restraint in such claims protects the credibility of the authors. The letter should also state any conflicts of interest and any preprint or prior posting.

The journal may require the deposition of data and code, a data availability statement and the statement of ethical approval. These should be prepared in advance so that submission is not delayed, and they should be consistent with the manuscript. Cumming (2014) argues that the estimates and their intervals should be the basis of the revised manuscript, so that the response to reviewers does not change the reporting in a way the reader cannot follow.

Revision in response to review should be structured. The response to reviewers addresses each comment in turn, stating the change made and where it appears, or the reason why no change was made. The revised manuscript should track its changes, so that the reviewer can see what was altered. If the review does not lead to publication, the authors may withdraw and resubmit elsewhere, and Ioannidis (2005) described how an honest assessment of the work, rather than the pursuit of a particular journal, serves the field best.
