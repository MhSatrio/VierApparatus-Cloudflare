The results section reports what the study found, and it should begin with the primary outcome that the study was designed to estimate. The effect is given as an estimate with its interval, and the number of units analysed for the comparison is stated alongside it. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval conveys the precision and the size of the effect together, and a reader who sees only a p-value cannot judge either.

All planned outcomes should be reported, including those that showed no effect. Omitting null results from the manuscript distorts the evidence, because the reader sees only the favourable part of the study. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the reporting of every planned analysis is a basic condition of an honest result, and a null finding with a narrow interval is often as informative as a positive one.

Exploratory analyses that were not planned should be labelled as exploratory, and their results should be presented as suggestions for further work. Presenting an exploratory finding as though it were confirmatory is one of the most common ways that results are overstated. Ioannidis (2005) described how the multiplicity of analyses, each reported when it reaches significance, produces a literature of false findings.

Descriptive statistics should allow the distribution of the data to be seen, not only their summary. A mean with a standard deviation hides a skewed or bimodal distribution, and a plot of the individual values often shows what a summary does not. The results should not emphasise the favourable findings at the expense of the others, and the conclusions drawn in the discussion should follow from the full set of results, not from a selection of them.
