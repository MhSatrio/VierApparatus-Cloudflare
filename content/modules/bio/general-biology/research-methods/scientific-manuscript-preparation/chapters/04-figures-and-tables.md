A figure is often the first thing a reader examines, and it can either reveal the data or hide them. A bar chart of means with a single error bar conveys a summary but conceals the shape of the distribution, which may be skewed, bimodal or driven by a few extreme values. Cumming (2014) argues that figures should show the individual data points or a plot of the distribution, so that the reader can judge the spread as well as the average.

Uncertainty must be shown and labelled. An error bar may represent a standard deviation, a standard error or a confidence interval, and these convey different things. The caption must state which one is shown, since a reader who assumes the wrong meaning will misjudge the precision. A confidence interval is usually the most informative choice for an estimate, because it conveys the range of values consistent with the data.

Axes should be labelled with their units, and their scale should be chosen so that the data are not distorted. An axis that begins above zero can exaggerate a small difference, and a truncated axis should be used only where zero is not meaningful and the truncation is clearly marked. Peng (2011) argued that figures are part of the evidence and must be generated from the data by reproducible code, not drawn by hand.

Captions must state the design, the sample size and the meaning of each symbol, colour and line. A caption that a reader can understand without reading the main text is a mark of a careful report. Colour choices should remain interpretable for readers with colour vision deficiency, and the underlying data should be deposited so that others can regenerate the figure and examine the distribution for themselves. Ioannidis (2005) described how selective presentation of data misleads readers, and a figure that shows the full data resists this.
