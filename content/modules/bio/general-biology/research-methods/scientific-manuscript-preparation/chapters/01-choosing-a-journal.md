Choosing a journal is a decision that affects who reads the work and how it is judged. A journal whose scope matches the question and the design will send the manuscript to appropriate reviewers, while a mismatch leads to rejection or to a review by readers unfamiliar with the methods. The authors should read the aims and scope of several journals and examine their recent articles, since the stated scope and the published content may differ, and the recent articles reveal the standards the editors apply.

Journal policies matter as much as scope. Some journals require data to be shared, some require the registration of trials, some accept registered reports and some restrict preprints. A manuscript that does not meet the policy will be returned before review, so the policy should be checked early. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the policies of journals are among the most powerful levers for changing research practice, and authors should choose venues whose policies support transparency.

Predatory journals charge fees for publication while offering little or no genuine peer review, and their claims of rigorous evaluation cannot be trusted. Signs include unsolicited invitations, unclear editorial boards, rapid acceptance and fees that are not disclosed in advance. Ioannidis (2005) described how the appearance of publication can be mistaken for the validation of findings, and a published article in a predatory journal may not carry the credibility its authors hope for.

Choosing between a specialist and a general journal depends on the readership the authors wish to reach. A specialist journal reaches experts who are likely to use the methods, while a general journal reaches a wider audience but may judge the work less closely on technical grounds. Cumming (2014) argues that the choice should be made with the precision of the claims in view, since a result with a wide interval may be better suited to a venue that values careful reporting than to one that rewards striking findings.
