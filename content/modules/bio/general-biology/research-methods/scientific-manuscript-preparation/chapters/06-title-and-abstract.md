The title and abstract are the parts of a manuscript that most readers see, and many decide on the basis of them whether to read further. The title should name the design and the population or system studied, and it should avoid claims that the study cannot support. A title that announces a cause when the study is observational, or a general effect when the study is in one species, misleads the reader before the abstract is reached. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the representation of a study in its title is part of its honesty.

The abstract should be structured, so that the background, the objectives, the methods, the results and the conclusions can each be found quickly. The results must report the main estimate with its interval and the sample size, not a p-value alone. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval is the most informative single element of the results, and an abstract that omits it gives the reader less than they need to judge the finding.

An abstract that reports only the favourable outcomes distorts the study, and it is one of the most consequential forms of selective reporting. The abstract should report the primary outcome whatever it showed, together with the main limitations. Ioannidis (2005) described how the impression created by an abstract often outlives the full paper, and an accurate abstract corrects the record for readers who never reach the main text.

The abstract must be consistent with the main text, and each figure in it should match the figure in the results. Keywords help indexing and should describe the design and the subject accurately. Before submission, the abstract should be checked line by line against the full manuscript, since abstracts are often revised late and the numbers and claims can drift from the text.
