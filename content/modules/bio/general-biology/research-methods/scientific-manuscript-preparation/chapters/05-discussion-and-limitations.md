The discussion interprets the findings, and it is where the authors are most tempted to overstate. It should begin by restating the main finding with its estimate and its interval, so that the reader returns to the precise result rather than to a memory of it. Ioannidis (2005) described how interpretations that outrun the data are a principal source of misleading conclusions, and the discussion should be written to resist this pull.

The findings should be compared with the existing evidence, including studies that disagree. A discussion that cites only the studies that agree gives a partial picture, and the reader needs to see where the new result fits and where it conflicts. Cumming (2014) argues that the comparison should be made on the basis of the estimates and their intervals, so that the reader can judge whether two studies genuinely differ or whether their intervals overlap.

The interpretation must stay within what the design can support. An observational study cannot establish cause, and a small study cannot establish the absence of an effect. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that causal language should be used only where the design justifies it, and claims of causation from correlational data are a common source of error in published work.

The limitations of the design, the sample, the measurement and the analysis should be stated, with the consequence of each for the interpretation. A limitation named without its consequence is of little use to the reader. Alternative explanations for the findings should also be considered, and the discussion should conclude with a summary of the evidence that does not claim more than it contains.
