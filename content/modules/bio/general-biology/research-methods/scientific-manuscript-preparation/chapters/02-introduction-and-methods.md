The introduction tells the reader why the study was done. It should begin with the context that a reader in the field will share, narrow to the specific gap in the evidence and end with the question and the aims. A literature review that does not lead to the study wastes the reader's attention, and each sentence should move the argument toward the gap. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that a clear statement of the question is the first condition of a transparent report, since everything else follows from it.

The methods section is the part of the manuscript that determines whether the study can be repeated. It must describe the design, the materials, the procedures, the sample and how it was selected, the randomisation and blinding used, the controls and the statistical analysis, including the software and its version. Peng (2011) argued that a method is reproducible only when its steps are stated completely, and general descriptions that a reader must interpret leave room for different results.

Sandve et al. (2013) recommended that the computational steps be described in enough detail to be repeated, and the methods should point to the deposited code and data where they exist. The statement of ethical approval and consent should appear here, with the committee and the reference number. A reader who wants to check the study should find everything needed in this section, without needing to contact the authors.

The methods should also state the analysis planned before the data were collected, where a registration exists, and should identify any deviation from it. Ioannidis (2005) described how undisclosed flexibility in analysis produces findings that do not hold, and a methods section that records the plan and its departures allows the reader to judge the analysis for itself.
