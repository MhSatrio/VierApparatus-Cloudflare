A scientific manuscript is the formal record of a study, and it is the document on which readers, reviewers and later researchers will rely. Its purpose is to let a reader understand what was done, what was found and how far the findings can be trusted. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the quality of the report is part of the quality of the science, and a manuscript that obscures its methods or its uncertainty weakens the evidence it presents, whatever the strength of the study itself.

The structure of the manuscript follows the logic of the study. The introduction states the question and the gap it addresses, the methods describe how the data were obtained and analysed, the results present what was found and the discussion interprets it in the light of the evidence and the limitations. Each section should be written so that a reader can check the claims against the data, and the conclusions should not go beyond what the results support. Ioannidis (2005) described how interpretations that exceed the data mislead the field.

Reporting guidelines specify the items that a manuscript should describe for a given design, such as a randomised trial or a systematic review. Following the guideline for the design ensures that the essential details are present, and many journals require a completed checklist. Page et al. (2021) provide the example of such a guideline for systematic reviews, and the principle applies to other designs.

Results should be presented with effect sizes, intervals and sample sizes, not with p-values alone. A p-value conveys whether a difference crosses a threshold, but not its size or precision, and the reader needs both. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval should be the central element of the reporting, since it shows the range of effects consistent with the data. Figures and tables should show the data honestly, with axes that begin where they should and with the uncertainty visible.
