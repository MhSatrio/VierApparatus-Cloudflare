A drug discovery pipeline begins with a biological target, a molecule whose activity can be changed to affect a disease. The first task is to find compounds that bind the target and alter its activity, these are called hits, and the best hits are then optimised into leads that are potent, selective and suitable for the body. Jorgensen (2004) described how each stage requires different information, and computation is valuable at each one.

Lead optimisation improves the potency of a compound and its selectivity for the target over related proteins, and it adjusts its properties of absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion. The optimised candidate is then tested in cells and in animals to establish its activity and its safety, before it is considered for clinical trials. Each step eliminates many candidates, and the progress of the pipeline is measured by the rate at which candidates survive.

Most candidates fail. They may prove inactive in more complex systems, toxic in animals or ineffective in patients, and the attrition is high at every stage. The high rate of failure is a central fact of drug development, and it means that small improvements in the early stages have large effects on the cost of the whole pipeline. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that is promising in an early stage may not survive the later ones, and the attrition should be reported.

Computation reduces attrition by eliminating unsuitable candidates early and by predicting properties before compounds are made. The productivity of the pipeline is described by metrics such as the hit rate, the quality of leads and the proportion that reaches the clinic, and these should be reported with their uncertainty. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the criteria used at each stage so that the decisions can be reviewed.
