A hit from a screen may be a false positive, a compound that appears active without binding the target in the intended way. Aggregation is one of the most common causes. Some compounds form colloidal particles at the concentrations of the assay, and these particles adsorb the enzyme and inhibit it non-specifically. Shoichet (2004) noted that such artefacts can make a library appear to contain many active compounds, and the hits must be checked for them.

Some compounds are active across many unrelated screens, because they are reactive, redox-active or they interfere with the detection method. These pan-assay interference compounds are a particular hazard, since they appear as hits for many targets and can mislead a discovery programme. Their chemical features are recognised by filters that flag known interference motifs, and the filters remove them from the libraries used for screening.

Fluorescence and absorbance artefacts distort the readout of an assay. A compound that fluoresces at the wavelength of the detection, or that absorbs the light used to measure the signal, may appear to change the activity without acting on the target. The artefacts depend on the assay format, and their detection requires controls in which the target is absent.

Hits must be confirmed by orthogonal assays, which measure the same activity by a different method, so that an artefact of one method is unlikely to appear in the other. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that rests on one method may be an artefact of that method, and the orthogonal assay is the check. The hit rates should be reported with the assays used and the criteria by which a compound was judged active, and Cumming (2014) argues that the hit rates should be given with their intervals.
