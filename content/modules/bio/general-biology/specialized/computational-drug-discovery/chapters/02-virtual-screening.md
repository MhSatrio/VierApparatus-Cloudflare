Virtual screening evaluates a library of compounds in silico to choose those most likely to bind a target, so that only a small number need to be tested in the laboratory. The library must be prepared first, with its structures standardised, its charges assigned and its three-dimensional conformations generated, since the quality of the preparation affects the results. Shoichet (2004) described how virtual screening became practical as libraries grew to millions of compounds, and the preparation is a large part of the work.

In a structure-based screen, each compound is docked into the binding site of a protein whose three-dimensional structure is known, and the fit is scored. The compounds with the best scores are selected for testing. The structure of the target must be appropriate, since the binding site may change shape when a ligand binds, and the choice of structure can determine which compounds are found.

A ligand-based screen does not need a protein structure. It searches for compounds that resemble known active compounds, on the assumption that similar molecules have similar activity. The choice of similarity measure determines the compounds found, and the method is limited by the diversity of the known actives.

The success of a screen is measured by its hit rate, the proportion of the selected compounds that are confirmed active, and this must be compared with the hit rate of a random selection from the library. Ioannidis (2005) described how a method that selects the best of many candidates will tend to overstate its performance, and the random comparison is the honest measure of the gain. The hits must be confirmed experimentally, since the score alone does not establish activity. Cumming (2014) argues that the hit rate should be reported with its interval.
