Forensic biology applies biological methods to questions of law, most often to link a person to a crime scene or to exclude a suspect. The first forensic use of DNA profiling, described by Gill et al. (1985), compared genetic patterns from a sample with those of a person, and the method has since become the principal tool of forensic identification. Its power comes from the rarity of the profiles in the population, and the strength of any match is an estimate that must be stated with care.

The topic begins with DNA profiling and its statistics. The profile is made from the lengths of short tandem repeats at a set of loci, and the probability that an unrelated person shares the profile is the random match probability. That probability depends on the allele frequencies in a reference population, which must be representative and must be stated. The chapters that follow examine mixtures, degraded samples, the identification of species and body fluids, the interpretation of evidence and the quality of the laboratory.

Forensic laboratories have been reviewed to improve their standards, and Butler (2015) described the international initiatives that aim to make forensic DNA analysis consistent and accurate. The standards cover the validation of methods, the proficiency of analysts and the reporting of results, and they are necessary because errors in forensic evidence can have severe consequences.

The interpretation of forensic evidence depends on the proposition that is compared, and a match probability alone does not answer the question of who left the sample. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result can be misread when its context is ignored, and Cumming (2014) argues that the uncertainty of each statistic should be reported. Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the methods, the reference data and the calculations so that forensic results can be checked.
