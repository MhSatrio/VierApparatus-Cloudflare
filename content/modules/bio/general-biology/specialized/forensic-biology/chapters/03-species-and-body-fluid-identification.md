A forensic investigator often needs to know two things about a sample: who left it, and what kind of material it is. The species of a stain may matter when the question is whether blood came from a human or an animal, and the body fluid may matter when the question concerns the activity that produced the trace. Butler (2015) noted that the identification of the source of a sample is part of the standards that a forensic laboratory must meet, and the methods must be validated for the purpose.

Species are identified by sequencing a standard marker region, such as the mitochondrial DNA or a DNA barcode, and comparing it with a reference library. The identification depends on the completeness of the library, and a species that is absent from it can be assigned only to a relative. The library must be curated, and the identification should state the reference used. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the reference version and the matching criteria, since the identification depends on them.

Body fluids are identified by the expression of cell-type markers in messenger RNA, since blood, saliva, semen and skin express characteristic genes. The presence of the marker indicates the fluid, but the marker may also be expressed in other tissues, and its specificity must be tested. In mixed samples, a marker from one fluid may be detected in another, and the interpretation requires the expected levels of each marker.

The degradation of RNA and DNA with time after deposition has been studied as a means of estimating the time since a trace was deposited. The degradation depends on the environment, and the estimates are uncertain, so they must be presented with their intervals and the conditions. Ioannidis (2005) described how an estimate of time may be overstated when the environmental conditions are unknown, and Cumming (2014) argues that the identification should be reported with its specificity and its limit of detection.
