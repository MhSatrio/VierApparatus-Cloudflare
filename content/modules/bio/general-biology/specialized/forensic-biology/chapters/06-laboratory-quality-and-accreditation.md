A forensic result can change the course of a person's life, and the laboratory that produces it must meet standards that make the result reliable. Butler (2015) described the international efforts to set and enforce these standards, which cover the methods, the people and the records. The aim is that a result obtained in one laboratory should be as reliable as the same result obtained in another, and that the reliability can be demonstrated.

Each method must be validated before it is used on casework. The validation establishes the sensitivity, the specificity, the reproducibility and the range of samples over which the method performs, and it is documented so that the method can be assessed. A method validated with clean reference samples may fail on the degraded or mixed samples of casework, and the validation must include such samples.

Proficiency testing checks the performance of analysts against samples of known composition that are sent to them without the answer. The results reveal the errors of individual analysts and of the laboratory as a whole, and they provide evidence that the laboratory's results are reliable. Ioannidis (2005) described how a method that is not checked against known truth can produce confident errors, and proficiency testing provides this check.

Accreditation to international standards requires the laboratory to show that its quality system meets the requirements, and it is confirmed by audit. The documentation and the audit trail allow each result to be traced to the samples, the methods and the analysts involved. Independent review of results, by a second analyst who does not know the conclusion of the first, reduces the risk of confirmation bias. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with its records, and Cumming (2014) argues that the error rates of the laboratory should be reported with their intervals.
