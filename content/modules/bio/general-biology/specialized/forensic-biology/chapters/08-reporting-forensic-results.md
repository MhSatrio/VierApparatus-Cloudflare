A forensic biology report must allow the court and other experts to understand what was done, what was found and how strongly the evidence supports each proposition. The evidence items should be listed with their handling, since the chain of custody and the condition of the sample affect the result. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and for a forensic result those means are the records of the methods, the profiles and the calculations.

The methods used should be named, with their validation and their version, and the profiles obtained should be reported with the loci, the quality of each locus and any drop-out or mixture. The controls and their outcomes must be given, since a contaminated control invalidates the batch in which it appears. Butler (2015) described the standards that require these elements, and the report should show that they were met.

The propositions considered should be stated explicitly, and the statistics should be reported as likelihood ratios or match probabilities with the reference population and the assumptions behind them. The limitations of the evidence and the uncertainties in the statistics should be stated, since a result presented without its limits invites misinterpretation. Cumming (2014) argues that the uncertainty should be shown with each statistic, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a statistic that is quoted without its assumptions can mislead.

The analyst and the independent review should be identified, and the records should allow the work to be audited. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended keeping the inputs, steps and outputs of an analysis together, and in forensic science this is a requirement of the adversarial process.
