Forensic evidence is interpreted against propositions, which are the competing accounts of how the evidence arose. The prosecution proposes one account and the defence another, and the evidence is evaluated by how much more likely it is under one than under the other. Gill et al. (1985) introduced DNA evidence to the courts, and the interpretation of its statistics against propositions has since become the accepted framework in many jurisdictions.

The propositions should be formulated at a level that the evidence can address. A proposition at the level of source states whether the suspect is the donor of the DNA, while one at the level of activity states how the material came to be deposited. The choice of level determines which factors must be considered, and a DNA result addresses the source far more directly than the activity.

The likelihood ratio is the ratio of the probability of the evidence under the prosecution proposition to its probability under the defence proposition. It states the strength of the evidence, and it is multiplied by the prior odds held by the court to give the posterior odds. The prior odds are a matter for the court and not for the scientist, and the scientist's role is to report the likelihood ratio.

A common error is the transposed conditional, in which the probability of the evidence given innocence is mistaken for the probability of innocence given the evidence. A small random match probability does not imply that the suspect is probably guilty, because the number of people who could have left the sample must also be considered. Ioannidis (2005) described how such errors arise when the context of a statistic is ignored, and Cumming (2014) argues that the strength of the evidence should be communicated with its uncertainty and in terms that a non-specialist can understand accurately.
