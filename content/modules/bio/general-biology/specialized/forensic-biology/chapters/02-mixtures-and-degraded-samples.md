Many forensic samples contain the DNA of more than one person, and the profile of a mixture is harder to read than that of a single contributor. Each locus may show more alleles than a single person carries, and the peak heights reveal the relative contributions. The number of contributors is estimated from the maximum number of alleles seen at any locus and from the balance of peak heights, and the estimate must be made before the mixture can be interpreted. Butler (2015) described the standards that guide such interpretations in the forensic community.

Probabilistic genotyping calculates the likelihood of the observed peaks under every combination of genotypes that the contributors could have, and it uses models of peak height, stutter and degradation to weigh each combination. The approach uses all the information in the profile rather than discarding loci with ambiguous peaks, and its results depend on the model that is assumed. The model and its parameters must be validated and reported.

Low-template DNA, from a small amount of cells, produces stochastic effects in which an allele may fail to be detected, a phenomenon called drop-out. Drop-out makes a heterozygote appear as a homozygote, and it biases the statistics if it is not modelled. Contamination introduces alleles from another source, called drop-in, and both effects must be considered in the interpretation of a low-level profile.

Degraded samples lose the longer DNA fragments first, so that the loci with larger amplicons fail while the smaller ones remain. The profile is therefore incomplete in a pattern that depends on the degradation, and the interpretation must account for the missing loci. The threshold that separates true alleles from noise is set from validation data, and the choice affects the profile that is interpreted. Ioannidis (2005) described how a threshold set after the result is known may bias the interpretation, and Cumming (2014) argues that the uncertainty of the likelihood ratio should be reported with its interval.
