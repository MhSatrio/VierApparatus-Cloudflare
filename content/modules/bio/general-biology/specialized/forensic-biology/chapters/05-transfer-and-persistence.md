A DNA profile tells the investigator whose DNA is present, but it does not tell how that DNA came to be where it was found. The material may have been left by direct contact, or it may have been carried there by an intermediary, and the answer changes the meaning of the evidence. Gill et al. (1985) established the identification of individuals from DNA, and the question of transfer is the next step in the interpretation of such evidence.

Primary transfer occurs when a person touches an object directly, and the amount of DNA transferred depends on the pressure, the duration of contact and the shedding propensity of the individual. Some people shed large amounts of DNA and others little, and this individual variation must be considered when the amount of DNA is used in interpretation. Secondary transfer occurs when the DNA is carried by an intermediary, such as a shared object or another person, and it can produce a profile on an object that the suspect never touched directly.

The persistence of DNA on surfaces depends on the time since deposition, the nature of the surface and the environment, including temperature, humidity and exposure to light. Persistence experiments measure how the amount of DNA changes over time under controlled conditions, and they provide the background against which a case is evaluated. The experiments must reflect the conditions of the case, since persistence varies widely.

Transfer and persistence allow the investigator to address activity-level questions, such as whether a person handled an object or was present in a room. The answers depend on the experiments, and they carry uncertainties that must be stated. Ioannidis (2005) described how an activity-level inference can be made with more confidence than the evidence allows, and Cumming (2014) argues that the transfer and persistence rates should be reported with their intervals. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the conditions of each experiment so that its results can be applied to a case.
