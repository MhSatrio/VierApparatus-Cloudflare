Forensic DNA is so sensitive that a trace of material can produce a profile, and this sensitivity is also its weakness. Contamination can enter a sample at the scene, during collection, in the laboratory and during analysis, and a contaminating profile can be indistinguishable from a genuine one. The control of error is therefore central to the reliability of forensic evidence, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that is not checked for such artefacts may be mistaken for a finding.

Contamination in the laboratory can come from the analysts, from the reagents or from earlier samples, and it is detected by negative controls that contain no DNA and that are processed with each batch. A contaminated control shows that the batch is compromised, and the results must be treated with suspicion. Cumming (2014) argues that the frequency of contamination events should be reported with its interval, since a small number of events may be informative about the process.

Errors of inclusion and exclusion both occur. A coincidental match can include an innocent person, and this is controlled by the statistics of the profile, while allelic drop-out can exclude a true donor by failing to detect an allele. Each error has its own cause and its own control, and the laboratory's rate of each should be known.

Cognitive bias can influence the interpretation of an ambiguous profile, since the analyst who knows the suspect may be inclined to see a match. Procedures that shield the analyst from task-irrelevant information, and the independent review of the interpretation, reduce this risk. Butler (2015) described the standards that address these issues, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the controls, the procedures and the corrective actions for every case.
