DNA in the faeces of an animal reveals what it has eaten, because prey DNA survives digestion in part and can be amplified and sequenced. Metabarcoding of faecal samples can identify many prey species in a single sample, and it has revealed diets that were hard to observe directly. Taberlet et al. (2012) described the approach as one of the most productive applications of metabarcoding in ecology, since it allows the interactions of animals to be documented without watching them.

The proportion of reads from a prey species is not the proportion of the diet it represents. Prey differ in how much of their DNA survives digestion and in how efficiently the primers amplify them, so a prey that is eaten in large quantities may yield few reads, and a prey that is eaten rarely may yield many. The read counts must therefore be treated as presence indicators, and quantitative diet estimates require calibration with feeding trials.

Detections of prey can be combined into a network of interactions, which describes the food web of a community. The network is built from the links detected in many samples, and its structure reflects the detection of links rather than their strength. A link that is missed by the sampling is absent from the network, and the network should be reported with the sampling effort that produced it.

The same approach detects parasites and pathogens that share the host, and the DNA on pollen reveals the plants visited by pollinators. These detections show associations but not necessarily the strength or the consequence of the interaction. Ioannidis (2005) described how an association detected in a few samples may not reflect the typical interaction, and Cumming (2014) argues that the detection rates should be reported with their intervals.
