The structure of a population is the pattern of genetic differences among its parts, and it records how individuals have moved. In many species, populations that are farther apart are more genetically different, a pattern called isolation by distance, which arises when gene flow is limited by the distance that individuals disperse. Allendorf et al. (2010) described how genomic data have made this pattern easier to detect and to separate from other causes of differentiation.

Barriers such as rivers, mountains, roads and fragmented habitat interrupt gene flow, and they produce differentiation that is not explained by distance alone. A river that separates two populations of the same distance from each other may show more differentiation than a stretch of land of equal length, and the detection of such barriers requires comparing the observed differentiation with the expectation from distance. Habitat fragmentation can reduce gene flow among patches and leave each patch genetically distinct.

Assignment tests identify the population from which each individual most likely came, using the allele frequencies of the populations. The test is useful for detecting dispersers, who carry the alleles of a population other than the one where they were found. The probability of assignment depends on how differentiated the populations are, and assignments of recent migrants are uncertain when differentiation is weak.

Dispersal of individuals is not the same as gene flow, since an individual that disperses may fail to breed, and a gene may move through many breeding events without the individual that carried it moving far. Sex-biased dispersal, in which one sex disperses more than the other, can be revealed by comparing markers with different inheritance, such as mitochondrial DNA, which is maternally inherited, and nuclear markers. Cumming (2014) argues that the estimates of gene flow should be reported with their intervals, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a pattern of structure may reflect the sampling design rather than the process.
