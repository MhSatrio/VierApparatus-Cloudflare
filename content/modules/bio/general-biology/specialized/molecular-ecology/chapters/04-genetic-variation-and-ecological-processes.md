Genetic variation and ecological processes influence one another. The genetic structure of a population reflects its dispersal and its landscape, and the genetic variation within it affects its ability to persist and to respond to change. Allendorf et al. (2010) emphasised that genetic diversity is one of the links between ecology and evolution, and the molecular tools make it possible to study this link directly in natural populations.

Genetic diversity can affect the resilience of a population to disturbance. A population with more variation may contain individuals that tolerate a new stress or a new pathogen, and the population can recover more readily. The evidence for this relation comes from comparisons of populations with different diversity and from experiments that manipulate diversity, and the strength of the effect varies with the trait and the disturbance.

Genetic variation in traits affects the interactions of a species with its predators, competitors and parasites. A prey whose genotypes differ in their defences may maintain a community structure that a uniform population would not, and a parasite may evolve in response to the host's genetic variation. These interactions produce evolutionary dynamics that feed back on the ecology of the community.

The separation of genetic from environmental effects on a trait requires a design in which individuals of different genotypes are grown in a common environment, which is called a common garden, or in which they are moved between environments in a reciprocal design. Without such a design, differences among populations may reflect the environment rather than the genes, and the environment often correlates with the genetic structure across a landscape. Cumming (2014) argues that the genetic and environmental components should be estimated with their intervals, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a correlation across a landscape may be mistaken for a causal link.
