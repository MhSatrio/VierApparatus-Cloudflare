Species interact with one another in ways that shape the structure of communities, and these interactions leave signatures in their genomes. Parasites and their hosts evolve in response to one another, pollinators and plants adjust their traits, and symbionts coevolve with their hosts. Genomic methods reveal the genes involved, and Allendorf et al. (2010) emphasised that genomics can identify the genetic basis of traits that were once inferred only from their ecological effects.

Host-parasite coevolution can produce arms races, in which a host evolves resistance and the parasite evolves to overcome it. The genes that recognise the parasite and those that the parasite uses to evade the host often show signatures of rapid evolution, and the comparison of the two genomes can reveal the molecular battle. The inference of such signatures requires that the rapid evolution be distinguished from demographic effects and from the weaker constraints on other genes.

Mutualisms such as pollination and symbiosis have genomic bases that can be identified through comparisons of the partners and of their expression. The genes that control the production of nectar, of symbiotic signals and of the receptors that recognise partners are candidates for the mutualism, and their function must be tested. Expression changes in response to a partner can reveal the genes that are switched on during the interaction.

A genomic signal of interaction is hard to attribute when several species interact, because a change in one gene may reflect the presence of a competitor, a predator or a parasite. Experimental tests, in which the partner is removed or added and the gene's response is measured, are needed to attribute the signal. Ioannidis (2005) described how a signal that is attributed to a favoured partner may have a different cause, and Cumming (2014) argues that the change in expression should be reported with its interval. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the interacting species and the experimental design.
