Migration links the populations of a species across space and time, and molecular tools can track it. A migrant that arrives at a breeding site carries the genetic signature of the population it came from, and genetic assignment compares its genotype with the reference populations to estimate its origin. The method has been used to show which breeding sites are connected by migration and how migrants move between them, and it complements tracking by tags and by satellites.

Assignment is reliable only when the populations differ enough in their allele frequencies to be distinguished, and when the reference populations are sampled well. Where the populations are similar, the assignment of an individual is uncertain, and a resident may be wrongly identified as a migrant. The assignment probability should be reported for each individual, and migrants should be identified only with a probability that is stated.

Isotopes and genetic markers can be combined to locate the origin of a migrant more precisely. Isotopes reflect the environment where the animal grew, and genetic markers reflect its ancestry, so the two together can narrow the origin. Each has its own variability, and the combination must account for it. Ioannidis (2005) described how the agreement of two methods may be overstated when they share a common bias, and the independence of the two sources should be considered.

The number of migrants per generation can be inferred from the genetic differentiation between populations, since migration homogenises allele frequencies. The inference assumes that the populations are in equilibrium between migration and drift, and that the migrants are equally likely to breed in either population. These assumptions must be stated, since violations change the estimate. Cumming (2014) argues that the number of migrants should be reported with its interval, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the reference populations and the assignment method.
