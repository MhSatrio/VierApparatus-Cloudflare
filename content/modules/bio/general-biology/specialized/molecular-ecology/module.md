Molecular ecology uses DNA and related molecules to answer questions about how organisms live, move and interact in nature. Before molecular methods, ecologists relied on observation and on marks that were visible or recaptured, and many of the processes that govern populations were hidden. Genetic markers now reveal who is related to whom, where individuals came from, what an animal has eaten and which species share a habitat, and Taberlet et al. (2012) showed how these methods extend to whole communities.

The topic begins with the genetic structure and movement of populations, which reveal how far individuals disperse and how isolated populations are. The methods then extend to the identification of species and individuals, the reconstruction of diets and interactions, and the link between genetic variation and ecological processes. Allendorf et al. (2010) argued that genomic approaches are changing what can be inferred about populations, and the molecular data must be interpreted with the same care as any other evidence.

Each inference carries errors. Samples may be contaminated, genotypes may be wrong, and the markers may not represent the whole genome. The sampling design determines which processes can be detected, and a study that samples poorly may mistake a pattern of sampling for a pattern of nature. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that is drawn from a small or selected sample may not describe the population, and Cumming (2014) argues that each estimate should be reported with its interval.

Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the samples, the methods and the analyses so that the inferences about nature can be reproduced and their assumptions examined.
