Coherence is the quantum property that allows a system to exist in a superposition of states, with a definite phase relation between the components. When a system is coherent, its components interfere, and this interference can show up as oscillations in measurements of its behaviour. Lambert et al. (2012) explained that coherence is the feature that distinguishes a quantum process from a classical one, and it is the feature that quantum biology seeks in living systems.

Coherence is fragile. Any interaction with the environment, such as collisions with surrounding molecules or the vibrations of a protein, carries away information about the phase of the system, and the coherence decays. This decoherence is the central obstacle to quantum effects in warm systems, because the environment of the cell is continually interacting with the molecules that might be coherent.

The thermal energy available at biological temperatures is comparable to the energy spacings of many molecular states, which means that thermal fluctuations can easily populate and mix the states. The energy spacings of electronic states are large, but those of vibrational and rotational states are small, and the coherence that could survive thermal noise is more likely to involve states of this kind. The comparison is therefore essential to the plausibility of any claimed effect.

The time over which coherence can persist must be compared with the time scales of the biological process. A coherence that lasts a few femtoseconds may be irrelevant to a process that takes microseconds, and only coherence that outlasts the process it is said to assist can have a functional role. Lambert et al. (2012) emphasised this comparison, and any claim should state the lifetime of the coherence and the duration of the process. Cumming (2014) argues that the lifetime should be reported with its interval, since it is estimated from noisy measurements. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the conditions under which the lifetime was measured.
