Enzymes accelerate reactions by lowering the energy barrier that the reactants must cross. For a light particle such as a hydrogen atom, the barrier can be crossed by quantum tunnelling, in which the particle passes through the barrier rather than over it. Lambert et al. (2012) described tunnelling as one of the most plausible quantum effects in biology, since the mass of hydrogen is small enough for its wave nature to matter at the temperatures of living cells.

Tunnelling is revealed most clearly by the kinetic isotope effect. Replacing hydrogen with deuterium, which is twice as heavy, slows the reaction if it proceeds by tunnelling more than the classical theory predicts, because the heavier particle tunnels less readily. A large isotope effect, larger than the maximum expected from the classical model, is taken as evidence of tunnelling, and the size of the effect is measured by comparing the rates of the two isotopes.

The temperature dependence of tunnelling differs from that of thermal crossing. Classical crossing follows the Arrhenius relation, in which the rate rises steadily with temperature, while tunnelling contributes a rate that is nearly independent of temperature. Measurements across a range of temperatures can therefore separate the two, and a curved relation in the logarithm of the rate against inverse temperature indicates a contribution from tunnelling.

The role of tunnelling in enzyme catalysis is debated. Some enzymes, such as alcohol dehydrogenase and certain amine dehydrogenases, show large isotope effects, but the interpretation depends on the model used to predict the classical rate. Ioannidis (2005) described how a conclusion that depends on a model may not hold when the model changes, and Cumming (2014) argues that the isotope effects and their intervals should be reported with the model and the temperature range.
