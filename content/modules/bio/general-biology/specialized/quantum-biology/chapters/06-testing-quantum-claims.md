A claim of a quantum effect in biology must be tested against the classical explanations that could produce the same observation. The first step is to state the classical null model, which describes the observation through the classical physics and chemistry of the system. Only the observations that the classical model cannot explain can support a quantum claim, and the null model must be stated before the data are compared with it.

Control experiments remove the proposed quantum element and show whether the effect disappears. If the effect is due to coherence in a particular molecule, a modification that destroys the coherence should also remove the effect, while a modification that leaves the coherence intact should leave it. The controls must be designed so that they change only the quantum element, since a control that alters the chemistry or the structure produces a result that cannot be attributed to the quantum property alone.

Isotope substitution alters a quantum property, such as the mass of a nucleus or the spin of an electron, while leaving the chemistry essentially unchanged. Engel et al. (2007) and others used spectroscopic methods that separate electronic from vibrational signals, and the isotope effect provides an independent test of the mechanism. A substitution that changes the signal in the predicted way supports the mechanism, and one that does not weakens it.

The statistical treatment of replicate measurements is essential, since the signals are small and noisy. Ioannidis (2005) described how a single striking experiment may not replicate, and the independent replication of each result is needed. Cumming (2014) argues that each effect should be reported with its interval across replicates, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the controls, the null model and the analysis used for each test.
