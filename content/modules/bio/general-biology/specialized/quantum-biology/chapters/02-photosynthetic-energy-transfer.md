A photosynthetic organism captures light in antenna complexes made of many pigment molecules held in a protein scaffold. An absorbed photon creates an excitation, which must travel through the array to a reaction centre, where it drives the chemistry. The classical description treats the transfer as a series of incoherent hops between neighbouring pigments, each hop driven by the energy difference and the coupling. The efficiency of this process is high in nature, and the question is whether quantum coherence helps.

The coherent model proposes that the excitation spreads as a wave over several pigments at once, sampling many paths and reaching the reaction centre more quickly than hopping allows. Engel et al. (2007) reported oscillations in a two-dimensional spectrum of a bacterial light-harvesting complex that lasted hundreds of femtoseconds, and they interpreted the oscillations as evidence of coherent superposition among the excited states. The result attracted wide attention, because it suggested a quantum mechanism for efficient transfer.

The interpretation has been challenged. The oscillations may arise from vibrations of the protein that couple to the pigments, and the vibrational motion can produce similar signals without any long-lived electronic coherence. Lambert et al. (2012) noted that the distinction between electronic and vibrational origins is subtle, and the experiments must separate them. Ioannidis (2005) described how an interpretation that fits an attractive model may be accepted before its alternatives are excluded.

The efficiency of energy transfer can be high without quantum coherence, since a classical model with suitable disorder and coupling can also reach the reaction centre efficiently. The functional significance of coherence therefore depends on showing that it improves the transfer beyond what the classical model achieves. Cumming (2014) argues that the transfer times should be reported with their intervals, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the spectroscopic conditions and the analysis that separates the signals.
