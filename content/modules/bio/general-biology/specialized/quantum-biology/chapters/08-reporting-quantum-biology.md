A quantum biology claim is extraordinary, and its report must allow the reader to judge whether the effect is quantum or classical. The system, the temperature and the medium in which it was studied should be given first, since quantum effects are most sensitive to these conditions. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and for a quantum measurement those means include the sample preparation, the apparatus and the analysis.

The method and its time resolution must be stated, along with the classical null model against which the effect is compared. Lambert et al. (2012) emphasised that a quantum claim is meaningful only relative to the classical alternative, and the report should show why the classical model fails to explain the observation. Without this comparison the claim cannot be assessed.

The controls that remove the proposed quantum element, and the manipulations such as isotope substitution used to test the mechanism, should be reported with their outcomes. A control that alters the chemistry alongside the quantum property does not isolate the effect, and the report should state how each control was designed. Ioannidis (2005) described how the selection of controls after the outcome is known can support a desired claim.

The lifetime and the magnitude of the effect should be reported with their intervals, along with the noise floor and the calibration of the instrument. Cumming (2014) argues that the uncertainty should be shown with each estimate, and the spectra and the analysis should be deposited with identifiers. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended keeping the inputs, steps and outputs together, so that the quantum claim can be checked and reanalysed by others.
