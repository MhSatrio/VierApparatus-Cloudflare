Quantum technologies are now used to sense and image biological systems, and they offer a sensitivity that classical instruments cannot reach. Nitrogen-vacancy centres in diamond are atomic-scale defects whose spin state changes with the magnetic field, and they can be used as sensors of magnetic fields and temperature at the scale of a single cell. Lambert et al. (2012) noted that such devices bring the methods of quantum physics into the study of living systems, even when the biology itself does not depend on quantum effects.

A diamond sensor placed near a cell can detect the weak magnetic fields produced by the currents of neurons or by the magnetic particles within the cell. The measurement is made by reading the optical signal of the defect as the spin is manipulated, and the sensitivity depends on the coherence time of the spin and on the optical readout. The device is a tool, and the quantum effect it uses is a property of the sensor rather than of the cell.

Quantum dots, semiconductor nanocrystals whose emission depends on their size, are used as fluorescent labels that are brighter and more stable than conventional dyes. Their emission is quantum-confined, so the colour depends on the particle size, and their use in imaging does not require any quantum effect in the cell. The quantum properties of the label are exploited for measurement, and this distinction should be kept clear.

Superconducting devices measure extremely weak biomagnetic signals from the heart and the brain, and they are limited by the noise of the instrument and the environment. The noise floor must be stated, since a signal close to it can be indistinguishable from noise. Cumming (2014) argues that the sensitivity and the measured signals should be reported with their intervals, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a measurement near the noise floor can produce an apparent effect that is not real.
