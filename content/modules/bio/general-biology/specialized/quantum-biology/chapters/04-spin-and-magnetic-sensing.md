Some migratory birds appear to navigate by the Earth's magnetic field, and one proposed mechanism involves quantum spin. When light strikes a photoreceptor protein, it can create a radical pair, two molecules each with an unpaired electron whose spins are initially correlated. The fate of the pair, whether it recombines or separates into products, depends on the orientation of the spins, and a weak external magnetic field can change this balance. Lambert et al. (2012) identified this radical pair mechanism as one of the best-supported candidates for a quantum effect in biology.

The protein cryptochrome is a leading candidate for the magnetoreceptor in birds. It contains a chromophore that can form a radical pair upon illumination, and its response to a field would be detected through a change in the signalling state of the cell. The mechanism would produce a directional signal because the field interacts with the anisotropic hyperfine couplings of the radicals, so the effect depends on the orientation of the field relative to the molecule.

The time scale of the mechanism is set by the spin relaxation and by the hyperfine interactions, and the radical pair must live long enough for the field to act. The sensitivity of the mechanism to these parameters determines whether it could work in the warm, noisy environment of the cell, and the parameters must be measured or bounded before the mechanism can be judged plausible.

Behavioural experiments support the idea that the magnetic compass depends on light and on the radical pair. Birds lose their magnetic orientation under some wavelengths of light, and the dependence on light is consistent with a photochemical mechanism. Yet these experiments do not identify the molecule, and a magnetic effect must be separated from chemical effects of the treatment. Cumming (2014) argues that the orientation effect should be reported with its interval, and Ioannidis (2005) described how the evidence from behavioural studies may be confounded by the methods used.
