Quantum biology asks whether quantum effects, which are most evident at very small scales and low temperatures, play a functional role in living systems. Lambert et al. (2012) surveyed the candidate processes, which include energy transfer in photosynthesis, the catalysis of enzymes and the orientation sense of some animals. The field is young and contested, and the central question is whether these effects are important to the organism or only present in it.

The best-studied case is coherent energy transfer in photosynthetic light-harvesting complexes. Engel et al. (2007) reported oscillations in the spectra of a bacterial complex that were interpreted as quantum coherence, in which excitation moves through several pigments along multiple paths at once. The interpretation was attractive, since coherent transfer could in principle move energy more efficiently than classical hopping, but the interpretation has been debated because similar oscillations can arise from vibrations in the protein environment.

The cell is warm, wet and noisy, and quantum states lose their coherence rapidly in such an environment. This process, called decoherence, limits the time over which quantum effects can persist, and it is a strong argument against the functional importance of long-lived coherence in biology. Any claim of a quantum effect must show that it survives the decoherence expected in the cell, or it must explain why it does not need to.

The chapters that follow examine the experimental methods, the candidate processes and the controls needed to make a quantum biology claim credible. Cumming (2014) argues that the effect size and its uncertainty should be reported, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a striking claim may be accepted before its classical alternatives are excluded. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the measurement conditions and the analysis for each claim.
