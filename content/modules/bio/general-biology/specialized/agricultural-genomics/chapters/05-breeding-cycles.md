A breeding programme advances by cycles of crossing, selection and testing, and the rate of progress depends on the gain made in each cycle and on the length of the cycle. The genetic gain per year is the product of the selection intensity, the accuracy of selection and the genetic variance, divided by the cycle length. Heffner et al. (2009) showed that genomic selection can raise the gain per year by shortening the cycle, since candidates can be chosen from their genotypes before they are grown in the field.

A conventional cycle takes many years, because the plants must be grown, crossed, selected and tested in several environments before a variety is released. Each stage adds time, and the length of the cycle limits how fast a programme can respond to new pests or to changing markets. Methods that shorten the cycle therefore have a large effect on the rate of progress.

Speed breeding grows plants under controlled light and temperature for long days, which allows several generations to be produced in a year. The method is effective for crops that respond to these conditions, but the plants grown under them may differ from those in the field, and the selection made under speed breeding must be checked in the target environment. Doubled haploid technology produces homozygous lines from a single cross in one generation, which removes several cycles of inbreeding.

Each method shortens the cycle at a cost. Genomic selection depends on the accuracy of prediction, which falls when the candidates are unlike the training population, and speed breeding may select for traits that do not express in the field. The trade-off between cycle length and accuracy determines which combination maximises the gain per year, and the choice should be justified with the data. Cumming (2014) argues that the gain should be reported with its interval, since it depends on several uncertain parameters.
