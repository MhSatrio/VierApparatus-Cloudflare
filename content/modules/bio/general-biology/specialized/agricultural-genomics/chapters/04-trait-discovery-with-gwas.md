Association mapping discovers the genes that control a trait by testing each marker in a diverse panel of varieties for an association with the trait. The panel contains many lines whose history of recombination is long, which gives high resolution, so that a significant marker lies close to the causal gene. The method uses the natural variation of the crop rather than a cross, and it can reveal alleles that a single cross would not contain.

The population structure of a crop panel, which reflects its breeding history, can produce associations that are not causal. Lines from the same breeding programme share alleles and traits, so a marker that distinguishes the programmes may appear associated with the trait. The structure must be modelled, for example with principal components or a kinship matrix, and the correction must be reported. Heffner et al. (2009) noted that population structure affects both association mapping and genomic prediction.

Biparental populations, derived from a cross between two lines, give greater power for common alleles, but their resolution is low because recombination is limited to the cross. Linkage mapping in such populations identifies regions of the genome, which are then fine-mapped with additional markers. The two approaches complement each other, and the choice depends on the trait and the resources.

A candidate gene near a significant marker is not proven to control the trait, because several genes may lie in the same region and the causal variant may be elsewhere. The candidate must be confirmed by a functional test, such as a mutant, a knockdown or a transgenic line, before it is credited with the trait. Ioannidis (2005) described how a candidate identified by association is often not confirmed, and Cumming (2014) argues that the effect sizes should be reported with their intervals. The threshold for significance must be corrected for the number of markers.
