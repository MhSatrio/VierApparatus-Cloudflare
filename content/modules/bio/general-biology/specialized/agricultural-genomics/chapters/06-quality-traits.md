Quality traits determine whether a crop is useful for food, feed and industry. The protein and oil content of grain, the starch composition that governs the use of cereals in baking and brewing, the content of micronutrients and vitamins, and the flavour and texture of fruit are all quality traits. They are controlled by biosynthetic pathways whose genes can be identified and manipulated, and Heffner et al. (2009) noted that quality traits are often measured in the same trials as yield and are included in the breeding objective.

The pathways that produce these compounds involve several enzymes, and the regulation of the pathway determines how much of each compound accumulates. The genes that regulate the pathway are often transcription factors that control the expression of many enzymes at once. Identifying these genes allows the quality of a crop to be changed by altering a small number of regulators.

Quality traits are strongly influenced by the environment, and the same genotype may produce grain with different protein content in different seasons and soils. The genetic effect must be separated from the environmental effect, which requires trials across several environments and an analysis that partitions the variation. A genotype that appears superior in one season may not be superior in another.

Quality is measured by chemical assays and by near-infrared spectroscopy, which predicts composition from the spectrum of the sample. The spectroscopic predictions depend on calibration against chemical reference values, and the calibration error must be reported. Cumming (2014) argues that the quality measures should be reported with their intervals, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a quality trait that is not measured consistently across trials may give misleading comparisons.
