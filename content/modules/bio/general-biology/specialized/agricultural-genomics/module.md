Agricultural genomics applies the methods of genomics to the crops and livestock on which food depends. Genomes of many crops have now been sequenced, and the genes that control yield, quality and resistance can be identified and used in breeding. Godfray et al. (2010) argued that the productivity of agriculture must rise to meet demand, and genomics offers a means of raising it faster than conventional breeding alone, by identifying the genetic variants that matter.

The topic begins with genomic selection, in which a model trained on genotyped and phenotyped individuals predicts the breeding value of new candidates from their genotypes alone. Heffner et al. (2009) described how this approach can shorten breeding cycles and increase the rate of genetic gain, and the method is now used in many crops and livestock. The chapters that follow examine the discovery of genes, the management of genetic resources, the acceleration of breeding, the genomics of quality, and the data and reporting that the work requires.

The success of genomic approaches depends on the quality of the phenotypes and the environment in which they are measured. A genetic prediction that is accurate in one environment may fail in another, and the field trials that provide the phenotypes must be designed to represent the target environments. Cumming (2014) argues that the accuracy of prediction should be reported with its interval, and Ioannidis (2005) described how an accuracy estimated on the training data overstates the value of the model.

Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended that the genotypes, the phenotypes, the environments and the analysis be recorded and deposited so that the predictions and the discoveries can be reproduced and built upon.
