Animal models allow experiments that cannot be done in the target species, but their results must be translated to that species, and the translation often fails. A model reproduces some features of a disease and omits others, and a treatment that acts on the reproduced features may have no effect on the disease as it occurs in the target. The choice of model therefore determines what can be concluded, and the features it reproduces should be stated with the result.

The husbandry of the animals and the conditions of the laboratory influence the results. Stress, diet, housing and the microbiome of the animal all change its physiology, and an effect observed in one laboratory may not appear in another. Ioannidis (2005) described how such unrecognised variation may produce findings that do not replicate, and the conditions should be recorded and, where possible, varied.

Many treatments that succeed in animal models fail in the target species, and the reasons include the differences in biology, the choice of endpoints and the design of the experiments. The rate of failure is a measure of how well the models predict, and it should be reported. Cumming (2014) argues that the effect in the animal should be reported with its interval, since the size of the effect determines whether it is likely to matter in the target.

Design features reduce the risk of misleading results. Randomised allocation of animals to treatments, blinding of the observers to the treatment, and the reporting of all experiments rather than only the successful ones, protect against bias. The number of animals should be justified by a power analysis, so that the study uses no more animals than needed and is able to detect the effect that matters. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the design and the conditions of every experiment.
