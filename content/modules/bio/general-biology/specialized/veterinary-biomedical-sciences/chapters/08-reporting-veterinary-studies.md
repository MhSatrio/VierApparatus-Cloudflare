A veterinary study informs decisions about the health of animals and, through them, of people, and its report must allow those decisions to be judged. The species, the breed, the age and the sex of the animals should be given first, since each affects the disease and the response to treatment. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and for a veterinary study those means include the animals, the procedures and the analysis.

The source and the health status of the population must be described, including whether the animals were vaccinated, treated or exposed to disease before the study. The design should be stated with its randomisation, its blinding and its controls, and the intervention should be described with its dose and its timing. Woolhouse and Gowtage-Sequeria (2005) showed that the health of one species can depend on the others it meets, and the population should be characterised for such exposure.

The outcome measures must be given with their source, whether objective or reported by the owner or the observer, and the diagnostic tests should be described with their validation. Cumming (2014) argues that each estimate should be reported with its interval, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a result from a small number of animals may not be reproduced in the wider population.

The sample size, the number of animals lost to follow-up and the statistical analysis should be reported, along with the deposition of the data and the protocols with identifiers. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended keeping the inputs, steps and outputs of an analysis together, so that the study can be checked and its implications for animal and human health assessed.
