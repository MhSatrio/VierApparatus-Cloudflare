Surveillance detects disease in animal populations so that outbreaks can be controlled before they spread. Passive surveillance relies on farmers and veterinarians to report animals that are sick or that die, and it is inexpensive but biased, since the animals that are reported are those that are noticed and that the owner chooses to report. Jones et al. (2008) relied on such reports in their analysis of emerging disease, and the reporting effort itself varies between regions, which affects the apparent distribution of events.

Active surveillance samples animals systematically, and it detects infection in animals that show no signs. The design of an active survey must state the probability of detecting disease if it is present at a given prevalence. The number of animals to sample depends on the prevalence that is to be detected, the population size and the accuracy of the test, and the calculation should be reported.

Sentinel animals are kept to detect pathogens that circulate in the environment, and wildlife is sampled to detect pathogens that are carried without causing disease in the host. These approaches reveal the presence of a pathogen before it reaches livestock, and they provide early warning. The sampling of wildlife is often opportunistic, and its bias must be considered.

Surveillance data from different regions and species must be integrated to show the overall pattern, and the integration requires consistent definitions and reporting. The detection of a novel pathogen by sequencing of samples is increasingly used, and the detection must be followed by confirmation and characterisation. Ioannidis (2005) described how the apparent absence of a disease may reflect an inadequate surveillance effort, and Cumming (2014) argues that the prevalence should be reported with its interval and the detection probability of the surveillance.
