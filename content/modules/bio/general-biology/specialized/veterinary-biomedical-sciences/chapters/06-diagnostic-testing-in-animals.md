Diagnostic tests detect infection or disease in animals, and they are used to decide treatment, to certify animals for trade and to monitor disease in populations. Serological tests detect antibodies that the animal has made in response to a pathogen, and they show past or present exposure. Molecular tests detect the genetic material of the pathogen itself, and they can reveal an active infection with high sensitivity. Each type answers a different question, and the choice should follow the question asked.

The performance of a test is described by its sensitivity, the proportion of infected animals that test positive, and its specificity, the proportion of uninfected animals that test negative. These properties belong to the test, but the predictive value of a positive or negative result depends on the prevalence of the disease in the population tested. A test with high sensitivity and specificity applied to a population in which the disease is rare will give many false positives, and the predictive value must be calculated for the population of use.

Vaccination complicates serological diagnosis, because a vaccinated animal produces antibodies that a test cannot distinguish from those of an infected animal. Tests that distinguish the two, such as those that detect antibodies to proteins not present in the vaccine, are used in such cases, and their performance must be validated in vaccinated populations. Ioannidis (2005) described how a test validated in unvaccinated animals may fail in the populations in which it is used.

Field tests are used at the point of care, and their accuracy under field conditions may be lower than in the laboratory because of the temperature, the handling and the skill of the operator. Validation against a reference standard, in the conditions of use, is essential, and the agreement with the reference should be reported with its interval. Cumming (2014) argues that the sensitivity and specificity should be given with their intervals, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the test, the reference standard and the population.
