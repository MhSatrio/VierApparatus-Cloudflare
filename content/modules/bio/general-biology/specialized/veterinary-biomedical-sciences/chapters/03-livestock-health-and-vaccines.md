Livestock disease causes large economic losses and threatens food supplies, and vaccination is one of the principal means of control. A vaccine induces an immune response that protects the vaccinated animal from infection or from severe disease, and when enough animals are vaccinated the spread of the pathogen is interrupted across the herd, a protection called herd immunity. The proportion of animals that must be vaccinated for this depends on how readily the pathogen spreads, and the threshold coverage can be estimated from the reproduction number.

Woolhouse and Gowtage-Sequeria (2005) described how pathogens that infect several host species can persist in livestock populations and reinfect vaccinated herds from other hosts, which can limit the protection that vaccination achieves. The control of such pathogens therefore requires coordination across the species that carry them.

The efficacy of a vaccine is measured in a trial, in which vaccinated and control animals are compared under controlled conditions. The effectiveness in the field may be lower, because the animals are exposed to a wider range of challenges, because the vaccine is not stored or administered perfectly, and because the coverage is incomplete. Both must be reported, since a vaccine with high efficacy may still fail when its coverage is low.

Husbandry and biosecurity reduce transmission in ways that complement vaccination, including the control of movement of animals, the cleaning of premises and the separation of age groups. The economic losses from disease justify the cost of these measures, and the benefit should be estimated with its uncertainty. Cumming (2014) argues that the efficacy and the coverage should be reported with their intervals, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a vaccine trial with few animals may give an efficacy that does not hold in the field.
