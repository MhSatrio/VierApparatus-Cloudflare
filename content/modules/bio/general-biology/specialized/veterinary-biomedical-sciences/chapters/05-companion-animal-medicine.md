Companion animals suffer from many of the diseases that affect people, including cancers, diabetes and heart disease, and they also suffer from diseases that arise from their breeding. Purebred dogs and cats carry an increased risk of some genetic disorders, because the populations are small and the breeds were formed by selection on a few individuals. Genomic studies have identified the variants responsible for many of these disorders, and the identification allows the risk to be tested and reduced.

A genetic test for a breed disorder must be validated before it is offered. The association of a variant with the disease must be replicated across individuals and breeds, and the test's sensitivity and specificity must be known. Ioannidis (2005) described how an association found in one population may not hold in another, and a test offered without validation may give false reassurance or false alarm.

Clinical trials in companion animals test drugs and procedures against the outcomes that matter to the animal and its owner. Randomised allocation and controls are essential, as in human trials, and the outcomes should be measured objectively where possible. The number of animals in many veterinary trials is small, and the estimates of effect have wide intervals, which should be reported.

Owner-reported outcomes, such as the perceived quality of life of the animal, are subject to bias, because owners who have chosen a treatment may view its effect more favourably. Blinding of the owner and the use of validated scales reduce this bias. Cumming (2014) argues that the effect should be reported with its interval, and the source of each outcome, objective or owner-reported, should be stated.
