Antimicrobials are used in livestock to treat disease, to prevent it and, in some countries, to promote growth. Each use exerts selection on the bacteria in the animal, and the bacteria that survive are those carrying resistance. The resistant bacteria and their genes can leave the animal in faeces, enter the food chain and reach people, and they can persist in the environment. Woolhouse and Gowtage-Sequeria (2005) described how the transfer of pathogens between hosts links animals and people, and resistance follows the same routes.

Resistance genes move between bacteria on mobile genetic elements, which allows them to spread between species and across the boundary between animals and people. The genes can therefore appear in human pathogens even when the human has had no direct contact with the antimicrobial. The extent of this transfer varies with the antimicrobial, the bacterium and the environment, and it must be measured to be controlled.

Resistance in animal bacteria is monitored by culture and susceptibility testing, and increasingly by sequencing the resistance genes directly. The sequencing identifies the genes and their carriers, but it does not show whether the genes are expressed or functional. The susceptibility method, the breakpoints used to define resistance and the sample source should all be reported, since each affects the proportion of resistant isolates.

Use can be reduced by vaccines that prevent infection, by husbandry that limits the spread of disease and by stewardship that restricts treatment to the cases that need it. The effect of each measure on resistance must be measured over time. The attribution of resistance in human infection to animal use is difficult, because people acquire resistance from many sources, and the evidence for attribution should be stated with its uncertainty. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the isolates, their sources and their testing methods so that the surveillance can be repeated.
