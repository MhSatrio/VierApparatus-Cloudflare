A zoonosis is an infection that passes from animals to people, and many of the pathogens that cause the most serious new diseases have arisen this way. The emergence of a zoonosis requires a spillover, in which a pathogen moves from one host to another and establishes an infection there, and most spillovers do not lead to sustained transmission. Woolhouse and Gowtage-Sequeria (2005) showed that the pathogens with the widest host ranges are the most likely to cross into new species, since they are already adapted to many hosts.

Jones et al. (2008) analysed the global record of emerging infectious diseases and found that the events are concentrated in particular regions and that many are zoonotic. They linked the emergence to drivers such as land-use change, the intensification of livestock production, the trade in animals and the expansion of human settlements into wild habitats, which bring people, livestock and wildlife into closer contact. The drivers act together, and their effects differ between regions.

The evidence linking a driver to spillover is often observational, and the association must be examined for confounding. Regions with high rates of emergence may differ in many respects, and the attribution of an event to one driver requires controlling for the others. Ioannidis (2005) described how such attributions are easily overstated, and the evidence should be assessed with its uncertainty.

Surveillance detects spillover early enough for a response to be effective. The detection of a new pathogen in an animal or a person must be followed by identification and assessment of the risk of spread. Cumming (2014) argues that the rates of emergence should be reported with their intervals, since the record of events is incomplete and biased towards places with better surveillance.
