Veterinary biomedical sciences apply the methods of biomedicine to the health of animals, and through animals to the health of people. Animals are the source of many of the pathogens that affect people, and they are the patients, the production stock and the companions whose welfare the profession serves. The field draws on infectious disease research, pharmacology, diagnostics and epidemiology, and it must consider the animal, the people who keep it and the environment that links them.

The topic begins with zoonoses and emerging infectious disease. Woolhouse and Gowtage-Sequeria (2005) examined the host range of pathogens and found that those that infect many host species are more likely to emerge in new ones, and Jones et al. (2008) showed that the emergence of new diseases is concentrated in particular regions and driven by land use and by wildlife. The later chapters examine animal models, livestock health, antimicrobial resistance, diagnostics, surveillance and the reporting of studies.

Animal models are used in biomedical research, and the translation of results from animals to the species of interest is often uncertain. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result from a model may not hold in the setting it is meant for, and Cumming (2014) argues that the effects in animals should be reported with their intervals before any claim of benefit is made for another species.

Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the animals, the procedures and the analyses so that veterinary findings can be reproduced and their implications for animal and human health judged.
