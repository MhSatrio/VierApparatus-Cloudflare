Genomic markers can reveal the stress of organisms before harm is visible in their numbers or their appearance. The expression of stress-response genes rises when an organism encounters a pollutant, a change in temperature or a pathogen, and the pattern of expression can identify the agent. Such a marker is attractive for monitoring because it responds quickly, but it must be interpreted with care, since a rise in expression may be an adaptive response with no harmful consequence.

Some stressors leave lasting marks on the genome, through methylation of DNA and other epigenetic changes that record past exposure. These marks can persist for generations in some organisms, and they may serve as a record of exposure. The stability and the inheritance of such marks differ among species, and they must be established before the marks are used as evidence of past exposure.

Populations exposed to a stressor may show genetic signatures of selection, in which the frequencies of certain alleles change because individuals carrying them survive better. Population genomic scans identify loci whose variation is unusual compared with the rest of the genome, and these loci may be under selection by the stressor. The scans are sensitive to demographic history and to population structure, which can produce similar signals without selection.

Markers are useful only if they relate to fitness and to population health. A marker of stress that does not change survival, growth or reproduction tells the manager little about the consequences of exposure. The link must be demonstrated in controlled exposures and in the field, and the marker should be validated across the conditions in which it will be used. Ioannidis (2005) described how a marker that is validated in one setting may fail in another, and Cumming (2014) argues that the relation between marker and effect should be reported with its interval. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the markers, the populations and the confounders considered.
