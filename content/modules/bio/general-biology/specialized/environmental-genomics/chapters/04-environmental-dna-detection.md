Environmental DNA detection looks for a particular species by measuring whether its DNA is present in a sample. Organisms shed DNA through their skin, faeces, mucus and gametes, and the DNA can be detected in the surrounding water or soil for some time afterwards. Taberlet et al. (2012) described the principle of finding organisms from their genetic traces, and the method is now used to detect rare and elusive species in many habitats.

The DNA does not persist indefinitely. It is degraded by ultraviolet light, by microbes and by heat, and it is carried away from its source by currents and by the movement of the water. The persistence of the DNA, and therefore the distance over which a detection indicates the species, depends on these processes. A detection indicates presence within the time and the distance that the DNA persists, which must be estimated for each habitat.

The probability of detecting a species depends on the concentration of its DNA, the number of samples taken and the sensitivity of the assay. A species at low density may be missed by a single sample, and the probability of detection must be estimated from replicated sampling. Cumming (2014) argues that the detection probability should be reported with its interval, since a non-detection does not establish absence.

False positives arise from contamination of the samples and from the amplification of closely related species that share the target sequence. Both must be controlled by negative samples and by a test of the specificity of the primers. Ioannidis (2005) described how a false positive can be mistaken for a real finding, and the controls should be reported.

Occupancy models account for imperfect detection by combining the detections across samples with the probability of detecting the species when it is present. They estimate the proportion of sites that the species occupies, with an interval that reflects the uncertainty in the detection probability. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the sampling design, the controls and the model used to estimate occupancy.
