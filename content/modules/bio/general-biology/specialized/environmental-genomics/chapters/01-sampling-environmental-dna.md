Environmental DNA is collected from water, soil and sediment, and the method of collection determines which organisms are represented. Water is usually filtered through membranes with a defined pore size, which captures cells and some free DNA, while the rest passes through. The pore size determines the size of the organisms retained, and a survey that filters only large volumes may detect the organisms that shed the most DNA while missing the rare ones. Taberlet et al. (2012) emphasised that the sampling protocol shapes the species list as much as the sequencing does.

Soil and sediment are cored or scooped, and the DNA is extracted from a small portion of the sample. The extraction must recover DNA of varied length and quality, since degraded DNA may be lost by methods that favour long fragments, and the efficiency of extraction differs between sample types. The choice of extraction method determines which organisms are detected, and it should be validated against known communities where possible.

Samples must be preserved quickly, since DNA degrades after collection, and the preservative and the temperature affect how much DNA survives. A sample that is left warm for hours may lose much of its DNA and bias the result towards organisms whose DNA is more stable. The time and conditions of preservation should be recorded with the sample.

The number of field replicates determines the chance of detecting rare species, since a single sample may miss a species that is present at low abundance. The replicates should be spread across the habitat, and the number needed depends on the habitat and the target species. Cumming (2014) argues that the detection probability should be reported with its interval, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a survey with few samples may miss what is there. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the collection, the preservation and the extraction for every sample.
