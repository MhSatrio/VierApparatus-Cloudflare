Environmental DNA is extremely sensitive, which makes it vulnerable to contamination. A single molecule of DNA from the laboratory, from a previous sample or from the field can be amplified into a detectable signal, and such contamination can create the appearance of a species that is absent. The control of contamination is therefore a central part of any environmental genomic survey, and the controls must be designed to detect each source of error.

Field blanks are samples of clean water or sterile material taken and handled in the field alongside the real samples. They reveal contamination introduced during collection, such as DNA carried on equipment or in the air. Extraction blanks contain no sample and are processed with the others, revealing contamination in the extraction reagents, and PCR negative controls reveal contamination during amplification. Each control shows a different source, and all should be reported with the survey.

Cross-contamination between samples processed together is a particular risk, since a sample with a high concentration of a species can transfer its DNA to the next sample. Physical separation of the pre-amplification and post-amplification work, the use of dedicated equipment and decontamination of the laboratory reduce the risk. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that is not checked for such artefacts may be mistaken for a real signal.

A read count below a threshold may be treated as contamination, and the threshold must be chosen from the negative controls. A species detected in a control at a level comparable with the samples cannot be attributed with confidence to the environment. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with its controls, and the threshold and the controls should be recorded. Cumming (2014) argues that the detection rates in the controls should be reported with their intervals, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the control results with every survey.
