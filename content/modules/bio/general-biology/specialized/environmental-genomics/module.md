Environmental genomics applies sequencing to samples taken directly from the environment, so that the organisms present in a habitat can be surveyed without capturing them one by one. A litre of water or a gram of soil contains DNA from many organisms, and sequencing it reveals the species and the genes that are present. Taberlet et al. (2012) showed that a short standard gene region can be used to identify many species from such a sample, a method called metabarcoding, which has transformed the survey of biodiversity.

The topic begins with the sequencing of environmental samples, which must be collected, preserved and extracted in ways that represent the community without bias. The chapters that follow examine metabarcoding, the sequencing of entire communities, the detection of species from traces of their DNA, the genomic markers of stress, the bioinformatic pipelines that turn reads into species lists, and the control of contamination.

Each step introduces error. Samples may be contaminated, primers may amplify some species better than others, and the reference databases used to assign sequences to species are incomplete. The species list produced by a survey is therefore an estimate with errors of omission and of commission, and the reporting must show the errors that could affect it. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result produced by a long pipeline may hide its errors, and the pipeline should be validated.

Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended that the samples, the pipelines and the databases be recorded so that a survey can be reproduced, and Cumming (2014) argues that the number of species and the abundances should be reported with their uncertainty.
