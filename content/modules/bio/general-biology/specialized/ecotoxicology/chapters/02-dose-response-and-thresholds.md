The dose-response relation is the central relation of toxicology. It describes how the probability or the magnitude of an effect changes as the dose of a toxicant increases. Many regulatory decisions rest on a threshold model, in which there is a dose below which no effect occurs, and the acceptable exposure is set below the threshold with a margin for uncertainty. The model is simple and widely used, and it gives a clear basis for setting limits.

The threshold is often estimated as the no-observed-effect level, the highest dose at which no effect was seen in an experiment. This estimate depends on the doses tested and on the number of animals in each group, and a study with few animals may fail to detect an effect that is present. The benchmark dose is an alternative, which fits a curve to the data and estimates the dose that produces a defined change in response, with its confidence interval. It uses all the data and gives a more precise estimate than the no-observed-effect level.

For carcinogens, a linear relation without a threshold is often assumed at low doses, on the reasoning that a single molecular event can initiate a tumour. This assumption is precautionary, but it is not a demonstration that no threshold exists. Vandenberg et al. (2012) argued that for hormone-active chemicals, the assumption of a monotonic relation may be wrong, and the dose-response can be non-monotonic, with an effect at a low dose that disappears at a higher one.

A non-monotonic relation requires a different design, with doses spanning the low range and sufficient animals to detect the effect at each dose. A design that tests only high doses will miss the low-dose effect, and a design with few doses cannot distinguish the shapes. Ioannidis (2005) described how the choice of doses can determine the conclusion, and Cumming (2014) argues that the response at each dose should be reported with its interval and the confidence band of the fitted curve.
