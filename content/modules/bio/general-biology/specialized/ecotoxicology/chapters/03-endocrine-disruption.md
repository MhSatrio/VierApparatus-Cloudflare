Endocrine disrupting chemicals are substances that interfere with the hormone systems of animals. Some mimic natural hormones and bind their receptors, some block the receptors, and others alter the production or breakdown of hormones. Vandenberg et al. (2012) described how such chemicals can disturb development, reproduction and metabolism, because hormones act as signals that set the course of these processes. A chemical that interferes with the signal can therefore change the outcome of development even when the dose is small.

The timing of exposure is critical. The developing organism is most sensitive during windows such as the foetal period and early infancy, when hormones direct the formation of organs and tissues. An exposure in these windows may have effects that are not visible until adulthood, and an exposure in adulthood may have much smaller effects. The timing must therefore be recorded and tested, since a study that exposes adults alone may miss the effects that matter most.

Low-dose effects have been reported for some endocrine disrupting chemicals, and Vandenberg et al. (2012) argued that these effects can occur at doses below those that produce overt toxicity, and that the dose-response can be non-monotonic. The interpretation is contested, because the reproducibility of low-dose effects has been variable and because the mechanisms may differ from the standard toxicology assumptions. Ioannidis (2005) described how a striking effect reported in a few studies may not replicate, and the independent replication of low-dose effects is essential.

A study that aims to detect such effects must have adequate numbers of animals, a range of doses including low ones, a control group, and measures of the relevant endpoints in whole organisms. Mixtures of compounds that act on a shared pathway may also act together, and this must be considered. Cumming (2014) argues that the effect at each dose should be reported with its interval, and the design should be stated in full.
