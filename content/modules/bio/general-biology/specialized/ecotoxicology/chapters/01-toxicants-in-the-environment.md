Toxicants reach the environment from many sources. Industrial processes release metals, solvents and persistent organic compounds, agriculture applies pesticides and fertilisers to fields, and households and hospitals release pharmaceuticals and the additives of consumer products into sewage. Vandenberg et al. (2012) noted that many of the chemicals of concern are used in everyday products, which makes the exposure of the public broad and continuous.

Once released, a toxicant is transported and transformed. It may be carried by water, deposited in sediment, volatilised into air or taken up by soil, and along the way it may be broken down by light, by microbes or by chemical reaction. The fate determines where the toxicant ends up and in what form, and a compound that is stable in water may be degraded quickly in soil. Each compartment must be considered in the assessment of exposure.

The bioavailable fraction of a toxicant is the part that organisms can absorb, and it is often much smaller than the total concentration. A metal bound to organic matter or a compound sorbed to sediment may be unavailable, while the free form may be highly toxic. The measurement of total concentration alone can therefore overstate the exposure, and the bioavailable fraction should be measured where possible.

Persistent compounds resist breakdown and remain in the environment for long periods, and some are transported over long distances. Their concentrations may be low in water and yet high in organisms that accumulate them. The measurement of environmental concentrations requires sampling and analytical methods with stated detection limits, and Cumming (2014) argues that the concentrations should be reported with their intervals across sites and times. Ioannidis (2005) described how a single sample may not represent the exposure of an area, and the sampling should be designed to capture the variation.
