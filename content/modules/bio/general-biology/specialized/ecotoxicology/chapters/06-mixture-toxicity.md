Organisms are rarely exposed to a single chemical, and the effect of a mixture may differ from the effect of its components. Two reference models describe the expected effect of a mixture. Concentration addition applies when the chemicals share a mode of action, and their effects add as if they were different amounts of a single compound. Independent action applies when the chemicals act through different mechanisms, and their effects combine as probabilities, so that the response to the mixture is the chance that at least one component produces it.

Concentration addition is used for chemicals with a common target, such as a group of compounds that all inhibit the same enzyme. The toxic units of each chemical are summed, and a mixture with a total of one toxic unit is expected to produce the effect of a single unit of the reference compound. The model is simple and widely used in regulation, since it allows the risk of a mixture to be estimated from the components.

Independent action describes chemicals that act on different targets, and the combination of their effects is the probability that the organism responds to any one of them. The predicted effect is lower than the sum of the individual effects, and it approaches the sum only at high effects. The choice between the two models depends on the mode of action, which must be understood before the mixture is assessed.

Synergism occurs when the combined effect exceeds the prediction of the reference model, and antagonism when it falls below. Both are detected by comparing the observed response with the expected response, and the comparison must use the appropriate model. Ioannidis (2005) described how a deviation from a model may be an artefact of the model's assumptions or of the experimental design, and Cumming (2014) argues that the deviation should be reported with its interval.
