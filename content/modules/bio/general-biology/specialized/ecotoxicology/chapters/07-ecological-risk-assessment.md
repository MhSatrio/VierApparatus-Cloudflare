An ecological risk assessment estimates the likelihood that a chemical will harm the ecosystem in which it is released. The assessment follows a structured sequence, beginning with the problem formulation, which identifies the ecosystem components and the endpoints that are to be protected. The exposure assessment then estimates the concentrations that the organisms will receive, and the effects assessment derives the concentrations at which harm is expected. The risk is characterised by comparing the two.

The effects assessment usually relies on toxicity tests on a few species, which give the concentrations that cause specified effects. These data are used to derive a concentration that should protect most species. The species sensitivity distribution describes how the sensitivity varies among species, and a low percentile of this distribution, such as the concentration that affects five per cent of species, is commonly used as the protective level. The derivation is a statistical estimate with its own uncertainty.

When data are limited, an uncertainty factor is applied to the lowest effect concentration to give a safe concentration. The factor is a judgement, and it is often large, which makes the resulting limit conservative but uncertain. Vandenberg et al. (2012) argued that such factors may not capture effects at low doses, and the adequacy of the factor for a given chemical must be considered.

The risk characterisation compares the predicted exposure with the protective concentration, and the ratio or the difference is taken as the measure of risk. The assumptions of each step, the choice of species and the factors applied should be reported with the result. Cumming (2014) argues that the protective concentration should be given with its interval, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a decision that depends on an arbitrary factor is only as sound as the factor. The evidence for each factor should be recorded.
