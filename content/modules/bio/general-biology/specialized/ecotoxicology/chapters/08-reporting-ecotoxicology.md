An ecotoxicology report must allow another group to repeat the test and to check its conclusions. The chemical should be identified with its purity and its source, since impurities can change the toxicity, and the test organisms should be described with their species, their strain and their life stage. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and for a toxicity test those means include the chemical, the organisms and the procedure.

The exposure conditions and the measured concentrations should be reported. A nominal concentration is what was intended, but the measured concentration is what the organisms received, and the two can differ substantially as a chemical is lost or adsorbed. Vandenberg et al. (2012) emphasised that the dose and the timing of exposure determine the response, and both must be reported. The endpoints should be given with the methods used to measure them.

The dose-response analysis should be reported with its model, its parameters and their intervals. The benchmark dose or the effective concentration should be stated with its confidence interval, and the number of replicates at each concentration should be given. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval conveys the precision of the estimate, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a result from few replicates may not hold. The biomarkers and the risk assessment assumptions should be reported with their validation and their uncertainty factors.

The raw data and the analysis code should be deposited with identifiers. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended keeping the inputs, steps and outputs together, so that the toxicity result can be reanalysed and its risk estimate checked by others.
