Ecotoxicology studies how chemicals and other agents harm living things, from individual cells to populations and ecosystems. Toxicants enter the environment from industry, agriculture, medicine and the products of daily life, and they reach organisms through water, air, soil and food. The field asks how much of a substance is harmful, to which organisms, at what concentration and over what time, and its answers guide the regulation of chemicals and the management of contaminated sites.

The topic begins with the sources and the fate of toxicants, which determine the exposure that organisms receive. The dose-response relation describes how the effect grows with the concentration, and the question of whether a threshold exists below which no harm occurs is central to the regulation of many substances. Vandenberg et al. (2012) argued that for some hormone-active chemicals the relation can be non-monotonic, so that low doses may have effects that high doses do not, and this challenges the assumption of a simple threshold.

Endocrine disrupting chemicals act on the hormone systems of animals, and their effects on development and reproduction can occur at very low doses. The evidence for such effects is contested, and the interpretation depends on the design of the experiments and on the dose range studied. The later chapters examine bioaccumulation, biomarkers, mixtures and risk assessment, which together determine how a chemical is judged in practice.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the results of toxicity studies can be shaped by the choice of dose and endpoint, and Cumming (2014) argues that the effects should be reported with their intervals across doses and replicates. Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the exposure conditions, the test organisms and the analysis so that toxicity results can be reproduced and compared.
