Planetary health is the idea that human health cannot be separated from the health of the natural systems on which people depend. The climate, the air, the water, the soil, the biodiversity of ecosystems and the chemistry of the environment all influence the health of populations, and changes in them can affect disease, nutrition and well-being. Whitmee et al. (2015) set out this view in a commission report, which argued that the threats to health from environmental change are large and that they require coordinated responses across many disciplines.

The topic begins with the pathways by which environmental change reaches human health. Climate change alters the range of disease vectors and the seasons of transmission, pollution harms the lungs and the cardiovascular system, and the degradation of ecosystems changes the supply of food and water. Each pathway has its own evidence and its own time scale, and the linkages between them complicate any attribution.

The later chapters examine specific pathways, the models that predict the risk of vector-borne disease, the indicators by which planetary health is monitored, and the reporting of studies. The attribution of a health change to an environmental cause is difficult, because many factors vary together and because the health effects often lag the exposure. Ioannidis (2005) described how associations between environment and health can be exaggerated when confounders are not controlled, and Cumming (2014) argues that the size of each effect should be reported with its interval.

Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the environmental and health data, and the methods by which they were linked, so that the claimed effects can be reproduced and their attribution assessed.
