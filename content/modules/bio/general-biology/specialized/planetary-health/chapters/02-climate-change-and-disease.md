Climate change affects the transmission of many infectious diseases, and the clearest examples involve vectors such as mosquitoes and ticks. Warmer temperatures extend the range of these vectors to higher latitudes and altitudes, and they shorten the time that a pathogen needs to develop inside the vector, which increases the rate of transmission. Whitmee et al. (2015) identified these changes as among the most important health consequences of the altered climate, and they are a major concern for public health planning.

The effect of temperature on transmission is not a simple increase. Each vector and each pathogen has an optimal range of temperature, above which survival and development decline, and the response is therefore non-linear. The consequences of warming depend on where a population lies relative to this optimum, and a region that becomes too hot may see a fall in transmission even as the climate warms.

Extreme weather affects disease in other ways. Floods can spread water-borne pathogens and bring rodents into contact with people, while droughts can concentrate people around scarce water and alter the ecology of disease reservoirs. These effects are often episodic, and they are harder to project than the gradual shift of vector ranges.

Attributing a change in disease to climate requires controlling for other drivers, including land use, travel, urbanisation and the public health measures that reduce transmission. A rise in cases may reflect a change in surveillance, a change in the population at risk or a change in the vector control programme as well as the climate. Ioannidis (2005) described how associations can be attributed to the wrong cause when confounders are not controlled, and Cumming (2014) argues that the estimated effect of climate should be reported with its interval and the adjustments made.
