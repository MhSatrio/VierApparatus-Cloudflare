A planetary health study claims a link between an environmental change and a health outcome, and its report must allow that link to be assessed. The exposure should be described with its measure and its source, and the outcome with its definition and the data from which it was counted. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and for a linked study those means include the data sources and the linking procedure. Whitmee et al. (2015) argued that such links are the basis of planetary health, and their reporting must be clear.

The population, the place and the period studied should be stated, along with the method by which exposure and outcome were linked. The confounders that were controlled, and those that could not be measured, should be listed, since a link may disappear once a confounder is accounted for. Ioannidis (2005) described how an association is easily overstated when the confounders are not reported, and the adjustments should be shown.

The lag between exposure and outcome should be reported, since many environmental effects on health appear only after a delay. A study that measures the outcome at the wrong time may miss the effect or attribute it to the wrong exposure. The lag structure of the model should be stated, and the sensitivity of the result to the chosen lag should be shown.

The estimate of the association should be reported with its interval and the number of cases on which it is based. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval conveys the uncertainty of the estimate, and the linked data and the analysis code should be deposited with identifiers. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended keeping the inputs, steps and outputs together, so that the link between environment and health can be checked and extended by others.
