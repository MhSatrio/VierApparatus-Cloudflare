Air pollution is among the largest environmental causes of disease. Fine particles, which are small enough to penetrate deep into the lungs, are associated with respiratory and cardiovascular illness and with premature death, and ozone and nitrogen dioxide add to the burden. Whitmee et al. (2015) identified air pollution as a major threat to health in the Anthropocene, and the evidence for its effects has grown with the improvement of exposure measurement and the number of studies.

Fine particles reach the alveoli and can pass into the circulation, where they provoke inflammation and affect the blood vessels and the heart. The inflammatory response and the changes in blood clotting are plausible routes to the cardiovascular events that pollution is linked with. The mechanisms are supported by experimental evidence, but the size of the effect in people depends on the dose and on the susceptibility of the individual.

The dose-response relation for particles appears to be approximately linear, with harm observed even at concentrations below the regulatory limits in many places. This suggests that no threshold exists below which the particles are harmless, and it has implications for the setting of standards. The shape of the relation at low concentrations is uncertain, and the uncertainty should be reported with the estimate.

Exposure is measured by fixed monitors, by satellite observations and by personal sensors, and each method has its limits of accuracy and coverage. Confounding by smoking, socioeconomic status and temperature is a serious concern, since these factors correlate with pollution and with disease. Ioannidis (2005) described how an association between pollution and disease may be overstated when confounders are not controlled, and Cumming (2014) argues that the effect should be reported with its interval and the adjustments that were made.
