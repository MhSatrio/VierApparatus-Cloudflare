The link between biodiversity and human health has been debated. One hypothesis, the dilution effect, proposes that a greater diversity of host species can reduce the risk of some zoonotic infections, because the pathogen is spread among many hosts, some of which are poor carriers. The contrary view holds that biodiversity loss can increase transmission in some systems, for example when the species that remain are the good carriers. Whitmee et al. (2015) placed biodiversity among the natural systems that underpin health, and the relation is not yet settled.

Natural ecosystems provide medicines, clean air and water, and a buffer against floods and other extreme events. These services are real but often difficult to value and to measure, and their contribution to health depends on the state of the ecosystem and the population that uses it. The provision of services is one of the routes by which biodiversity may benefit health.

The evidence from field studies on the dilution effect is mixed, and the results depend on the system studied and on the measure of diversity used. Studies that find a relation may be confounded by habitat, climate and the sampling effort, and the causal direction is hard to establish. Ioannidis (2005) described how a striking relation in a few systems may not generalise, and the evidence should be examined across many systems.

Ecosystem restoration is proposed as a health intervention, since the restoration of habitat can provide services and can change the ecology of disease. The effect of restoration must be measured against a control and over a period long enough for the ecosystem to respond. Cumming (2014) argues that the change in diversity and in disease should be reported with their intervals, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the scale, the taxa and the diversity measure used in each study.
