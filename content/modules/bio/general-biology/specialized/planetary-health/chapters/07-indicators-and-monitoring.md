Planetary health is monitored with indicators, which are measurable quantities that summarise the state of the environment or of the health of a population. Environmental indicators describe the climate, the quality of air and water, and the state of land and biodiversity. Health indicators describe mortality, illness and nutrition. Whitmee et al. (2015) argued that a monitoring system should cover both, and that the link between them should be made explicit, since the purpose of monitoring is to show how environmental change affects health.

Linking indicators connect an environmental change to a health outcome in the same population and over the same time. Such a link is essential, because an environmental indicator and a health indicator measured in different places or periods cannot show that one affects the other. The design of the monitoring must therefore place both indicators in the same population, with a sampling scheme that allows the link to be estimated.

Monitoring programmes require spatial and temporal coverage, and their cost rises with the precision and the frequency of the data. A trade-off exists between the cost of collection and the precision of the indicator, and the design should state which precision is needed for the decisions the monitoring supports. Satellite and sensor data reduce the cost of environmental monitoring, but they must be calibrated against ground measurements, and their errors must be reported.

Indicators must be reported with their definitions, their sources and their uncertainties. A change in an indicator may reflect a change in its definition or its collection rather than in the phenomenon measured, and this must be checked. Cumming (2014) argues that each indicator should be reported with its interval, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a trend in an indicator may be an artefact of the method used to collect it. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the definitions and the procedures for every indicator.
