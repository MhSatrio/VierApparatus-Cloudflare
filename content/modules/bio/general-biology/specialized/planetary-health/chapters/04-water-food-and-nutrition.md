Human nutrition depends on food and water that come from ecosystems, and the health of those ecosystems determines how much food and water are available and what they contain. Drought and flooding reduce crop yields, rising temperature alters the nutritional content of some crops, and the decline of fisheries removes a source of protein for many coastal populations. Whitmee et al. (2015) argued that these links make the health of ecosystems inseparable from the health of the people who depend on them.

Water security depends on the quality of the water as well as its quantity. Agricultural runoff carries fertiliser and pesticide into rivers and groundwater, and sewage carries pathogens, and where safe water is not supplied these contaminants cause water-borne disease. The removal of these hazards requires treatment and management of the catchment, and the health gain from them can be measured.

Food systems are changing, and many populations are shifting towards diets high in processed food and low in fresh produce. This nutritional transition is associated with rising rates of obesity and of diet-related disease, and its drivers include trade, marketing and urbanisation as well as the availability of food. The transition is an example of how changes in the food system, rather than in the ecosystem alone, shape health.

Food and water security must be measured with indicators that connect the state of the ecosystem to the nutrition of the population. Such indicators should be measured in the population affected, since a national average may hide the populations most at risk. Cumming (2014) argues that the indicators should be reported with their intervals across groups, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a relation between ecosystem and nutrition may be confounded by income and other factors that must be controlled.
