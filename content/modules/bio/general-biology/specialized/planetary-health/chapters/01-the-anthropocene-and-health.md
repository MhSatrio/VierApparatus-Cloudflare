The Anthropocene is the proposed period in which human activity has become the dominant influence on the planet's physical and biological systems. Its signs include the change of the climate, the loss of biodiversity, the alteration of the nutrient cycles and the transformation of land and sea. Whitmee et al. (2015) argued that these changes are environmental and also threats to health, since they alter the conditions in which people live, eat and fall ill.

The concept of planetary boundaries describes the limits within which human societies can operate safely over the long term. Several of these boundaries are believed to have been transgressed, including those for climate, biodiversity and the flows of nitrogen and phosphorus. The transgression of a boundary is not itself a health outcome, but it changes the conditions that determine health, and its effects reach people through several routes.

The routes include the spread of infectious disease as the ranges of vectors shift, the malnutrition that results from disrupted food systems and the illness caused by pollution and heat. Each route has its own time scale and its own population at risk, and the people who suffer most are often those least responsible for the changes. The health burden is therefore distributed unevenly, and this unevenness is part of the problem.

Whitmee et al. (2015) argued that health should be a central measure of the success of responses to environmental change, since it captures the outcomes that people experience. The argument requires that health outcomes be measured with indicators that link them to the environment, and that the responses be evaluated on those outcomes. Cumming (2014) argues that the change in each outcome should be reported with its interval, since the estimates of environmental effects on health are often uncertain.
