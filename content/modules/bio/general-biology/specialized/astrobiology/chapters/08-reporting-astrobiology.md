An astrobiology result is among the most scrutinised in science, and its report must allow the claim to be judged on its evidence. The definition of life used, and the criteria by which the evidence was assessed, should be stated first, since the same observation can count as evidence under one definition and not under another. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and for an astrobiology result those means include the instrument, its calibration and the data reduction.

The instrument should be described with its calibration and its detection limit, and the uncertainty of each measurement should be given. Remote observations depend on models, and the models must be reported with their assumptions. Des Marais et al. (2008) emphasised that a claim about life depends on the instruments and the interpretation together, and both should appear in the report.

The abiotic explanations that were considered should be listed, with the evidence by which each was excluded, since a biosignature is credible only when its alternatives have been examined. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result accepted before its alternatives are excluded may not stand, and the report should show the exclusion step by step. Cumming (2014) argues that the strength of the evidence should be stated with its uncertainty, rather than as a yes or no.

The contamination controls and the bioburden record should be reported, along with the data archived with identifiers. The report should distinguish a detection, which is an observation, from a candidate biosignature, which is an interpretation, and from a confirmed result, which requires independent confirmation. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended keeping the inputs, steps and outputs of an analysis together, so that the claim can be checked against its data.
