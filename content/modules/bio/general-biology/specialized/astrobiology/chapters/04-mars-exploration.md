Mars is the planet most studied for evidence of life beyond Earth. Spacecraft orbiters and landers have shown that liquid water once flowed on its surface, that lakes and perhaps seas existed billions of years ago, and that groundwater may persist in its subsurface. Des Marais et al. (2008) identified these findings as the basis for the search, since where water was present, conditions for life may have existed. The question of whether life did arise there remains open.

Sedimentary rocks are the most promising places to look for signs of life, because sediments can preserve the traces of organisms and the chemistry of the environment in which they formed. The landers and rovers examine these rocks for minerals, organic molecules and textures that could be biological. The interpretation of such evidence requires knowing the abiotic processes that produce similar features, and the instruments must be able to distinguish them.

Instruments on the surface analyse the chemistry and the isotopes of rocks and soils, and they have detected organic molecules in some samples. The origin of these molecules is uncertain, since they may be formed by non-biological chemistry or may have been delivered by meteorites, and their presence alone does not establish that life was present. Cumming (2014) argues that such detections should be reported with their uncertainty and the detection limit of the instrument.

Sample return missions would allow Martian material to be analysed in Earth laboratories with far more powerful instruments. Such samples must be contained, to prevent any possible Martian organisms from reaching Earth, and the samples must be protected from contamination by Earth life. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that is obtained with contaminated material may be mistaken for a finding, and planetary protection measures are designed to prevent such contamination in both directions.
