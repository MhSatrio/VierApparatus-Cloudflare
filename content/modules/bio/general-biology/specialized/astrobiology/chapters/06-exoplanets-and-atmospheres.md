Thousands of planets have been found around other stars, and some are of a size and at a distance from their star that could allow liquid water on their surface. The transit method detects a planet by the small dimming of its star as the planet passes in front of it, and the radial velocity method detects it by the wobble it induces in the star. Both reveal the size or the mass of the planet, and together they show its density and its likely composition.

When a planet with an atmosphere transits its star, some starlight passes through the atmosphere, and the gases absorb characteristic wavelengths. The transit spectrum therefore reveals the composition of the atmosphere, and the detection of gases such as water, carbon dioxide and methane has been reported for some planets. The signal is faint, and it is often comparable to the noise of the instrument and the variability of the star.

Gases such as oxygen and methane may be biosignatures when they coexist, since their combination is hard to sustain without a biological source. The interpretation depends on models of the atmosphere, its chemistry and the sources of each gas, and a detection without such a model cannot be interpreted as biological. Cumming (2014) argues that the significance of each detection should be stated, and the model assumed should be given with it.

The habitable zone is the range of distances from a star in which liquid water could exist on a planetary surface, and it depends on the star and on the atmosphere of the planet. A planet in this zone is a candidate for habitability, which is not the same as evidence of life. Ioannidis (2005) described how a candidate can be treated as a finding before its alternatives are excluded. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the data, the reduction and the model used, so that a detection can be reanalysed when the data improve.
