Planetary protection prevents two kinds of contamination. Forward contamination is the introduction of Earth organisms into an environment on another world, where they could multiply and make it impossible to tell whether any life found there was native or terrestrial. Backward contamination is the return to Earth of material from another world that might carry an organism hazardous to life here. Des Marais et al. (2008) identified both as constraints on the search for life, since a contamination of either kind would compromise the scientific result or the safety of the planet.

Forward contamination is controlled by limiting the bioburden of spacecraft, the number of viable microbes they carry, and by cleaning and sterilising the parts that contact the target environment. The bioburden must be measured and recorded, and the cleaning procedures must be validated, since a spacecraft that is found to carry unexpected microbes can cast doubt on any life detected near it.

Backward contamination is controlled by the containment of returned samples in facilities that prevent any material from escaping. The containment must be designed for the worst case, and the samples must be examined under conditions that protect both the samples from Earth contamination and Earth from the samples. The protocols for opening and analysing the samples must be set in advance and documented.

Missions are classified into categories according to the likelihood that they could contaminate a target, and the stringency of the protection depends on the category. The classification and the measures applied to each mission should be reported. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the measurements and procedures that bear on the integrity of a result, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that is compromised by contamination may be mistaken for a finding. Cumming (2014) argues that the bioburden should be reported with its uncertainty and its detection limit.
