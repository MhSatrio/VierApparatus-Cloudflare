Several moons of the outer solar system are covered by thick shells of ice, and beneath some of them spacecraft measurements suggest liquid water oceans that may be in contact with rock. The evidence includes the magnetic signatures of conducting water, the flexing of the surface by tides and, for some moons, plumes of water vapour that erupt through the ice. Des Marais et al. (2008) identified these worlds as important targets, since water and an energy source are among the conditions that life requires.

Energy is needed to sustain life, and in these oceans it could come from tidal heating, which warms the interior as the moon is flexed by its parent planet, or from chemical reactions at the rocky seafloor, where minerals and water react to release hydrogen. The chemistry of the ocean and its interaction with the rock would determine how much energy is available, and these are not yet measured directly.

Ice plumes offer a way to sample the ocean without drilling through the ice, since material from the ocean is carried upward and can be analysed by a spacecraft flying through the plume. The organic molecules in such plumes would be of interest, though their detection would not by itself show that they are biological, and the abiotic sources of organic matter must be considered.

A habitable ocean is a candidate for life, not evidence of it. The presence of water and energy shows that life could exist, but it does not show that it does. Ioannidis (2005) described how a candidate can be treated as a finding before it has been tested, and the distinction between habitability and life must be kept clear. Cumming (2014) argues that the uncertainty in the estimated properties of the oceans should be stated, since many of the inferred values depend on models.
