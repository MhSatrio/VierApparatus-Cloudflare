A search for life requires a definition of what is being sought, and the definition is harder to state than it appears. The properties usually associated with life include metabolism, the exchange of energy and matter with the environment, self-maintenance against decay, reproduction, heredity and evolution. Each of these is shared by some systems that are not living, such as a flame that consumes fuel, a crystal that grows and reproduces its structure, or a chemical cycle that copies itself. No single property is therefore sufficient.

Des Marais et al. (2008) argued that a useful approach is to specify a set of features that life must show in combination, and to treat the observations that match them as evidence. This avoids reliance on any one property, but it requires that the features be stated precisely enough to be tested, and that their combination be shown to be distinctive.

A narrow definition ties life to the chemistry familiar on Earth, such as carbon-based molecules and liquid water. This definition makes the search tractable, since it identifies the places and the signals to look for, but it may exclude forms of life that use other solvents or other chemistry. A broader definition is more inclusive, but it is harder to apply and may admit non-living processes.

The definition chosen determines which observations count as evidence. A search that looks for carbon-based molecules will report the detection of such molecules as evidence, and a search that looks for a particular metabolism will look for its products. The definition should therefore be stated before the observations are made, and the evidence should be weighed against the definition rather than fitted to it. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the definition and the criteria used to assess the evidence.
