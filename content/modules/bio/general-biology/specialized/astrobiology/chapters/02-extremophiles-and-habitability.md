Extremophiles are organisms that thrive in conditions that would be lethal to most life. Thermophiles grow at temperatures above boiling in hot springs and deep-sea vents, halophiles require high salt, acidophiles live in acid waters, and psychrophiles grow in permanent cold. Radioresistant organisms survive intense radiation, and piezophiles live under the pressure of the deep ocean. Des Marais et al. (2008) argued that these organisms widen the range of conditions considered habitable, since they show that life can adapt to environments once thought hostile.

Some extremophiles do not depend on sunlight at all. Chemosynthetic organisms obtain their energy from the oxidation of inorganic compounds such as hydrogen sulphide or hydrogen, and they form the base of communities around hydrothermal vents. These metabolisms show that life can be sustained by the chemical energy of rock and water, which is important for the search for life on worlds without sunlight.

The subsurface biosphere of rock and deep sediment contains microbes that may live for long periods on little energy, and their existence extends the habitable volume of Earth far below the surface. Similar environments may exist on other worlds, and the study of these microbes is used to estimate how life might persist in the subsurface of other planets.

Habitability is judged from the tolerance limits of organisms, but the conditions that allow survival are not the same as those that allow growth. A cell may survive in a condition for years without dividing, and the survival does not establish that life can sustain itself there. The distinction must be made clear, since a claim of habitability based on survival alone may overstate the case. Cumming (2014) argues that the tolerance limits should be reported with their intervals and the duration of exposure, since the limit depends on how long the organism is exposed.
