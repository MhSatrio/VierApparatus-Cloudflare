Astrobiology asks whether life exists or has existed beyond Earth, and how it began and spread. The question is one of the oldest in science, and it has become testable as spacecraft explore the planets and as telescopes detect planets around other stars. Des Marais et al. (2008) set out the research agenda of the NASA Astrobiology Roadmap, in which the origin, evolution and future of life are studied together, and the field draws on biology, chemistry, geology and planetary science.

The first difficulty is the definition of life. A search for life requires a clear statement of what is sought, and the definitions in use are based on the properties of life on Earth. A definition that is too narrow may miss life that differs from ours, and one that is too broad may admit non-living processes. The definition chosen governs the signals that a search will look for.

Life on Earth tolerates conditions that were once thought to preclude it. The extremophiles that live in hot springs, in salt lakes, in deep rock and in the cold of polar ice show that life can persist across a wide range of temperature, pressure, salinity and radiation. This tolerance widens the places where life could exist, and the chapters examine the signals by which it might be detected.

Biosignatures are features that might indicate life, such as particular molecules, isotopic patterns or the structures of rock. Each can be produced by non-biological processes, and a biosignature must be shown to be unlikely to arise without life before it is claimed as evidence. Ioannidis (2005) described how a striking finding can be accepted before its alternatives are excluded, and Cumming (2014) argues that the strength of evidence should be stated with its uncertainty. Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the data and the methods, since a claim about life beyond Earth will be scrutinised intensely.
