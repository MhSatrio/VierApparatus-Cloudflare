Evolutionary developmental biology, often called evo-devo, asks how changes in the process of development generate the variation on which natural selection acts. Carroll (2008) argued that morphological evolution is largely a matter of changes in the genes that control development, rather than changes in the protein-coding genes that build the parts directly. The field therefore links two subjects that were once separate, the study of how an organism forms and the study of how lineages change over time.

Development is controlled by networks of regulatory genes that switch other genes on and off in particular places and at particular times. Changes in the regulatory sequences of these genes can alter where a structure forms, how large it grows or whether it forms at all. Hoekstra & Coyne (2007) described how such regulatory changes often underlie adaptive differences, since they can alter one structure without disrupting the many other functions of the same gene. The chapters on gene regulatory networks and on the Hox genes examine these mechanisms in detail.

Not every developmental change is possible, because development is constrained by the way structures are built and by the pleiotropic effects of genes that act in several places. The concept of developmental constraint explains why certain forms recur across lineages and why some variants never appear. Carroll (2008) suggested that these constraints channel evolution toward a limited set of outcomes, and the comparative chapters explore the evidence for this view.

The methods of the field combine comparative genomics, experimental manipulation of gene expression and the study of natural populations. Claims about the developmental basis of an evolutionary change must be tested by showing that the same change can be reproduced or reversed, and by documenting the population differences that the mechanism is thought to explain. The final chapter describes the study designs that make such claims defensible.
