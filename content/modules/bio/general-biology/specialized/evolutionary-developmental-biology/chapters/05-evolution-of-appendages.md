Appendages such as limbs, fins and wings are formed by a shared genetic program in many animals. Regulatory genes establish the axes of the appendage and direct the growth of its tissues, and the same families of genes are involved across vertebrates and some invertebrates. Carroll (2008) argued that this shared program explains why such diverse structures can be built from common components, and it provides the starting point for explaining how they diverged.

Differences in the shape of appendages often arise from changes in how long a structure grows or how many repeated elements it forms. The number of digits, the length of a bone and the pattern of fin rays can each be altered by changing the expression of regulatory genes in the growing tissue. Hoekstra & Coyne (2007) noted that such changes can be adaptive, since a shift in shape may improve locomotion or feeding without requiring a different set of genes.

Limbs have been lost in several lineages, and the loss is often associated with changes in the expression of genes that promote limb growth. In some cases the loss results from the repression of an early step in limb formation, so that the structure never begins to develop. Carroll (2008) described how such losses can be as informative as gains, because they reveal which steps in development are essential for the structure to form.

Novel appendages such as insect wings are harder to explain, because they lack a simple homologue in all lineages. The evidence suggests that they arose by co-opting existing developmental programs for new purposes. Peng (2011) argued that claims about the origin of a structure should be supported by reproducible comparisons, and the evidence for wing origin must be evaluated with that standard in mind.
