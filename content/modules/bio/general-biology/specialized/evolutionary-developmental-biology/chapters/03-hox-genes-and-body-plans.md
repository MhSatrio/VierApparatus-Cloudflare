The Hox genes are a family of regulatory genes that specify identity along the head-to-tail axis of animals. In many species they are arranged in clusters on the chromosome, and their order along the cluster matches the order in which they are expressed along the body. This correspondence, called colinearity, means that the genetic organisation of the cluster is mirrored by the organisation of the body. Carroll (2008) described the Hox system as one of the clearest examples of how regulatory genes organise morphology.

Each Hox gene specifies the identity of the segments in which it is active. Changing which Hox genes are expressed in a segment alters what that segment becomes, so that a segment that would form a leg may instead form a wing or a different appendage. Hoekstra & Coyne (2007) noted that such changes in identity can be produced by changes in regulatory sequence, which alter where and when a Hox gene is expressed, rather than by changes to the gene's protein.

Hox clusters have been duplicated and lost during the evolution of animal lineages. Duplication provides additional genes that can take on new roles, and loss can simplify the body plan. The number of clusters varies among groups, and the comparison of these arrangements allows the history of duplication to be reconstructed. Such comparisons should be treated with care, since the inference of ancestral states depends on the accuracy of the gene trees used.

Experiments that alter Hox expression in model organisms have shown that the body plan can be changed in dramatic ways, and these experiments support the view that the regulatory system is a major source of morphological variation. Ioannidis (2005) described how a single striking result can overstate a general conclusion when the experimental conditions are narrow, so the transformations should be interpreted together with the range of species in which they have been observed.
