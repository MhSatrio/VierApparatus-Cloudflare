Evo-devo uses experimental and comparative methods to test whether a developmental mechanism explains an evolutionary change. Model organisms with well-studied developmental genetics are used for experiments, and the findings are then extended to species that are less tractable in the laboratory. Carroll (2008) described how the choice of organisms shapes the questions that can be answered, and the comparative studies must be designed to make the most of the diversity available.

Comparative analysis of gene expression across species asks whether a regulatory gene is active in the same tissue in each. Differences in expression are then mapped onto a phylogeny to infer where changes occurred. Because species share ancestry, the comparison must account for relatedness, since a shared feature may reflect common descent rather than independent evolution. Peng (2011) argued that such analyses are reproducible only when their data and steps are fully recorded.

A correlation between a regulatory difference and a morphological difference does not show that the first caused the second. Manipulation of the regulatory element, through gene knockdown or the introduction of a transgene, tests whether the change is sufficient to produce the morphology. Hoekstra & Coyne (2007) described how such tests are the strongest evidence for a causal developmental mechanism, and they are an essential complement to comparative work.

Morphology must be measured with the same care as the genetics. Imaging and quantitative shape analysis allow developmental differences to be expressed as numbers, and these numbers should be reported with their intervals. Cumming (2014) argues that the variation within groups should be shown alongside the difference between them. Ioannidis (2005) described how small or unrepresentative samples can produce striking results that do not replicate, and the number of individuals and species studied should be stated clearly.
