A gene regulatory network is a set of genes whose products control one another's expression. Transcription factors bind to short sequences in regulatory DNA, called enhancers, and either activate or repress the genes nearby. The pattern of activity across a developing embryo is the output of many such interactions, and it determines which cells adopt which fate. Carroll (2008) described these networks as the decision-making machinery of development, and they are the level at which evolutionary changes in form are most often explained.

Enhancers are modular. A single enhancer may integrate the inputs of several transcription factors, so that it is active only where all the required signals are present. This arrangement allows a gene to be expressed in one tissue without affecting its expression elsewhere. Hoekstra & Coyne (2007) noted that such modularity makes regulatory changes less disruptive than changes to a protein, because the same gene can be altered in one place while its other functions are preserved.

Networks contain recurring motifs, such as feedback loops and cascades, that stabilise developmental decisions and make them robust to small fluctuations. A feedback loop can lock a cell into a fate once a threshold is passed, and a cascade can translate a single signal into a complex pattern. Peng (2011) argued that the structure of a computational or biological system should be described precisely if its behaviour is to be understood, and the same principle applies to the mapping of these motifs.

Changes in enhancer sequence are a major source of evolutionary change because they can alter the expression of a gene in a particular structure without affecting its other roles. The consequences can be large, since a shift in the timing or location of expression can change the shape or number of a structure. The next chapter examines how the Hox genes, which specify regional identity along the body, are organised in this way.
