For much of the twentieth century, evolutionary theory treated development as a black box. Population genetics explained how allele frequencies change under selection, but it said little about how an embryo becomes an adult with a particular form. Early comparative embryologists had recognised that vertebrate embryos share patterns, and these observations were largely set aside as the synthesis of genetics and selection took shape. Carroll (2008) described this separation as a gap that the modern field was created to close.

The molecular revolution changed the terms of the debate. Genes that control the formation of the eye, the limbs and the nervous system were found to be shared across animals as distant as flies and mice, which suggested that the toolkit of development is ancient and conserved. Hoekstra & Coyne (2007) argued that the genetics of adaptation can be studied directly, because the genes that vary among populations are often the same genes that control development.

The concept of the developmental genotype-phenotype map describes how genetic variation is converted into variation in form. The map is not linear, because a single gene may affect many structures and many genes may contribute to one. Changes in the map alter which variants are produced and which are available to selection, and this is why developmental processes shape the direction of evolution.

This view does not replace natural selection with development. Selection still determines which variants persist, but the range of variants on which it acts is set by development. Carroll (2008) emphasised that understanding evolution requires knowing both the process that generates variation and the process that sorts it, and the remaining chapters examine each in turn.
