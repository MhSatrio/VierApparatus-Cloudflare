Developmental constraint is the limitation that the process of development places on the variation it can produce. A gene that affects several structures, a property called pleiotropy, means that a change selected for in one structure may alter the others. The constraint is not absolute, since many genes have separable effects, but it biases the direction of change. Hoekstra & Coyne (2007) noted that pleiotropy is one reason why some adaptive changes are rare, since they require the simultaneous adjustment of several traits.

Development is also modular. Some parts of the embryo form from tissues that can be altered independently of others, and this modularity allows a trait to evolve without disrupting the rest of the body. Modules are the units on which evolution can act most freely, and many of the most striking morphological changes involve modules that were already separable. Carroll (2008) argued that the modular organisation of development is one of the main reasons that body plans can be varied without collapse.

Constraint can make certain forms more likely to appear than others. Lineages often produce the same structures repeatedly, because the developmental system tends to generate a limited set of outcomes from the variation available. This bias is distinct from the absence of variation, since a form that is strongly favoured may still be reached by different routes. The difference between a constraint that prevents a form and a bias that favours one is an important distinction in the field.

The evidence for constraint comes from comparative studies that show recurring forms across unrelated lineages, and from experiments that show which changes are easy to produce and which are hard. Such evidence must be interpreted carefully, since a recurring form may reflect selection as much as development. Cumming (2014) argues that the strength of any claim about constraint should be reported with its uncertainty, because the comparisons available are often few.
