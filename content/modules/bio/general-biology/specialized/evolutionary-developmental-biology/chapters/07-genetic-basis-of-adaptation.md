The genetic basis of adaptation is established by linking the variation in a trait to the variation in the genome. Crosses between divergent populations and association mapping across many individuals identify regions of the genome that co-vary with the trait. Hoekstra & Coyne (2007) argued that such mapping can reveal the loci responsible for adaptive differences, and that these loci are often more accessible to study than the general mechanisms of development.

The regions identified are not always the genes that cause the trait. Linked variation can be carried along with the causal locus, so a region may be associated with a trait without contributing to it. Ioannidis (2005) described how associations found in one study often fail to replicate, and the candidate loci must therefore be tested through independent populations and experiments before a causal role is claimed.

Changes that affect the regulatory sequence of a gene are often favoured over changes that alter its protein, because regulatory changes can modify one structure while leaving other functions intact. Carroll (2008) noted that this reduces the cost of adaptation, since a protein change may have consequences across the whole organism. The distinction is central to the field, and it explains why adaptive changes often involve enhancers rather than coding sequences.

Parallel evolution, in which the same trait arises independently in different populations through the same genes, is strong evidence for a shared developmental basis. Pigmentation and armour plates in fish are examples in which the same loci recur. Hoekstra & Coyne (2007) emphasised that such recurrence makes the genetic basis of adaptation more predictable, although the recurrence must be confirmed in enough independent populations to be convincing.
