Developmental plasticity is the ability of one genotype to produce different phenotypes under different environmental conditions. A temperature, a diet or the presence of a predator can change the regulatory state of a developing organism, and the result is a different form. Carroll (2008) noted that plasticity shows that the developmental system is not a fixed blueprint, and that the relation between genes and form depends on the environment in which development occurs.

Some plasticity is discrete, producing alternative morphs from one genome. Examples include horned and hornless forms in some insects, and seasonal changes in the colour of certain animals, in which a cue such as day length triggers a different developmental programme. Hoekstra & Coyne (2007) described how these polyphenisms can be adaptive, since each morph is suited to a different condition, and they illustrate how a single genome can contain alternative developmental routes.

Plasticity can precede evolutionary change. A trait that first appears in response to the environment may later become fixed in the population by selection on the genes that regulate it, a process called genetic assimilation. This sequence suggests that plasticity can make a new form available for selection, and that the environment may initiate changes that genetics later stabilises. The evidence for this sequence requires showing that the genetic change followed the environmental response.

Separating environmental effects from genetic effects requires experiments in which individuals of known genotype are raised in controlled conditions. Cumming (2014) argues that the magnitude of each effect should be reported with its interval, since the difference between two treatments is often smaller than the variation within them. Plasticity has costs and limits, and the design of any study must account for both the range of responses and the uncertainty in their size.
