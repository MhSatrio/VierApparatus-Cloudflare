Plants defend themselves with an immune system that recognises microbes in two layers. The first layer detects molecules that are shared by many microbes, such as the flagellin of bacterial flagella and fragments of fungal cell walls, through receptors on the cell surface. Jones and Dangl (2006) described this pattern-triggered immunity as the first line of defence, which slows the growth of many potential invaders, and it responds to beneficial as well as harmful microbes.

The second layer detects the effectors that pathogens deliver into the plant cell to suppress the first layer. Intracellular receptors recognise these effectors, either directly or through the damage they cause, and they trigger a stronger response. This effector-triggered immunity often includes a hypersensitive response, in which the infected cells die to contain the pathogen, and the response is strong enough to stop the infection in many cases.

Pathogens evolve effectors that suppress the first layer, and plants evolve receptors that recognise the effectors, in a continuing contest. The contest produces a pattern of rapid evolution in the genes involved, and it explains why resistance in a crop may be overcome by a new pathogen strain. The durability of resistance depends on the diversity of the receptors and the rate at which the pathogen evolves.

Plants must distinguish beneficial microbes from pathogens, since many beneficial microbes trigger the same first-layer responses as pathogens. The discrimination depends on the timing and the strength of the response, on the molecules that are presented and on the context of the interaction. The evidence for such discrimination requires measuring the response to each microbe under controlled conditions. Cumming (2014) argues that the strength of the response should be reported with its interval across plants, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a response measured in few plants may not reflect the population.
