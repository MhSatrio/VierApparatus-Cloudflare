The symbiosis between legumes and rhizobia is one of the most important biological nitrogen sources in agriculture. The plant releases flavonoids from its roots, and these signals induce the bacteria to produce Nod factors, molecules that the plant recognises and that trigger the formation of a nodule. Berendsen et al. (2012) described this exchange as a clear example of how the plant shapes its microbiome, and the nodule is the organ in which the partnership takes place.

Within the nodule the bacteria are housed in cells of the plant and are supplied with carbon compounds, which the plant provides from photosynthesis. The bacteria carry the nitrogenase enzyme, which reduces atmospheric nitrogen to ammonia, and the enzyme is extremely sensitive to oxygen. The nodule maintains a low concentration of free oxygen through a protein that binds it, and this regulation allows the enzyme to work without being destroyed.

The fixed nitrogen passes from the bacteria to the plant in the form of compounds such as ureides or amides, and the plant uses it for growth. In return, the plant supplies the carbon that fuels the bacterial metabolism, and the exchange is governed by the plant's control of resources. Plants can reduce nodulation when nitrogen is abundant in the soil, which illustrates the regulation of the partnership.

The amount of nitrogen fixed is measured by isotopic methods, which compare the nitrogen isotope composition of the legume with that of a non-fixing reference plant. The difference shows how much nitrogen came from the atmosphere, and the measurement must separate fixed nitrogen from soil nitrogen. Cumming (2014) argues that the proportion fixed should be reported with its interval across plants, and Ioannidis (2005) described how the reference plant and the soil conditions can change the estimate, so both should be reported.
