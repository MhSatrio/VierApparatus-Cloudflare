Biocontrol organisms are beneficial microbes applied to crops to suppress pests and diseases, and they offer an alternative to chemical pesticides. They act by several mechanisms. Some compete with pathogens for space and nutrients on the root or leaf, some produce antibiotic compounds that inhibit pathogens, some parasitise pathogens directly, and some induce the plant's own defences. Berendsen et al. (2012) described how the root microbiome already performs such functions, and biocontrol seeks to strengthen them.

Each mechanism must be shown in the system of use. A strain that inhibits a pathogen in a dish may not do so on the root, and the induction of plant defence must be shown in the plant rather than inferred from the microbe. The attribution of an effect to a mechanism requires experiments that remove or alter the proposed mechanism and show that the effect changes accordingly.

Inoculants are formulated and delivered in carriers that protect the microbes and help them to survive in the soil. The survival of the introduced strain depends on the soil, the carrier and the native community, which may compete with or prey upon it. A strain that disappears within weeks cannot provide a lasting benefit, and its persistence must be measured.

Performance varies across soils and seasons, and an inoculant that works in one field may not work in another. The competition between the introduced strain and the native community determines whether the strain establishes, and the native community may already suppress the pathogen. Ioannidis (2005) described how a benefit that depends on conditions may not be general, and field trials across sites and seasons are needed. Cumming (2014) argues that the effect should be reported with its interval across trials, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the strain, the carrier, the soil and the season.
