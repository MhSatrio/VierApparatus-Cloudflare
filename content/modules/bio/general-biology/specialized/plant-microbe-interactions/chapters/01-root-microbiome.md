The roots of a plant are surrounded by a zone of soil, the rhizosphere, that is enriched in microbes by the sugars, amino acids and other compounds that roots release. Berendsen et al. (2012) described how the plant uses this release to attract and select a community, and the rhizosphere contains many more microbes than the bulk soil at a distance from the roots. The composition of this community is not random, since the plant favours some groups and discourages others.

Within the root tissues, the endosphere contains a smaller and more selected community. The microbes that enter the root must pass the barriers of the root surface and the immune responses of the plant, and only some succeed. The endosphere is therefore a more specific subset of the rhizosphere, and its composition reflects the plant's recognition of its microbes.

The community is shaped by the genotype of the plant, which determines the composition of its exudates and the strength of its immune responses. Different cultivars of the same crop can host different root communities, and this difference may be heritable. The soil type, the management and the history of the field also shape the pool from which the plant selects, so the same plant genotype may host different communities in different fields.

The communities are sampled by collecting soil from the rhizosphere, which is the soil that adheres to the roots, and by extracting DNA from the root tissues for the endosphere. The two samples differ in their composition, and the method of separation must be reported, since the sampling determines the zone that is described. Ioannidis (2005) described how a community characterised from a single sample may not represent the plant, and Cumming (2014) argues that the variation between plants should be reported with its interval.
