A microbe that performs well in the laboratory must be shown to perform in the field, where the benefit is weighed against the variation of soil, weather and management. The field trial is therefore the decisive test. It should use randomised plots, with the microbe and a control applied in each block, and it should be replicated across sites and seasons, since the performance varies with each.

The traits measured must include yield and the traits that the microbe is expected to change, such as nutrient uptake, disease incidence or stress tolerance. A microbe that suppresses a disease should be assessed by the disease, not only by the yield, since the yield may be affected by other factors. The traits should be chosen before the trial, so that the outcomes are not selected after the results are known.

The microbial effect must be separated from the effects of soil, weather and management. Soil fertility, rainfall and fertiliser all influence the crop, and a comparison in one season may reflect the weather rather than the microbe. The effect of the microbe is estimated as the difference from the control within each block and season, and the interaction with the environment is examined.

The genotype of the crop can change the benefit of the microbe, so that an inoculant helps one cultivar and does nothing for another. This genotype by microbe interaction must be tested in the design, since a trial with a single cultivar may overstate or understate the benefit. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that depends on an unrecognised interaction may not be reproduced, and Cumming (2014) argues that the effect should be reported with its interval for each cultivar and environment.
