Plants depend on microbes for much of their nutrition. Soil organic matter contains nitrogen and phosphorus in forms that the plant cannot take up, and microbes convert them into available forms through mineralisation. The microbes of the rhizosphere therefore sustain the supply of nutrients that the root absorbs, and Berendsen et al. (2012) described this as one of the main benefits that the rhizosphere community confers on the plant.

Phosphorus is often bound to soil minerals in forms that are unavailable, and some microbes solubilise it by producing acids and chelating compounds that release it from the minerals. Mycorrhizal fungi extend the root system through a network of hyphae that explores a much larger volume of soil, and they increase the absorption of phosphorus and water in exchange for carbon from the plant. The symbiosis is among the oldest and most widespread partnerships in terrestrial ecosystems.

Iron is another limiting nutrient, and some microbes produce siderophores, molecules that bind iron tightly and make it available to the plant. The siderophores can also be taken up by the plant, which provides a route for iron supply. The effectiveness of this route depends on the soil and on the plant species, and the benefit to the plant must be measured.

Field experiments must separate the microbial contribution from the supply of fertiliser, since a crop that receives fertiliser may show little response to the microbes. The comparison requires treatments with and without the microbes at each level of fertiliser, and the effect of the microbes should be estimated across these levels. Ioannidis (2005) described how a benefit that depends on conditions may not be general, and Cumming (2014) argues that the effect should be reported with its interval across sites and fertiliser levels.
