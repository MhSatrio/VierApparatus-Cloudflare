A plant-microbe study can be attributed to the microbe only if its design and its materials are fully described. The plant species and its genotype should be given, since the response to a microbe depends on the plant. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and for a plant-microbe study those means include the plant material, the strains and the procedure. Berendsen et al. (2012) showed that the soil and the plant together shape the microbial community, and both must be described.

The soil should be characterised by its texture, its organic matter, its nutrient status and its history. The microbial strains should be identified by their genomes, with their origin and their delivery, and the inoculation procedure should be described with the dose and the timing. Controls must be included, and the control should receive the same procedure without the microbe, since handling alone can change the plant.

The sampling of the rhizosphere or the endosphere, and the sequencing of the community, should be reported where they were used. The measured traits should be given with their methods, and the field sites, seasons and management should be stated. Cumming (2014) argues that the effects should be reported with their intervals, and the number of plants and plots should be given.

The statistical design should be stated, and the data and the strain information should be deposited with identifiers. Ioannidis (2005) described how an effect that is reported without its design may be overstated, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended keeping the inputs, steps and outputs together, which allows a plant-microbe result to be reproduced in another field.
