Plant pathogens deliver effector proteins into the host to overcome its defences. Some effectors act outside the cell, in the spaces between cells, while others enter the plant cell and act on its machinery. Jones and Dangl (2006) described how the effectors interfere with the receptors of the first layer of immunity and with the signalling that leads to defence, so that the pathogen can colonise the plant without triggering a full response.

Plants have evolved resistance proteins that recognise particular effectors, either directly or through the damage that the effector causes to a host protein. Once an effector is recognised, the resistance protein triggers a strong defence, often including the death of the infected cells. This effector-triggered immunity is the basis of many resistance genes used in crops, and it is effective until the pathogen changes the effector.

The pathogen can evolve by losing or altering the recognised effector, and a resistance gene can then be overcome within a few seasons of its deployment. The rate of this evolution depends on the genetic variation of the pathogen and on the strength of the selection that the resistance imposes. The durability of resistance is therefore a property of the pathogen population as well as of the plant.

Effectors are identified by genomic analysis of the pathogen, which reveals the secreted proteins, and their functions are tested by expressing them in plants and measuring the effect on immunity. Ioannidis (2005) described how a candidate effector identified from sequence may not have the function attributed to it, and Cumming (2014) argues that the effect of an effector on immunity should be reported with its interval across plants.
