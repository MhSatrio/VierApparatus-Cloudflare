Plants do not grow alone. Their roots, leaves and seeds carry dense communities of microorganisms, and these communities influence the growth, the nutrition and the health of the plant. Berendsen et al. (2012) described the rhizosphere, the soil zone around the roots, as a site where the plant selects a community that can protect it from disease and help it acquire nutrients. The interaction is a partnership in which the plant shapes the microbes and the microbes shape the plant.

The topic begins with the root microbiome and the mechanisms by which the plant assembles it. The chapters that follow examine the plant immune system, which must distinguish beneficial and harmful microbes, the nutrient supply provided by microbes, the nitrogen-fixing symbioses of legumes, the effectors that pathogens use to overcome immunity, and the biocontrol organisms applied to crops. Jones and Dangl (2006) described the immune system as a layered defence, and this layering explains much of the specificity of plant-microbe interactions.

Many laboratory effects of microbes on plants do not reach the field, because the field environment contains other microbes, variable weather and different soils. A microbe that protects a seedling in a sterile pot may fail in an open field, and a performance claim must be tested under the conditions in which the crop grows. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result from a controlled setting may not transfer, and the field trial is the necessary test.

Cumming (2014) argues that the effects of microbes on plant performance should be reported with their intervals across plants and sites. Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the plant genotype, the microbial strains and the conditions so that the interactions can be reproduced.
