Behaviour is the output by which the nervous system is observed most directly. Experiments measure how animals and people learn, remember, decide and move, and the designs must separate the effect of the intervention from the many other influences on behaviour. A behavioural experiment needs a control group, randomised allocation of the subjects and blinding of the observer, since each protects against a conclusion that reflects the expectations of the experimenter or an accident of allocation.

The measures of learning, memory and decision making are often indirect, and their reliability must be established. A test that gives different results on repeated occasions cannot distinguish a change in the subject from the noise of the measure. Button et al. (2013) noted that the small samples common in behavioural neuroscience make it hard to detect effects reliably, and the reliability of each measure should be reported.

Computational models of behaviour predict choices across conditions from a few parameters, and they can distinguish between explanations that the raw behaviour cannot. The models must be fitted and tested on separate data, since a model that fits the data it was built from will always look good. Ioannidis (2005) described how such flexibility can produce results that do not hold, and the model should be pre-specified where possible.

Individuals and laboratories differ in ways that affect behaviour. The strain of an animal, its age, its housing and the sex of the experimenter can all change the results, and an effect found in one laboratory may not appear in another. The variation must be recorded and, where possible, included in the analysis. Cumming (2014) argues that the effect should be reported with its interval across individuals and laboratories, since a single estimate conceals this variation.
