Neurodegenerative diseases involve the progressive loss of neurons and their connections, and they include Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease, which together affect a large number of people. The diseases are characterised by the accumulation of misfolded proteins in affected tissue, such as amyloid and tau in Alzheimer's and alpha-synuclein in Parkinson's, and by the death of specific populations of neurons. The causal relation between the aggregates and the death of the cells remains debated, and this matters for the design of therapies.

The mechanisms proposed for cell death include mitochondrial dysfunction, in which the energy supply of the cell fails, oxidative stress, in which damaging reactive molecules accumulate, and inflammation, in which the immune cells of the brain contribute to injury. Each mechanism has supporting evidence, but the evidence is often correlational, and the order of events in human disease is difficult to establish. Ioannidis (2005) described how a mechanism that is supported by a few striking studies may not hold when tested more broadly.

Genetic risk factors have been identified by association studies, and some rare mutations cause inherited forms of the disease. The common risk variants have small effects, and their relation to the biology of the disease is not always clear. The genetic evidence is strongest when it is replicated and when the variant can be linked to a mechanism, and Cumming (2014) argues that the effect of each variant should be reported with its interval.

The development of therapies has been slow, and many treatments that worked in animal models failed in clinical trials. The reasons include the imperfect reproduction of the disease in animals, the late stage at which patients are treated, and the choice of endpoints. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the models, the endpoints and the stage of disease studied, so that the translation of a result from animals to people can be judged.
