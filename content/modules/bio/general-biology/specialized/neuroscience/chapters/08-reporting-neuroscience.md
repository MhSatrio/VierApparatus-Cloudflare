A neuroscience finding can be judged only if the reader knows the subjects, the design and the analysis that produced it. The species, the strain, the age, the sex and the housing of the animals should be given, since each can change the result, and the same information is needed for human participants. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and in neuroscience those means include the animals, the apparatus and the procedure.

The sample sizes should be reported at each level, with the number of animals, the number of cells or sessions and the criteria by which any were excluded. The randomisation of the allocation and the blinding of the observer should be stated, since these protect against bias. Button et al. (2013) showed that small samples and low power are common in the field, and the reported sample sizes allow readers to judge the power of the study.

The methods of recording or imaging should be given with their settings, the processing steps and the statistical analysis, including the correction for multiple comparisons. The effect sizes should be reported with their intervals, and the significance of a comparison should not be the only result given. Cumming (2014) argues that an interval shows the range of effects compatible with the data, which is more informative than a test alone.

The data and the analysis code should be deposited with identifiers. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended keeping the inputs, steps and outputs of an analysis together, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that cannot be examined in its raw form cannot be assessed for the flexibility in its analysis.
