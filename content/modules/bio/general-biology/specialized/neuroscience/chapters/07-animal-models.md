Animals are used in neuroscience because their nervous systems can be manipulated and observed in ways that are not possible in people. A model may carry a mutation that causes a human disease, may be given a toxin or an injury that mimics a feature of the disease, or may be studied in its normal state to reveal basic mechanisms. The choice of model follows from the question, and a model that answers one question may be useless for another.

No model reproduces a human disease completely. A mouse model of a neurodegenerative disease may show the protein aggregates of the disease but not its progressive loss of neurons, or the reverse, and the features that the model reproduces should be stated. The limits of the model determine what can be concluded from it, and a result in an animal is a hypothesis about the person until it has been tested in people.

Strain, age, sex and housing can all change the results of an animal study. Different mouse strains differ in their behaviour, their immune responses and their susceptibility to injury, and the environment in which the animals are kept affects their stress and their brain. These factors must be controlled, reported or included in the analysis, since a result obtained in one strain may not hold in another.

The number of animals used should be justified by a power analysis, both to avoid using more animals than needed and to ensure that the study can detect an effect of interest. Button et al. (2013) showed that many neuroscience studies are underpowered, and an underpowered study wastes animals as well as producing unreliable results. Ioannidis (2005) described how the use of small numbers and flexible analysis produces findings that do not replicate, and the sample size should be fixed before the study begins. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the strain, the age, the sex, the housing and the procedure for every animal.
