The neuron is a cell specialised for signalling. Its dendrites receive synaptic input from other neurons, its soma integrates the input over space and time, and its axon carries the output to other cells. Yuste (2015) described this organisation as the basis of the modern view of the nervous system, in which each neuron is a computational element whose output depends on the combined input it receives.

The action potential is a brief change in the voltage across the membrane, generated when voltage-gated sodium channels open and depolarise the membrane beyond a threshold. Potassium channels then open to restore the resting voltage, and the cycle makes the signal a discrete event. The sequence of channel openings is governed by the membrane voltage, and it can be described by equations that reproduce the observed shape of the impulse.

The action potential travels along the axon without loss of amplitude, because each segment of membrane regenerates the impulse in the next. The speed of propagation depends on the diameter of the axon and on myelination, which insulates the axon and allows the impulse to jump between gaps. The timing of arrival at the synapse is part of the information the neuron transmits.

At a chemical synapse the arrival of an action potential opens calcium channels, the calcium triggers the release of neurotransmitter, and the transmitter binds receptors on the target cell. Excitatory synapses depolarise the target and inhibitory synapses hyperpolarise it, and the sum of these inputs determines whether the target fires. Synapses also change their strength with activity, a property called plasticity, which is thought to underlie learning and memory. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the conditions and the analysis of electrophysiological and imaging data so that such findings can be repeated.
