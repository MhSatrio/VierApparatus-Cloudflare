A neural circuit is a set of neurons connected to one another so that their joint activity performs a function, such as the detection of a visual edge or the generation of a rhythm for breathing. Yuste (2015) argued that understanding the brain requires the study of such circuits, since the behaviour of a single neuron is shaped by the network it belongs to. The architecture of the connections determines how a signal is transformed as it passes through the circuit.

Feedforward circuits pass a signal in one direction through successive layers, and they can sharpen, delay or combine the signal at each stage. Recurrent circuits contain loops, in which neurons feed back onto one another, and they can hold activity over time, generate oscillations and amplify or suppress inputs. The same elements can be arranged in either way, and the function of a circuit follows from its wiring and the strengths of its connections.

Populations of neurons often synchronise their activity into oscillations, which coordinate the timing of signals across regions. The frequency of an oscillation and its phase relation with other oscillations are believed to carry information, and they are measured by recording the activity of many neurons or of the local field potential. The interpretation of an oscillation depends on the circuit that generates it, which must be identified separately.

A stimulus is often represented by the joint activity of many neurons rather than by a single cell, and this population code can carry more information than any neuron alone. The inference of connectivity from recorded activity is difficult, because correlated activity can arise from a common input as well as from a direct connection. Ioannidis (2005) described how a model fitted to limited data may appear to show a connection that is not there, and Cumming (2014) argues that the strength of each connection should be reported with its interval. The approaches that combine anatomy, stimulation and recording are needed to test a circuit.
