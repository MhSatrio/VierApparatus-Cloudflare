Electrophysiology records the electrical activity of neurons, and it is the most direct measure of their signalling. Whole-cell patch clamp places a fine pipette on a single cell and measures its membrane voltage or the currents through its channels, giving a precise picture of one neuron at the cost of recording from few cells at a time. Extracellular recording places an electrode near one or more neurons and detects the spikes they generate, which allows many neurons to be recorded together but gives less information about each one's internal state.

The local field potential is the slow electrical signal produced by the summed activity of many neurons and their synapses in a region. It reflects population activity and is used to study oscillations, and its interpretation depends on the geometry of the cells and the source of the currents. Spike sorting assigns the detected spikes to individual neurons on the basis of their shape, and the sorting is uncertain when neurons have similar spikes or when the recording is noisy.

Recordings are distorted by artefacts, such as the electrical noise of the equipment, the movement of the animal and the drift of the electrode. Filtering removes some of these, but filtering can also alter the shape of a signal, and the filter settings should be reported. The criteria used to accept or reject a cell or a recording must be stated, since the choice changes which neurons contribute to the result.

A recording samples only the neurons that the electrode can reach and that remain stable enough to record, and the sample may be biased towards the most active or the largest cells. Cumming (2014) argues that the number of cells, animals and sessions should be reported with the estimates, since the effective sample size is often the number of animals rather than of cells. Ioannidis (2005) described how a small number of animals can produce findings that do not replicate, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the recording settings and the sorting method.
