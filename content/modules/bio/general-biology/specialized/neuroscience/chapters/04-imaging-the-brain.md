Imaging makes the structure and the activity of the brain visible, and it allows the same tissue or the same person to be observed over time. Calcium imaging uses fluorescent indicators that brighten when a neuron's calcium concentration rises during activity, and it can follow hundreds of neurons at once in an animal. Two-photon microscopy excites the fluorescence only at the focus of a laser, which allows deep tissue to be imaged with less damage and less background than conventional microscopy. Each method reveals a different aspect of the brain.

Functional magnetic resonance imaging does not measure neurons directly. It infers activity from the changes in blood oxygenation that accompany the increased metabolism of active tissue, and the signal is delayed and blurred in time and space by the haemodynamic response. Its spatial resolution is coarse compared with microscopy, but it can be applied to humans, and it is the most widely used method for mapping the human brain.

Each method trades spatial resolution against temporal resolution and depth. Calcium imaging is slow relative to the spikes it reports, while electrophysiology is fast but records few cells. The choice of method determines which questions can be answered, and the limits of the method should be stated with the findings.

Imaging data are affected by motion of the animal or the person, by the drift of the instrument and by the physiological signals of breathing and the heart. These artefacts must be corrected or modelled, and the correction must be reported. A study that tests many brain regions or many time points makes many comparisons, and the correction for multiple comparisons is essential to avoid false positives. Button et al. (2013) showed that low power is common in neuroscience, which increases the risk of spurious findings in imaging as well. Cumming (2014) argues that the effect sizes should be reported with their intervals, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the acquisition, the correction and the statistical procedure.
