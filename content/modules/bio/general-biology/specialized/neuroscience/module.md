Neuroscience studies the nervous system, the organ that generates perception, movement, memory and thought. Its basic units are the neurons, cells that receive, integrate and transmit electrical and chemical signals, and the synapses through which they communicate. Yuste (2015) described how the view that the neuron is the fundamental unit has given way to an understanding that circuits of neurons, and their dynamics, are needed to explain brain function, and the field now spans scales from the molecule to the whole animal.

The topic begins with the neuron and the synapse. A neuron generates an action potential when its membrane is depolarised beyond a threshold, and the signal travels along its axon to the synapse, where neurotransmitter is released and binds receptors on the next cell. The properties of these elements determine how a signal is transmitted, and they are the subject of a large body of measurement and theory.

The chapters that follow examine the circuits formed by many neurons, the methods that record their activity, the study of behaviour and cognition, and the mechanisms of disease. The reliability of neuroscience is a central concern. Button et al. (2013) showed that many neuroscience studies have small samples and low power, which makes their positive findings less likely to be true, and this problem runs through the field.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the pressure to report significant findings from small studies produces results that do not replicate, and neuroscience has been affected by this pattern. Cumming (2014) argues that the effects should be reported with their intervals rather than as significant or not, and Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the methods and the data so that the findings can be reproduced.
