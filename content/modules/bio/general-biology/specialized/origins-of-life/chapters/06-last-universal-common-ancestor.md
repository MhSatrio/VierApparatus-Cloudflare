The last universal common ancestor is the most recent organism from which all living things descend. It is not the first life, which must have been simpler and older, but the point at which the lineages that survive to the present day diverged from one another. Its features can be inferred from the genes that are shared by all living organisms, since a gene present in all three domains of life was likely present in their common ancestor. Woese et al. (1990) used this principle to define the domains of life from the sequences of ribosomal RNA.

Woese and colleagues showed that ribosomal RNA sequences divide living organisms into three domains, Archaea, Bacteria and Eukarya, and that these domains are more distant from one another than the diversity within each. The result changed the classification of life, since it showed that the familiar division into prokaryotes and eukaryotes does not reflect the deepest relationships. The three domains share a set of genes that trace back to a common ancestor.

The reconstruction is complicated by horizontal gene transfer, in which genes move between lineages by mechanisms other than inheritance from parent to offspring. Such transfers blur the history of individual genes, and different genes may give different pictures of the ancestor. Lane and Martin (2010) argued that the energetic features of the ancestor can be inferred from its genome, and the reconstruction must account for transfer to be sound.

The last common ancestor of living organisms is not the same as the first living thing. Many lineages may have arisen and disappeared before the ancestor of the present day, and the reconstruction says little about the earlier forms. The ancestor's environment and biology can only be inferred partially, and claims about its habitat should be stated as inferences. Ioannidis (2005) described how a reconstruction can appear more certain than its evidence allows, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the genes, the alignments and the methods used in each reconstruction.
