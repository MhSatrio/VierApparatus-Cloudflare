The structure of nucleic acids, which Watson and Crick (1953) described for DNA, makes a molecule able to store information and to copy it. Each strand carries a sequence of bases, and its complementary strand pairs with it in a defined way, so that the sequence of one strand specifies the sequence of the other. A molecule that can template its own copy is a replicator, and the replication of a sequence is the basis of heredity. The same principle applies to RNA, which can fold into structures and carry a sequence at once.

The RNA world hypothesis proposes that RNA molecules once performed both functions, storing information in their sequence and catalysing reactions through their folded structure. Such molecules are called ribozymes, and the discovery that RNA can catalyse reactions, including the making of peptide bonds in the ribosome, supports the idea that RNA was once central to life. The hypothesis explains why RNA appears in so many essential roles in modern cells.

The difficulty is that replication in the present day depends on enzymes, which would not have existed before the RNA world. Replication without enzymes is slow and error-prone, and the copies are often incomplete. An error catastrophe may occur when errors accumulate faster than selection can remove them, so that the information in the sequence is lost. The solution to this problem is among the central unsolved questions of the field.

Simpler replicators and the coevolution of RNA with peptides have been proposed to address these difficulties. Experiments have shown that some short RNA sequences can be copied under suitable conditions, though the copying is far from the efficiency of modern enzymes. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result obtained with a favourable sequence may not generalise to the many sequences that a prebiotic system would have contained, and the experiments should be reported with their scope. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the sequences and the conditions of each replication experiment.
