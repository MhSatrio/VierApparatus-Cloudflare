Hypotheses about the origin of life are difficult to test because the event happened once, long ago, and left few traces. The usual strategy is to test the parts of a hypothesis separately, by experiments that reproduce a proposed step and by geological and chemical evidence that constrains the environment and the timing. A hypothesis is credible if its steps are chemically plausible, if its environment existed when life arose, and if it explains the properties that life shares.

Rival hypotheses are compared by the properties of life that each explains. A hypothesis that accounts for the origin of replication, of metabolism and of compartments together is preferred to one that accounts for only one of them, and the comparison should consider how much each hypothesis explains rather than how much detail it supplies. Lane and Martin (2010) used the energetics of the genome to compare the features of early life, and such constraints discriminate between hypotheses.

Geological evidence constrains the environments and the timing. The ages of ancient rocks, the chemistry of ancient oceans and the presence of hydrothermal systems at particular times narrow the possible settings of the origin. The evidence is incomplete and open to different interpretations, and the uncertainty of each constraint should be stated.

Experiments are the most direct tests, but they carry a danger. An experiment that is designed or adjusted until it succeeds may demonstrate that a reaction is possible under the chosen conditions without showing that it happened on the early Earth. Ioannidis (2005) described how the selection of conditions after the outcome is known inflates the apparent support for a hypothesis, and the conditions of the experiment should be fixed by the geological evidence rather than by the desired result. Cumming (2014) argues that the yield under each condition should be reported with its interval, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the conditions and their justification.
