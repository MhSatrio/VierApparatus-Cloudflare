An origins experiment can be judged only if its conditions and its analysis are known in full. The hypothesis and the step of the origin that the experiment addresses should be stated first, since an experiment that reproduces one step says nothing about the others. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and for an origins experiment those means include the reactants, the conditions and the analytical methods.

The conditions should be reported with the reasons they were chosen. Where a condition is chosen to match the geological evidence, the evidence should be cited, and where it is chosen for convenience, this should be stated, since the relevance of the result depends on the choice. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the inputs, steps and outputs of an analysis together, and the conditions are among the inputs of an experiment.

The products should be identified by analytical methods with their detection limits, and the yields should be reported with their uncertainties across replicate experiments. The controls that exclude contamination, such as blanks and reactions without the reactants, must be included, since contamination by organic matter is a serious risk in this field. Cumming (2014) argues that each yield should be given with its interval, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a result with no controls may reflect the background rather than the chemistry.

Where an inference about ancestral genes is made, the alignments, the methods and the assumptions should be reported, as Woese et al. (1990) showed for the reconstruction of the domains of life. The data, the sequences and the code should be deposited with identifiers, so that the experiments and the reconstructions can be checked and built upon.
