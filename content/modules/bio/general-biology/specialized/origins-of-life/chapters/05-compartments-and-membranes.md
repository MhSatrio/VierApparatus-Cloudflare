Living cells are enclosed by membranes that separate their chemistry from the environment, and the first cells must have had a similar enclosure. Fatty acids, which are simple amphiphilic molecules, can assemble spontaneously in water into vesicles, closed bilayers with an aqueous interior. Such vesicles form readily under prebiotic conditions and can be induced to grow by the addition of more fatty acid and to divide by mechanical forces. They therefore provide a plausible physical basis for the first compartments.

Vesicles can take up nutrients and retain molecules within their interior. Small molecules can cross a simple fatty acid membrane, which allows nutrients to enter, but it also allows the escape of the molecules the vesicle wishes to keep. Experiments have shown that RNA can be encapsulated within vesicles, which suggests that a compartment could have held a replicating system together, although the retention of RNA depends on the composition of the membrane.

The permeability of the membrane creates a problem for maintaining a gradient. A cell that uses a gradient of protons or of ions to drive its metabolism must keep the gradient across its membrane, and a membrane that leaks the ions dissipates it. The transition to membranes that are less permeable, and to proteins that transport specific molecules, was therefore an important step in the origin of cells. Lane and Martin (2010) discussed the energetic consequences of this transition.

Compartments also concentrate reactants and protect molecules from degradation, which makes reactions inside them more likely. The experiments that test whether a compartment can grow, divide and retain information are central to the field, and they show whether the physical basis of a cell could have existed. Cumming (2014) argues that the retention and the growth rates should be reported with their intervals, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the membrane composition and the conditions of each experiment.
