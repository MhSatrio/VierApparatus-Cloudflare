Prebiotic chemistry asks how the molecules of life could have been made before biology existed. Experiments since the 1950s have shown that electrical discharges through mixtures of simple gases and water can produce amino acids and other organic compounds, and the result showed that the building blocks of proteins are not difficult to form from simple precursors. These experiments are the foundation of the field, although the mixtures they used differ from the atmosphere now thought to have existed on the early Earth.

The formation of nucleotides and sugars is more difficult. Ribose, the sugar of RNA, is hard to make in water in the presence of other compounds, and the nucleobases and the sugar must be joined to form a nucleotide. The chemistry of these steps has been explored in detail, and proposed routes depend on specific conditions of temperature, concentration and catalysis that may not have been common on the early Earth.

Mineral surfaces and wet-dry cycles may have helped to concentrate and link molecules. Minerals can adsorb molecules and catalyse reactions, and repeated drying and wetting of a surface could drive the condensation of molecules into longer chains. Lane and Martin (2010) emphasised that the energy available to these reactions shaped what could form, and the energetic constraints are central to the plausibility of each route.

The energy sources that might have driven prebiotic chemistry include lightning, ultraviolet light and the heat of hydrothermal systems. Each provides a different spectrum and quantity of energy, and each favours different products. The composition of the early atmosphere and ocean determines which reactions occur, and this composition is uncertain, which makes it difficult to say which route was realistic. Ioannidis (2005) described how experiments performed under convenient conditions may not describe the conditions of the early Earth. Cumming (2014) argues that the yields should be reported with their uncertainty and the conditions of the experiment.
