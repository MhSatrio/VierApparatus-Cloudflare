The origin of life is a historical question, and it cannot be answered by watching the event happen, since it occurred once, billions of years ago, and left few direct traces. Any account of it must explain the properties that living systems share: they store information, they copy it, they transform matter and energy, they are enclosed in compartments, and they evolve. The question is how these properties came together in a system that did not previously exist.

The timing of life is constrained by the geological record. Evidence from ancient rocks suggests that life was present on Earth early in its history, and the isotopic and structural signs that have been interpreted as biological are dated to billions of years ago. The timing matters because it limits the time available for the chemistry of origin, and the interpretation of the old signs is itself debated.

The origin of the first cell must be distinguished from the origin of the genetic code. A cell requires a membrane, a metabolism and a system of heredity, and each may have arisen at a different time or in a different way. Lane and Martin (2010) argued that the energy available to a cell constrains its organisation, and this energetic view helps to separate the questions.

The strategy of the field is to test the component hypotheses separately, by experiment on the chemistry that might have produced each component and by geological study of the environments that might have hosted it. No single experiment can establish an origin, and the claims must be judged by how far they explain the properties that life shares. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the assumptions about the environment and the chemistry, since an origin hypothesis depends on them.
