Metabolism-first hypotheses propose that the chemistry of the cell began as a network of reactions, which later came under the control of genetic systems. In this view, the first cells did not replicate a gene but carried out a set of reactions that produced their own building blocks, and heredity came afterwards. Lane and Martin (2010) pointed out that the energetics of the earliest cells constrain which reactions are possible, and that energy is a central part of the origin question.

The energetic argument favours environments in which a natural gradient of protons across a mineral barrier could drive the synthesis of molecules, as in the alkaline hydrothermal vents that have been proposed as the site of origin. Such a gradient provides the energy that the cell would later capture with membrane pumps, and the early cell could have used it directly. The proposal is supported by the similarity of some metabolic reactions to the chemistry of these vents.

Carbon fixation, the conversion of carbon dioxide into organic matter, is a central step of metabolism. Some carbon fixation pathways could have operated without enzymes if metal ions and minerals catalysed the reactions, and the reverse of some of these pathways are found in modern organisms. The plausibility of such a route depends on the availability of the catalysts and on the thermodynamics of each step.

Experiments test parts of these networks by reproducing the reactions under conditions that resemble the proposed environment. The experiments show whether the reactions proceed and whether the products can be used to drive further reactions. Ioannidis (2005) described how an experiment that reproduces a reaction in isolation may not show that the network can sustain itself, and the test must consider the network as a whole. Cumming (2014) argues that the rates and the yields should be reported with their intervals.
