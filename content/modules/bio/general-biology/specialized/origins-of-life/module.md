The origin of life is the transition from chemistry to biology, and it is among the least understood events in the history of the planet. Life as we know it is built from a set of molecules and processes that together can replicate, metabolise and evolve, and the question is how such a system could have arisen from the chemistry of the early Earth. No experiment has yet produced life from non-living materials, and the field proceeds by testing hypotheses about the intermediate steps.

The topic begins with prebiotic chemistry, which asks how the building blocks of life, such as amino acids, nucleotides, sugars and lipids, could have been made without biology. Lane and Martin (2010) discussed the energetic constraints that shaped the first cells, and the chemistry of the early environment determines which building blocks were available. The chapters that follow consider the replicating molecules, the metabolic networks, the compartments and the reconstruction of the common ancestor of living organisms.

The RNA world hypothesis proposes that RNA molecules that could both store information and catalyse reactions preceded DNA and proteins. Watson and Crick (1953) described the structure of DNA, which makes clear how a molecule can store information in a sequence, and the same principle applies to RNA. The hypothesis faces the difficulty that RNA must be made and copied without the enzymes that now do this, and the chemistry of this step is not yet fully explained.

Metabolism-first hypotheses propose that networks of chemical reactions, driven by the energy of the environment, came before any genetic system. Lane and Martin (2010) argued that the energy available to early cells shaped their organisation, and the energetic arguments favour certain environments. The evidence from these hypotheses is largely from the chemistry that can be reproduced in the laboratory, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a result obtained under convenient conditions may not describe the conditions of the early Earth. Cumming (2014) argues that the outcomes of such experiments should be reported with their uncertainties, and Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the conditions and the reconstruction methods.
