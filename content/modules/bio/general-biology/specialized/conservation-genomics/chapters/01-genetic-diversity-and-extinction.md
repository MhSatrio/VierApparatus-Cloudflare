Genetic diversity is the raw material of adaptation, and a population that loses it has fewer options when conditions change. In a small population, genetic drift removes variants at random, and heterozygosity declines each generation at a rate that depends on the effective population size. The decline is slow in a large population and fast in a small one, and a population of a few dozen individuals can lose much of its diversity within a few generations. Allendorf et al. (2010) described how genomic markers reveal this loss far more precisely than earlier methods.

Small populations also lose fitness through inbreeding, in which mating between relatives exposes harmful recessive alleles in the homozygous state. The inbreeding coefficient rises with each generation of close mating, and its effect on survival and reproduction can be large. Mildly harmful mutations, which are removed slowly by selection in large populations, can accumulate in small ones because drift overwhelms the weak selection against them.

The extinction vortex describes the interaction of these processes. A decline in numbers increases inbreeding and drift, which reduces fitness, which further reduces numbers, and each stage reinforces the next. Escaping the vortex requires breaking one of its links, such as by restoring gene flow or by increasing the population, and the effect of each intervention should be measured.

The measurement of diversity with genomic markers is far more informative than with a few loci, since it estimates the diversity across the genome and reveals the regions of low variation. The estimates depend on the markers and on the sample, and they should be reported with their uncertainties. Cumming (2014) argues that the heterozygosity and the effective size should be given with their intervals, and Ioannidis (2005) described how an estimate from a small sample may be misleading about the population as a whole.
