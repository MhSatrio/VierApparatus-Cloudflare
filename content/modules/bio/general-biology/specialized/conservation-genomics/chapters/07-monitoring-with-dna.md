DNA-based monitoring allows populations and communities to be surveyed without capturing or disturbing the animals. Faeces, hair, feathers and shed skin contain enough DNA to identify the species and often the individual that left them, and these samples can be collected along trails or in the habitat. Taberlet et al. (2012) showed that the same principles extend to whole communities, through the metabarcoding of environmental samples, which allows many species to be monitored at once.

Non-invasive genotyping supports capture-recapture estimates of population size. Each sample is genotyped, and the individuals are identified by their multilocus genotypes, so that the recapture of a known individual can be recognised. The genotypes are error-prone, since low-quality samples give incomplete or incorrect genotypes, and these errors can make one individual appear as two or two as one. The errors must be measured and corrected, since they bias the population size estimate.

Monitoring programmes detect changes in populations and communities over time, and the trends they reveal are only meaningful if the detection probability is known. A species that is detected in fewer surveys may have declined, or it may simply be harder to detect, and the two cannot be separated without estimating the detection probability. Cumming (2014) argues that the trends should be reported with their intervals and the detection probabilities on which they depend.

The sensitivity of monitoring depends on the sampling effort, and a programme that samples too little may fail to detect a decline that is occurring. The design should be chosen to detect the change of interest with stated power, and the effort should be justified before the programme begins. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result from a sparse survey may mislead, and the sampling effort should be reported with each trend.
