Local adaptation occurs when populations evolve traits that suit their own environments, so that an individual from one population may perform worse in another. Adaptation shapes the genomes of populations, and the genetic signatures of selection can reveal the loci involved. Nielsen (2005) reviewed the statistical signatures that selection leaves, including reduced diversity around a selected site and unusual differentiation of allele frequencies, and these signatures are the starting point for the identification of adaptive loci.

Genotype-environment association analyses relate the frequencies of alleles to environmental variables such as temperature, rainfall or soil. An allele whose frequency tracks an environmental gradient more closely than expected by chance is a candidate for adaptation to that gradient. The analysis must control for the shared history of populations, since populations that are close together share both their environment and their ancestry, and this shared history can produce spurious associations.

Genomic signatures alone do not confirm local adaptation. The most direct evidence comes from common garden or reciprocal transplant experiments, in which individuals from different populations are grown in the same environments, or in which they are moved between environments. If local individuals have higher fitness than foreign ones in their own environment, adaptation is confirmed. Cumming (2014) argues that the difference in fitness should be reported with its interval, since the experiment must be large enough to detect it.

Demographic history and isolation by distance can produce patterns that mimic selection, and the management of adaptation must consider this. The movement of individuals between populations may disrupt locally adapted genotypes, and the decision to move individuals should weigh this risk. Ioannidis (2005) described how a candidate that fits an attractive explanation may be accepted before the alternatives are excluded, and the evidence for adaptation should be tested in more than one way.
