Hybridisation occurs when individuals of two species or two distinct populations interbreed and produce offspring with ancestry from both. Genomic data reveal hybrids by the proportion of their genome that derives from each parent, and this ancestry can be estimated with models that assign each locus to one source or the other. Allendorf et al. (2010) described how genomic ancestry is more informative than the handful of markers used earlier, since it reveals the history of mixing across the genome.

Introgression is the movement of genes from one species into another through repeated backcrossing of hybrids with one parent. A rare species that hybridises with a more abundant relative can lose its distinctiveness, as its genome is gradually replaced by that of the abundant species. This process threatens the identity of the rare species, and it can continue even when the pure individuals are few, since the hybrids can still backcross.

The conservation of a species threatened by hybridisation requires a decision about how to respond. The hybrids may be removed to protect the rare species, or they may be accepted as part of the biodiversity to be conserved, and the choice reflects values as well as science. The consequences of each option for the species and for the ecosystem must be assessed, and the decision should be transparent.

The difficulty of defining species when they exchange genes is long-standing. Genomic data show that the boundaries between some species are porous, and that the genomes of related species contain segments of mixed ancestry. A conservation unit may therefore need to be defined by its genomic composition rather than by a sharp boundary. Cumming (2014) argues that the ancestry proportions should be reported with their intervals, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the markers, the reference populations and the assignment method.
