A conservation genomics report supports decisions that affect the survival of species, and it must allow those decisions to be judged. The species, the populations and the sampling design should be stated first, with the number of individuals and the way they were chosen. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and for a conservation study those means include the samples, the markers and the analysis.

The sample types and their quality should be described, along with the markers and the genotyping error rate. Non-invasive samples are often of low quality, and the error rate of their genotypes can change the estimates substantially. Allendorf et al. (2010) emphasised that the precision of genomic estimates depends on the data quality, and the report should show it.

The estimates of diversity, effective size and ancestry should be reported with their intervals, and the assumptions of each analysis should be stated. Cumming (2014) argues that each estimate should be shown with its precision, since a decision based on an imprecise estimate is a different decision from one based on a precise one. Ioannidis (2005) described how an estimate that is taken as certain may lead to a poor decision.

Each management recommendation should be linked to the evidence and the uncertainty behind it, and the monitoring data and their detection probabilities should be reported. The genotypes, the sequences and the analysis code should be deposited with identifiers, so that the recommendations can be checked and revised as new data arrive. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended keeping the inputs, steps and outputs together, which is essential when a decision will be revisited.
