Genomic tools provide the evidence that conservation managers use to make decisions. The most basic decision is which populations to manage as separate units, and genetic structure, measured by the differentiation of allele frequencies among populations, provides the evidence. Two populations that are strongly differentiated may have diverged for long enough to be distinct units, while populations that exchange many migrants are usually managed together. Allendorf et al. (2010) described how genomic markers have improved the resolution of these decisions.

Genomic data also identify individuals and their parentage, which allows the monitoring of populations without capture and the tracking of the offspring of particular individuals. Genetic identification can reveal the number of individuals in a population more accurately than counts of sightings, and parentage analysis can show which individuals contribute to the next generation. Each analysis depends on the markers and on the error rate of the genotyping, and both should be reported.

The origin of seized specimens can be traced by comparing their genomes with those of known populations, which helps to detect illegal trade and to direct enforcement. The assignment of a specimen to a population is probabilistic, and the probability should be reported, since a wrong assignment can lead to the wrong conclusion about the source. Ioannidis (2005) described how an assignment that is treated as certain may mislead a decision.

In captive breeding, genomic data can be used to choose the pairings that maximise the retained diversity, since the mean relatedness of the population determines how much diversity is lost over generations. The trade-off is that pairings that maximise diversity may not maximise the number of offspring, and the managers must balance the two. Cumming (2014) argues that the estimates of diversity and relatedness should be reported with their intervals, since they are sensitive to the markers chosen and to the sample of individuals.
