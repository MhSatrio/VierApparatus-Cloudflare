Genetic rescue restores a small and inbred population by introducing individuals or genes from another population. The new alleles reduce the expression of harmful recessive variants, which is the basis of inbreeding depression, and they increase the diversity available for adaptation. Allendorf et al. (2010) described genetic rescue as one of the most effective tools of conservation genetics, and it has been used in several species with clear benefits in survival and reproduction.

The benefit depends on the source population and on the degree of divergence between the populations. If the two are too different, their hybrids may have lower fitness than either parent, a phenomenon called outbreeding depression, which can make the rescue harmful. The choice of source population is therefore a balance: it should be genetically different enough to supply new variation, but not so different that the hybrids are poorly adapted.

The number of migrants needed depends on the size and the inbreeding of the recipient population, and on the rate at which the introduced alleles are lost. A small number of migrants may be enough to restore diversity, but the effect may fade without continued gene flow. The number and the frequency of migrants should be stated, and the program should be monitored to determine whether further introductions are needed.

Genomic data show whether the introduced alleles have spread and whether the diversity of the population has increased, but they do not show whether the population is healthier. Fitness measures, such as survival, growth and reproduction, are needed to demonstrate that the rescue has worked. Cumming (2014) argues that the fitness changes should be reported with their intervals, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a genomic improvement may not be accompanied by a biological one.
