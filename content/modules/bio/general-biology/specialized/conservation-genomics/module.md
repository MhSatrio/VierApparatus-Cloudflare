Conservation genomics applies the tools of genomics to the protection of species and populations. Many species are now small and fragmented, and their survival depends on whether they retain enough genetic variation to adapt and to avoid the harm of inbreeding. Allendorf et al. (2010) argued that genomic data can answer questions that earlier genetic markers could not, such as which populations are distinct, how much variation they carry and which genes allow them to live where they do.

The topic begins with the loss of genetic diversity in small populations, which reduces their capacity to respond to change and exposes them to the expression of harmful recessive alleles. The effective population size, which is smaller than the census number when sex ratio, variance in reproduction or fluctuations are considered, determines the rate of this loss. The chapters that follow examine the estimation of these quantities, local adaptation, genetic rescue, hybridisation and the monitoring of populations.

Genomic data reveal the signatures of selection, which identify genes that may underlie adaptation to local conditions. Nielsen (2005) reviewed the statistical signatures that selection leaves in genomes, and these signatures can guide the identification of populations that should be managed as distinct units. The inference is uncertain, since demographic history can produce similar signatures, and the uncertainty should be reported with each decision.

The management decisions based on genomic data affect the fate of species and the cost of conservation, and they must be supported by evidence that is reproducible and whose uncertainties are shown. Ioannidis (2005) described how a decision based on a single result may not hold, and Cumming (2014) argues that the estimates of diversity and effective size should be reported with their intervals. Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the samples, the markers and the analysis.
