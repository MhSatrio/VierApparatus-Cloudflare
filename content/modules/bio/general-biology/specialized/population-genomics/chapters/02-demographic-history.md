The demographic history of a population is recorded in its genome. The site frequency spectrum, which counts the variants at each frequency in a sample, is shaped by the population's size over time. A population that has recently declined in size has an excess of rare variants relative to the expectation at constant size in some models, and a population that has expanded has a similar excess, so the spectrum must be read together with other information. Nielsen (2005) noted that the demographic signal and the selection signal can be confused, which is why the history must be inferred with care.

Linkage disequilibrium records the population's size over recent generations, since a small population develops more linkage among loci. Runs of homozygosity in individuals record recent inbreeding and therefore recent small size. The combination of these sources can reveal bottlenecks, expansions and splits between populations, and the inference is strongest when several sources agree.

Model-based inference fits a history of population splits, migrations and changes in size to the observed data. The model specifies the number of populations, the times of the events and the sizes, and the fit is judged by the likelihood or by a summary statistic. The model must be specified before the data are fitted, and its assumptions, such as random mating and the absence of selection, must be stated.

Different histories can produce similar spectra, and this identifiability problem means that the data may not distinguish between them. Two histories with different sizes and times may give the same pattern, and the inference may favour one by chance. The alternatives that fit the data equally well should be reported, along with the uncertainty of each parameter, since presenting a single history as the answer conceals the ambiguity. Ioannidis (2005) described how the best of many fitted models may be presented without the others, and Cumming (2014) argues that the parameters should be given with their intervals.
