Linkage disequilibrium, the non-random association of alleles at different loci, decays as the distance between loci increases, because recombination breaks the association over generations. The rate of decay depends on the recombination rate and on the population's size and history, and it records these quantities in the pattern of association along the genome. Nielsen (2005) described how this record is used to infer both recombination and selection, and it is one of the richest sources of information in population genomics.

Linkage can be generated by recent admixture, in which two populations that differ in allele frequencies have mixed, as well as by drift in a small population. The two sources produce linkage over different distances, and they must be distinguished before the linkage is interpreted as population history. Admixture linkage is typically extended over long distances, while drift linkage is more local.

Recombination maps are estimated from population data by fitting the decay of linkage across the genome, and the estimates depend on the sample size, on the demography and on the model assumed. The maps are used to convert physical distances into genetic distances, and they are needed for many analyses. Their uncertainty should be reported, since small errors in the map can bias the inference of selection.

The hitchhiking effect occurs when a beneficial allele rises in frequency and carries neighbouring neutral variants along with it, reducing diversity near the selected site. Background selection, in which deleterious alleles are removed by selection and carry neighbouring variants out of the population, reduces diversity in a similar way over broader regions. Both effects are dependent on recombination, since the closer a variant lies to the selected site the more likely it is to be carried along. Cumming (2014) argues that the linkage estimates should be reported with their intervals, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the sample sizes and the recombination maps used.
