A selective sweep occurs when a beneficial allele rises rapidly in frequency, carrying with it the neighbouring variation on its chromosome. The region around the selected site loses diversity, and the frequencies of nearby variants are shifted in characteristic ways. Nielsen (2005) described these signatures in detail, and a genome scan searches the genome for regions that show them, measuring the diversity and the frequency patterns in windows along the chromosomes.

Balancing selection has the opposite effect, maintaining several alleles at a locus for a long time, and it leaves an excess of intermediate frequencies and of diversity. Some genes involved in immunity and in the recognition of pathogens show such signatures. The detection of balancing selection uses different statistics from those that detect sweeps, and the two should not be confused.

The statistics that detect sweeps include the composite likelihood ratio, which compares the fit of a sweep model with a neutral model along the genome, and the integrated haplotype score, which measures the length of haplotypes carrying the derived allele. Each statistic has its own power and its own sensitivity to demography, and the choice should be justified.

The outlier approach flags loci whose differentiation among populations is unusually high, which may indicate divergent selection. The outliers are judged against a neutral baseline, which is the distribution of differentiation expected from the genome as a whole given the demography. The baseline must be estimated from the data, and an incorrect baseline produces false outliers.

Many loci are tested, and the threshold for a candidate must be corrected for the number of tests, as Ioannidis (2005) described for any search over many comparisons. Candidate regions should be confirmed by function and by independent data where possible. Cumming (2014) argues that the strength of each candidate should be reported with its interval, and the baseline, the statistic and the threshold should be stated.
