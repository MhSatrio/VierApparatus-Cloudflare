A plant disease epidemic develops as the infected tissue increases through the season, and the progress of disease is often described by a logistic curve. The rate of increase depends on how readily the pathogen infects new tissue and on the density of susceptible tissue, and the curve rises slowly at first, accelerates as infected tissue produces inoculum, and levels off as susceptible tissue runs out. Dangl and Jones (2001) noted that the pathogen's infection cycle sets the pace at which this happens.

Epidemics often begin in foci, small patches of infection from which the disease spreads outward. The spatial pattern reveals the source of the inoculum and the direction of spread, and the analysis of spatial pattern can show whether the disease arrives from outside the field or develops within it. Spores carried by wind spread the disease over distances that depend on the wind speed and the height of the crop.

Weather strongly influences the timing and the size of epidemics. Temperature and leaf wetness determine the success of infection, and a run of favourable days can produce a sudden increase in disease. The relation between weather and disease is non-linear, and it is used in forecasting systems that predict when control should be applied.

The rate of an epidemic is estimated by fitting a curve to the disease severity recorded at several dates. The rate parameter summarises the epidemic, and it can be compared among treatments and seasons. The assessment dates and the number of plants assessed determine the precision of the estimate, and both should be reported with it. Ioannidis (2005) described how a rate estimated from few assessments may be unreliable, and Cumming (2014) argues that the rate should be reported with its interval.
