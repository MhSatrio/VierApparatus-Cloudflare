The diagnosis of a plant disease begins with its symptoms, but many diseases produce similar symptoms, and the visual diagnosis is often uncertain. A reliable identification requires the isolation or the detection of the pathogen. Culturing the pathogen on a selective medium allows it to be grown and identified by its morphology and its growth, but many pathogens cannot be cultured, and the method is slow.

Immunological tests detect pathogen proteins with antibodies, and they are quick and suitable for field use. They are less sensitive than molecular methods, and they may cross-react with related organisms. The choice of antibody determines the specificity, and the cross-reactivity must be tested against the organisms likely to be present.

PCR and quantitative PCR detect pathogen DNA with high sensitivity, and they can quantify the pathogen in a sample. Loop-mediated and other isothermal methods allow the test to be performed in the field with portable equipment. These molecular methods can detect a pathogen at very low levels, and they require careful controls to avoid false positives from contamination and from related organisms with similar sequences.

Diagnostic tests are characterised by their sensitivity, the proportion of infected samples that give a positive result, and their specificity, the proportion of healthy samples that give a negative result. These are estimated by comparison with a reference method, and the validation should use samples with known status. Ioannidis (2005) described how a test that is validated only on clear cases may perform poorly on the ambiguous ones that occur in practice, and Cumming (2014) argues that sensitivity and specificity should be reported with their intervals. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the assay, its controls and the validation samples.
