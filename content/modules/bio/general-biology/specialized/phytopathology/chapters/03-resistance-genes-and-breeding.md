Resistance genes are the most important tool for controlling plant disease, and they are bred into crops through selection. Jones and Dangl (2006) described how many major resistance genes encode receptors that recognise specific pathogen effectors, so that a plant carrying the gene resists the pathogen races that carry the matching effector. The relation between the plant gene and the pathogen gene is often described as gene-for-gene, in which resistance follows when the two match.

Major resistance genes give strong protection against the races they recognise, but a pathogen that loses or alters the matching effector overcomes them. Such a race can spread rapidly through a crop that carries the gene, and the resistance is lost within a few seasons. The durability of a major gene therefore depends on the pathogen's capacity to evolve, and its deployment should consider the frequency of the races that would overcome it.

Quantitative resistance is controlled by many genes, each with a small effect, and it reduces disease without eliminating it. It is often more durable than major gene resistance, because the pathogen must overcome many components at once, and the change in the pathogen is correspondingly slower. Breeding for quantitative resistance requires the measurement of disease severity across many plants and seasons, which is costly.

Several major genes can be combined in one cultivar, a strategy called pyramiding, so that a pathogen must overcome all of them to succeed. The combination is more durable than any single gene, but the selection of plants with several genes is more demanding. Cumming (2014) argues that the disease response should be reported with its interval across plants, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a resistance gene identified in one population may perform differently in another, so the evaluation should use several races and environments.
