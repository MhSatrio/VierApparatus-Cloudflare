Fungicides control disease by acting on a specific target in the pathogen, and a single mutation in that target can make the pathogen insensitive to the fungicide. Because the mutation confers resistance without a large cost, a population exposed to repeated sprays of the same fungicide quickly selects for the resistant strain, and the fungicide loses its effect. Dangl and Jones (2001) described the pathogen's capacity to evolve as a central feature of plant disease, and fungicide resistance is one of its clearest expressions.

Multi-site fungicides act on several targets at once, so that resistance requires several mutations, which are rarely achieved. They are therefore less prone to resistance, and they are used as a core of resistance management. Mixing a single-site fungicide with a multi-site one, or alternating fungicides with different modes of action, slows the selection of resistance because each application places pressure on different targets.

Sensitivity is monitored in pathogen populations by bioassays, in which isolates are grown on media containing the fungicide, and by molecular tests that detect the resistance mutations directly. The monitoring shows whether the sensitivity of a population is declining, and it allows a change of strategy before control fails. The number of isolates tested and the sampling across fields determine the precision of the monitoring.

Some resistant strains carry a fitness cost, so that they grow or survive less well than sensitive strains when the fungicide is absent. The cost can slow the spread of resistance when the fungicide is withdrawn, and it is part of the rationale for alternation. The size of the cost must be measured rather than assumed, since it varies with the strain and the environment. Ioannidis (2005) described how a claimed benefit of a strategy may not hold when tested more widely, and Cumming (2014) argues that the frequency of resistance should be reported with its interval.
