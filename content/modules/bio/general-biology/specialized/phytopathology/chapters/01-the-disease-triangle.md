Disease in a plant requires three things to coincide: a host that can be infected, a pathogen that can infect it, and an environment that allows infection and the development of the disease. This is the disease triangle, and the absence of any one side prevents disease. Dangl and Jones (2001) described the outcome of an infection as depending on the balance between the pathogen's attack and the host's defence, and the environment modifies that balance at every step.

The host is susceptible when its genotype and its physiology allow the pathogen to establish itself. Resistance genes, the thickness of the cuticle, the presence of wounds and the stage of development all influence susceptibility, and a young leaf may be more susceptible than an old one. Susceptibility is a property of the host at a particular time, and it changes with the growth of the plant.

The pathogen must be virulent, which means that it can infect the host, and aggressive, which means that it spreads and causes damage once it has infected. Virulence depends on the effectors and the other molecules the pathogen uses, and aggressiveness on its growth rate and its capacity to produce spores. A pathogen may be present in large numbers without causing disease if it lacks the virulence to infect the host.

The environment favours infection when temperature, moisture and light are suitable for the pathogen. Leaf wetness is often critical, since many fungal spores require water to germinate and infect. The environment can be modified by management, such as the spacing of plants and the timing of irrigation, and these modifications may reduce disease without any change in the host or the pathogen. Ioannidis (2005) described how the effect of an intervention may be confounded by environmental variation, and Cumming (2014) argues that disease severity should be measured as the proportion of infected tissue with its interval across replicate plots.
