Disease forecasting predicts the risk of an epidemic from the weather and the crop, so that control can be applied at the most effective time. Weather-based models calculate the periods during which infection is likely, using temperature, leaf wetness and humidity, and they signal when the risk is high. The forecast allows fungicides to be applied only when they are needed, which reduces cost and the selection of resistance in the pathogen.

A decision threshold translates the forecast into an action. When the predicted risk exceeds a chosen level, the grower is advised to apply control, and the threshold is set from the relation between risk and yield loss. The threshold must balance the cost of control against the loss that would occur without it, and the choice of threshold determines how often sprays are applied.

A forecast must be validated against observed disease in independent seasons, since a model fitted to one set of seasons may fail in others. The validation compares the predicted risk with the disease that subsequently develops, and it should be performed on seasons not used to build the model. Ioannidis (2005) described how a model judged on the data that built it overstates its accuracy, and the validation must be independent.

The economic evaluation compares forecast-guided spraying with calendar spraying, in which the crop is sprayed on a fixed schedule. The forecast saves sprays in seasons of low risk, but it may miss an epidemic if the forecast fails, and the balance of these outcomes determines its value. Cumming (2014) argues that the costs and the disease losses should be reported with their intervals across seasons, and the errors in the weather data and the forecast should be stated.
