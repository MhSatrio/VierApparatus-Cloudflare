Plant pathogens differ in how they feed on their hosts. Biotrophic pathogens take nutrients from living cells and must keep the host alive to continue, while necrotrophic pathogens kill host cells and feed on the dead tissue. Dangl and Jones (2001) noted that this difference determines which defences are effective, since the immune response that kills infected cells can help against biotrophs and assist necrotrophs. The distinction shapes the strategy of control for each pathogen.

Between crops, pathogens survive in seed, in the soil and in crop residues left in the field. A pathogen that survives in residue can infect the next crop from the start of the season, and the management of residue is an important means of control. The survival depends on the temperature, the moisture and the duration of the period between crops, and these must be considered when the risk is assessed.

Spores are dispersed by wind, water, insects and the seed itself. Wind dispersal can carry spores over long distances, and it links the disease in one field to that in another. The dispersal of spores is often the key to an epidemic, and the sampling of the air with spore traps reveals when and how much inoculum is arriving.

The infection process involves penetration of the host and colonisation of its tissues, and each stage requires particular conditions. The completion of the cycle produces spores that become the inoculum for the next infection, and the cycle may repeat several times in a season. Cumming (2014) argues that the number of spores and the timing of infection should be reported with their intervals, since they determine the timing of control.
