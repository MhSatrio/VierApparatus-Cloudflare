Phytopathology is the study of disease in plants. Plant diseases reduce the yield of crops and the quality of food and fibre, and they can destroy harvests when they spread in epidemics. The field draws on the biology of the pathogen, the immune system of the host and the influence of the environment, and it seeks to explain why disease occurs and how it can be prevented. Dangl and Jones (2001) described the integrated defences of plants against infection, which form the basis of much of the field.

The topic begins with the disease triangle, which states that disease requires a susceptible host, a virulent pathogen and a favourable environment, and that removing any one prevents disease. The life cycles of pathogens determine how they survive and spread, and the chapters that follow examine the immune responses that resist infection, the breeding of resistance, the spread of epidemics, the management of resistance to fungicides, diagnosis and forecasting.

Plant immunity has two layers, as described by Jones and Dangl (2006), and resistance genes bred into crops act on the second layer by recognising pathogen effectors. The durability of such resistance depends on the evolution of the pathogen, and the deployment of resistance must consider the pathogen's capacity to overcome it.

The reporting of disease studies must describe the pathogen, the inoculation method and the assessment of disease, since each affects the result. Ioannidis (2005) described how an effect of treatment on disease may be overstated when the assessment is subjective, and Cumming (2014) argues that the disease severity should be reported with its interval across plants and trials. Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the pathogen isolates, the methods and the environmental conditions.
