The microbiome is the community of microorganisms that live in and on a host, and in humans and other animals the gut microbiome is the largest and most studied. These communities contain bacteria, archaea, fungi and viruses, and they contribute to digestion, to the development of the immune system and to the defence against pathogens. Turnbaugh et al. (2007) set out the aims of the Human Microbiome Project, which sought to describe the communities of the body and to relate them to health, and the field has grown rapidly since.

The topic begins with the composition of the microbiome and the sequencing methods that reveal it. Marker gene sequencing and shotgun metagenomics describe the taxa and the genes present, and the diversity of a community is summarised by measures that depend on the sequencing depth and the sampling. The chapters that follow examine the links to health, the tests of causality, the influence of diet and antibiotics, the statistical analysis of compositional data and the reporting of studies.

Associations between the microbiome and disease are common in the literature, but an association does not show that the microbiome causes the disease. The disease may alter the microbiome, or a third factor may affect both. The germ-free animal, which lacks a microbiome, and the transplant of communities into such animals are the principal tests of causality, and they show whether the community is sufficient to produce an effect.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the flexibility of microbiome analyses, in choices of taxa, thresholds and models, can produce findings that do not replicate, and Cumming (2014) argues that the effect sizes should be reported with their intervals. Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended that the samples, the sequencing and the analysis be recorded.
