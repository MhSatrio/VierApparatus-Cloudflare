Diet is among the strongest influences on the gut microbiome. Changes in the proportions of fat, protein and carbohydrate can alter the community within days, and the shift is more pronounced when the diet changes its fibre content. Turnbaugh et al. (2007) emphasised the importance of diet in shaping the community, and the link has been confirmed in controlled feeding studies in animals and in people.

Dietary fibre is the substrate for many of the fermenting bacteria of the gut, and the fermentation produces short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate, propionate and acetate. These products nourish the cells that line the intestine and enter the circulation, where they influence the metabolism and the immune system of the host. The link between fibre, the community and the host is one of the most studied pathways in the field.

Animal studies have reported effects of artificial sweeteners and emulsifiers on the community and on the metabolism of the host, though the translation of these findings to people is not established. The effects depend on the dose, the duration and the host, and they must be tested in people with appropriate controls before their significance is judged.

Individuals differ in how their community responds to the same diet, and some people show little change while others show a large one. The variation may reflect the initial composition of the community, the host's genetics or other factors, and it limits the generality of any single dietary recommendation. Cumming (2014) argues that the response should be reported with its interval across individuals, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a dietary effect shown in a small group may not hold for the population.

Diet is measured imprecisely by questionnaires, which depend on memory and on the accuracy of food composition tables, and this measurement error weakens any association with the microbiome. Repeated dietary recording, biomarkers of food intake and controlled feeding studies improve the measurement, and their use should be reported.
