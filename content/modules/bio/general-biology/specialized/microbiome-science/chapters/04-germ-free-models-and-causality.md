The germ-free animal is reared in a sterile isolator, where it has no microorganisms at all. Such animals are used to test the causal role of the microbiome. If a germ-free animal develops a phenotype only when it receives a microbial community, the community is sufficient to produce the phenotype, and if the phenotype is absent when no community is present, the community may be necessary. Turnbaugh et al. (2007) pointed to such models as a route to understanding how the microbiome shapes the host.

In a typical experiment, a community is transferred from a donor, often by faecal transplant, into germ-free recipients. The recipients are then compared with those that receive a community from a different donor. A difference between the recipients that cannot be explained by the host's genotype or environment is attributed to the community. The design must ensure that the recipients are otherwise identical, which is achieved by housing and feeding them under the same conditions.

Donor diet and genotype can confound the transfer. A donor that is obese may carry a community that reflects its diet rather than its adiposity, and the recipient may then acquire the diet effect rather than a microbial one. The donors should be matched for diet and other characteristics where possible, and the transfer should be tested in several donors to show that the effect is general.

Transfer of a community that carries a phenotype from donor to recipient is evidence of sufficiency, but it does not identify the members of the community responsible. The identification of the responsible taxa or genes requires further experiments with defined communities or single strains. A transfer that reproduces a disease is stronger evidence than one that reproduces only a trait, since a trait may be a minor consequence of the community's composition. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result from a small number of animals may not replicate, and Cumming (2014) argues that the effect should be reported with its interval across recipients.
