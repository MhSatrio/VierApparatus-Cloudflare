Antibiotics are among the most powerful disturbances of the microbiome. They kill susceptible organisms across many taxa, and a course can reduce the diversity of the gut community for weeks or longer. Turnbaugh et al. (2007) noted that the microbiome is shaped by the environment and can be disturbed by external agents, and antibiotics are a clear example of such a disturbance.

The loss of diversity can leave gaps that other organisms, including pathogens, may fill. The best known example is infection by Clostridioides difficile, which can follow antibiotic treatment when the protective community has been reduced. The risk depends on the antibiotic, its duration and the composition of the community before treatment, and it is a central concern in clinical practice.

Antibiotics also select for resistant organisms and promote the spread of resistance genes within the community, through the transfer of mobile genetic elements. The community can therefore carry resistance long after the treatment has ended. The measurement of resistance genes in the community provides a record of this selection and of its persistence.

Recovery varies between individuals. Most communities return towards their original composition within weeks or months, but in some people certain taxa do not return to their former abundance, and the community settles into a different state. Faecal transplantation has been used to restore the community in patients with recurrent infection, and its effects on recovery are monitored by repeated sampling. Ioannidis (2005) described how a single post-treatment sample may suggest a recovery that is incomplete, and Cumming (2014) argues that the recovery should be reported with its interval across the time points sampled.
