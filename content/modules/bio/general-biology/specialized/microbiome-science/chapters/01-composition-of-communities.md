The microbial communities of the body are made up of many groups of organisms, and in the human gut a few bacterial phyla dominate in most individuals. Turnbaugh et al. (2007) described how these communities differ between body sites, with the gut, the mouth, the skin and the vaginal tract each supporting a characteristic set of organisms. The communities are shaped by the environment of each site, by the immune system and by the food and the other microorganisms that reach them.

The gut communities carry out fermentation of dietary fibre that the host cannot digest, producing short-chain fatty acids that nourish the intestinal wall and affect the metabolism of the host. The metabolic capacity of the community is therefore part of the host's physiology, and the loss of key groups may alter the products of fermentation. The roles of individual groups are inferred from their genes and from their products, and the inference must be tested where possible.

The community varies between individuals and within the same individual over time. Age, geography, diet and genetics all influence its composition, and the community of an individual tends to be stable over weeks and months, though it changes after disturbances such as illness or antibiotics. The degree of stability and the time needed to recover are measures of the community's resilience.

Diversity is summarised by alpha diversity, the richness and evenness within a sample, and beta diversity, the difference between samples. These measures depend on the sequencing depth and on the rarefaction used to standardise samples, and their values must be reported with the method. A single sample may not represent a person's typical community, since the community fluctuates, and repeated sampling gives a more reliable estimate. Cumming (2014) argues that the diversity should be reported with its interval across samples, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a single sample may give a misleading picture of an individual.
