Many studies have reported differences in the microbiome between people with and without a disease. Obesity, inflammatory bowel disease, metabolic disease and some cancers have each been associated with characteristic changes in community composition. Turnbaugh et al. (2007) anticipated that the microbiome would be linked to health, and the associations have since been reported in many conditions, although their consistency and their causal meaning remain under study.

The proposed mechanisms include effects on the immune system, in which microbial molecules train the response of immune cells, and effects on metabolism, in which the products of microbial fermentation influence the host's energy balance and its glucose handling. These mechanisms are supported by experiments in animals, but their translation to humans is uncertain.

A cross-sectional study measures the microbiome and the disease at one time, and it cannot distinguish cause from consequence. The disease may alter the microbiome through inflammation or treatment, or both may be produced by a third factor. Longitudinal studies that sample the microbiome before the onset of disease, and interventional studies that alter the microbiome and observe the outcome, are needed to establish a causal role.

Confounding is a serious problem. Diet, medication, host genetics and socioeconomic status affect both the microbiome and the risk of disease, and a comparison that does not control them may find an association that is not causal. The reproducibility of microbiome biomarkers across cohorts is also limited, since the same signature is often not found in another population. Ioannidis (2005) described how such inconsistency is a sign that many reported associations are not robust, and Cumming (2014) argues that the effect sizes should be reported with their intervals across cohorts.
