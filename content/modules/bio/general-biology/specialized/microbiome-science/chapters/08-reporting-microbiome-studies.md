A microbiome study can be judged only if the reader knows how the community was sampled, extracted, sequenced and analysed. The host population and its selection should be described, along with the age, the sex, the diet and the medication of the participants, since each affects the community. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and for a microbiome study those means include the samples, the chemistry and the software. Turnbaugh et al. (2007) emphasised that the characterisation of the community depends on how it is sampled.

The sampling site, its handling and the extraction method should be reported, since they change the community that is recovered. The sequencing approach, with the region or depth, must be stated. The controls for contamination and bias should be described and their results shown, because low-biomass samples are sensitive to contamination and the extraction and primers are sources of bias.

The bioinformatic pipeline, its version and its reference database should be reported, along with the compositional analysis and the handling of zeros. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the pipeline and its parameters, and the analysis should be described in enough detail that another group can reproduce it.

The associations should be reported with their effect sizes and intervals and with the confounders that were adjusted for. Cumming (2014) argues that the effect sizes and their intervals convey more than a significance test, and Ioannidis (2005) described how the many analyses possible in microbiome data can produce findings that do not replicate. The sequences and the code should be deposited with identifiers.
