Plants take up mineral nutrients from the soil through transporters in the membranes of root cells. Nitrate and ammonium supply nitrogen, phosphate supplies phosphorus, and potassium, calcium, magnesium and the micronutrients are taken up by specific transporters. Each transporter has its own affinity and capacity, and the uptake of each nutrient depends on the concentration in the soil and on the number of transporters the root expresses.

The plant regulates its transporters according to its nutrient status. A nutrient that is scarce in the plant induces the transporters that take it up, and a nutrient that is abundant represses them. Signals from the shoot also influence the root, so that the demand of the growing tissues is communicated to the uptake system. This regulation keeps the uptake matched to the need, and it can be measured by the expression of the transporter genes.

The architecture of the root system determines how much soil the plant can reach. Long, branched roots explore more soil and encounter more nutrients, and the root architecture responds to the local supply, growing more where the nutrient is concentrated. Mycorrhizal fungi and root bacteria extend this reach, as they do for phosphorus and nitrogen, and their contribution must be separated from that of the root.

The uptake of a nutrient can be measured by isotopes, which trace the nutrient from the soil into the plant, and by the nutrient content of the tissues. The measurement of uptake must consider the nutrient pool that the plant can access, and the balance of nutrients within the plant, since the nutrient taken up may be stored or moved to other organs. Ioannidis (2005) described how a measured uptake may reflect the sampling rather than the process, and Cumming (2014) argues that the uptake should be reported with its interval across plants and soils.
