Water moves through a plant from the soil to the atmosphere along a gradient of water potential. Water enters the roots, moves through the xylem of the stem and leaves, and evaporates from the surfaces of the mesophyll cells into the air spaces of the leaf, where it leaves through the stomata. The loss of water from the leaf, transpiration, creates a tension that draws water upward through the xylem, and the cohesion of the water column allows it to be lifted to great heights.

Stomata are the pores that admit carbon dioxide and release water vapour, and their opening is the main control of the balance between carbon gain and water loss. Ainsworth and Long (2004) noted that stomatal conductance responds to carbon dioxide, and the response of transpiration to rising carbon dioxide is therefore part of the water economy of the plant. The stomata close when the leaf loses water, which is signalled in part by abscisic acid produced in the roots and leaves.

The water column in the xylem is under tension, and it can break by cavitation when the tension becomes too large, forming an air embolism that blocks the conduit. Cavitation is a major cause of the death of plants in drought, and the vulnerability of the xylem to embolism is a key trait of drought tolerance. The loss of hydraulic conductance can be measured and is used to predict survival under stress.

The measurement of water potential and transpiration uses pressure chambers and porometers, and the conditions of measurement, including the time of day and the humidity, must be reported. Cumming (2014) argues that the water potential and the transpiration should be given with their intervals across plants, since they vary widely within a population. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the measurement procedures and the environmental conditions.
