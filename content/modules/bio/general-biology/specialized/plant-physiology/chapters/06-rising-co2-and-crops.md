Rising atmospheric carbon dioxide stimulates photosynthesis, because the carboxylating enzyme is not saturated at current concentrations and higher carbon dioxide also suppresses photorespiration. The stomata partly close in response, which conserves water. Ainsworth and Long (2004) synthesised the responses measured in free-air carbon dioxide enrichment experiments, in which crops and ecosystems are exposed to elevated carbon dioxide in the open field, and found a consistent stimulation of photosynthesis and biomass.

The response of crop yield is smaller than the response of photosynthesis, because other factors limit growth, including nitrogen, water, temperature and the capacity of the sink organs to use the extra carbon. The yield response also depends on the crop species, with C3 crops responding more strongly than C4 crops, which already concentrate carbon dioxide. Godfray et al. (2010) argued that these responses must be considered in the assessment of food security under a changing climate.

Elevated carbon dioxide often reduces the concentration of protein and micronutrients in the grain and leaves of crops, which affects the nutritional quality of food. The reduction is a concern for populations that depend on these crops for nutrition, and it has been measured in several crops grown under elevated carbon dioxide. The size of the reduction varies with the species and the nitrogen supply.

The responses interact with nitrogen, water and temperature, and the interaction determines the response in a given field. The synthesis of experiments shows average responses, but the variation across sites is large, and the response at a particular site may differ substantially from the average. Cumming (2014) argues that the responses should be reported with their intervals across experiments, and Ioannidis (2005) described how an average may conceal the variation that matters for a particular decision.
