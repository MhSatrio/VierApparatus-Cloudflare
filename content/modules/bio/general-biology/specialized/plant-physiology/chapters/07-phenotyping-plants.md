High-throughput phenotyping measures the physiology and the growth of many plants at once, using imaging, sensors and automated handling. Cameras record the colour and the architecture of the canopy, and the images are processed into traits such as leaf area, greenness and height. The method allows thousands of plants to be assessed in the time that manual measurement would take to assess a few, and it has transformed the study of plant performance.

Chlorophyll fluorescence measures the efficiency with which absorbed light is used for photosynthesis, since the fluorescence of the leaf rises when photosynthesis is inhibited. Thermal imaging measures canopy temperature, which rises when the plant closes its stomata in drought and transpiration falls. Both signals are indirect, and their relation to the underlying physiology must be established by calibration.

Automated watering and weighing record the water use of each plant over time, which allows the transpiration of many plants to be compared under controlled conditions. The weight changes reflect transpiration and growth together, and the separation requires care. The rooting of plants can be imaged in transparent growth systems, which reveal the architecture of the root system.

The images must be processed into traits, and the processing introduces error from lighting, background, shadows and the angle of the camera. The traits must be calibrated against manual measurements on a subset of plants, and the calibration should be reported with its interval. Cumming (2014) argues that the calibration error should be shown with the traits, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the imaging conditions and the processing pipeline.
