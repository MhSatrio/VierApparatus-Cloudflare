Plants cannot move away from stress, and their physiology must respond to it where they grow. Drought is the most widespread abiotic stress. The plant closes its stomata to conserve water, which also reduces carbon uptake, and it accumulates osmolytes such as proline and sugars to maintain the turgor of its cells. Ainsworth and Long (2004) noted that the response of photosynthesis to stress is part of the overall response of crops, and the balance between carbon and water determines the outcome.

Stress produces reactive oxygen species, which damage membranes, proteins and nucleic acids if they accumulate. Plants protect themselves with antioxidant enzymes and small molecules that scavenge these species, and the balance between their production and removal determines the extent of damage. The measurement of oxidative damage and of antioxidant capacity reveals how much stress the plant is experiencing.

Heat stress denatures proteins and disrupts membranes, and plants respond by producing heat shock proteins that act as chaperones to refold or protect other proteins. The temperature at which damage occurs depends on the species and on the duration of exposure. Salinity stress combines osmotic effects with the toxicity of sodium and chloride ions, and plants tolerate it by excluding ions, compartmenting them in the vacuole and accumulating compatible solutes.

Stress tolerance is measured by survival, by growth and by physiological indicators, and the choice of measure determines what is concluded. A plant that survives a stress may grow far less, and a physiological indicator may change without any loss of yield. Cumming (2014) argues that the tolerance should be reported with its interval across plants, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a tolerance demonstrated under one stress may not protect against another.
