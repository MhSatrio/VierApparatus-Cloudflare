Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy and fixes carbon dioxide into sugars. The light reactions, which take place in the thylakoid membranes of the chloroplast, split water, release oxygen and produce the energy carriers ATP and NADPH. The dark reactions, or the Calvin cycle, use these carriers to fix carbon dioxide into the three-carbon sugars from which the plant builds its tissues. Ainsworth and Long (2004) used the carbon fixation of crops as the basis of their synthesis of responses to rising carbon dioxide, and the Calvin cycle is central to that response.

The enzyme that fixes carbon dioxide, Rubisco, also reacts with oxygen, a reaction called photorespiration that wastes fixed carbon and energy. The competition between carbon dioxide and oxygen depends on temperature and on the concentration of carbon dioxide, and it is one of the main limits on the efficiency of photosynthesis. Some plants have evolved mechanisms that concentrate carbon dioxide around Rubisco, reducing this waste.

The C4 pathway fixes carbon dioxide first into a four-carbon acid in the mesophyll cells, and then delivers it to Rubisco in the bundle sheath cells, where its concentration is high. This arrangement suppresses photorespiration and allows the plant to use water and nitrogen more efficiently in hot and dry conditions. Crassulacean acid metabolism separates the fixation and the Calvin cycle in time, opening its stomata at night, which is advantageous in arid environments.

The rate of photosynthesis responds to light, carbon dioxide and temperature, and these responses are measured with gas exchange in leaf chambers. The measurements must report the conditions, since the rate depends on them, and the response across the range of conditions is more informative than a single value. Cumming (2014) argues that the response curves should be reported with their intervals, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a rate measured in a chamber may not represent the leaf in the canopy or the field.
