Plant development is coordinated by hormones, small signalling molecules that act at low concentrations and that control growth, division and differentiation. Auxin moves through the plant in a directional manner, and its gradients direct the growth of shoots and roots and the bending of organs towards light and gravity. Cytokinin promotes cell division and differentiation, and the balance between auxin and cytokinin determines the pattern of organ formation in the growing tissues.

Gibberellin promotes the elongation of stems and the germination of seeds, and its response is controlled by the repressors that it removes. Abscisic acid signals water deficit and other stresses, closes the stomata and maintains the dormancy of seeds. Ethylene is a gaseous hormone that controls the ripening of fruit and the response to damage and to submergence. The hormones interact in a network, with each one modifying the production and the response to the others.

The hormones are measured by extraction and mass spectrometry, which gives their concentrations, and by reporter genes that show where and when their signalling is active. The signalling responses are measured by the expression of the genes they regulate. The measurement must consider the sampling of the tissue, since a hormone may be present at very different levels in different cells.

The function of each hormone is revealed by mutants that lack its production or its signalling, and by applications that increase its level. The mutants show the developmental defects that the hormone normally prevents or promotes, and their analysis is a powerful way of establishing causality. Ioannidis (2005) described how a phenotype that is attributed to a hormone may have another cause, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the mutants, the treatments and the measurements.
