Plant physiology describes how plants work: how they capture light and turn it into sugar, how they draw water and nutrients from the soil, how they move resources through their tissues, and how they respond to hormones and to the stresses of the environment. These processes determine the yield of crops and the function of ecosystems, and they are the basis of efforts to improve plants for food, fibre and fuel. Godfray et al. (2010) argued that raising the productivity of plants is central to feeding a growing population, and physiology identifies the processes that limit productivity.

The topic begins with photosynthesis and the carbon economy of the plant. The rate of carbon fixation depends on light, on the concentration of carbon dioxide and on temperature, and the opening of the stomata that admit carbon dioxide also allows water to escape. The balance between carbon gain and water loss is central to the performance of a plant in dry conditions. The later chapters examine the water relations, the uptake of nutrients, the hormones, the stress responses, the effect of rising carbon dioxide and the phenotyping of plants.

Free-air carbon dioxide enrichment experiments have shown how crops and ecosystems respond to the rising concentrations of carbon dioxide, and Ainsworth and Long (2004) synthesised the responses across many such experiments. The responses are large for photosynthesis and smaller for the yield of crops, and the difference reflects other limits on growth. The results must be interpreted with the conditions of each experiment.

Cumming (2014) argues that the responses should be reported with their intervals across plants and experiments, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a response measured under one condition may not hold for others. Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the environmental conditions and the measurements so that physiological results can be reproduced.
