A mathematical model is a hypothesis expressed in equations. Its assumptions state which quantities matter and how they interact, and its consequences follow by calculation. Kermack and McKendrick (1927) began with a small set of assumptions about how people move between susceptible, infected and recovered states, and the epidemic curve they derived followed from those assumptions. The model is useful because its consequences can be compared with observations, and a model whose consequences are not observed is refuted.

Models serve different purposes, and it is useful to distinguish them. A descriptive model summarises data, with no claim about the mechanism. An explanatory model proposes a mechanism and asks whether it produces the observed behaviour. A predictive model is judged by its accuracy on new cases, without a claim that its mechanism is correct. A study must state which of these it claims to be, since the evidence required for each differs.

The assumptions determine what a model can show. A model that assumes the population is well mixed cannot represent spatial spread, and a model that assumes constant parameters cannot represent changes in behaviour. The assumptions are not incidental, and a conclusion drawn from a model is valid only within its assumptions. The assumptions should therefore be listed, and their consequences should be considered.

Models abstract and simplify, which means they omit detail, and the omitted detail may matter. The decision of what to leave out is a judgement that the modeller must justify. A model is not the system it represents, and a result from the model should not be presented as a fact about the system without the evidence that the abstraction holds. Ioannidis (2005) described how a model can be mistaken for the world when its assumptions are not examined, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the assumptions and the justification for each simplification.
