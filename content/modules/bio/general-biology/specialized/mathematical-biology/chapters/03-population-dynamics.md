Population models describe how the numbers of organisms change through birth, death, movement and interaction. The simplest is exponential growth, in which the population increases at a rate proportional to its size, which holds only while resources are unlimited. Limited resources are represented by a carrying capacity, and the logistic equation, in which the growth rate declines as the population approaches this capacity, is the standard form. The population then settles at a stable equilibrium set by the resources available.

The Lotka-Volterra equations describe a predator and a prey, in which the prey grow in the absence of predators and the predators decline in the absence of prey. The interaction produces cycles, in which the prey population rises, the predators follow, and the predators then reduce the prey until they decline in turn. The cycles are a property of the simple model, and they are neutrally stable, which means that small changes in the initial numbers persist as changes in the amplitude of the cycle.

Competition between species can lead to their coexistence or to the exclusion of one. Two species that compete for the same limited resource may coexist if each limits itself more than the other, and one will exclude the other if it is a better competitor. The outcome depends on the parameters, and the stability of the coexistence is determined by the balance of the interactions.

Harvesting and age structure affect the sustainability of a population, and the models must include them to estimate the yield that a population can support. Cumming (2014) argues that the predicted equilibria and cycles should be compared with the field data with their intervals. Ioannidis (2005) described how a model that reproduces a pattern in one population may not describe another, and the parameters should be estimated and tested separately for each population studied.
