Sleep is governed by two processes. The homeostatic process builds a pressure for sleep during wakefulness, and it is discharged by sleep. The circadian process, driven by the clock, sets the time of day at which the drive for sleep is strongest and at which wakefulness is most easily sustained. The timing and the length of sleep result from the interaction of these processes, and a disturbance of either shows as a change in the sleep that a person or an animal obtains.

Light at night and shift work are the main disturbances of this alignment. Light received at night signals to the clock that it is day, delaying the circadian signal for sleep, and shift work asks people to sleep when their clocks are set for activity. The result is a sleep that is shorter or of poorer quality, and a clock that is out of step with the environment. Panda (2016) linked such misalignment to disturbances of metabolism, and the chapter's concern is how to measure it.

Sleep can be measured by activity, by electroencephalography and by questionnaires, and each method has a limit. Activity monitors infer sleep from the absence of movement, which can be confused with quiet wakefulness, while electroencephalography measures the brain's electrical state directly but is costly. Questionnaires depend on recall and are often biased. The method must be chosen and reported with its limits.

The consequences of sleep loss for cognition and metabolism are substantial, and they are studied in controlled protocols in which the timing and length of sleep are set. The conditions of the protocol, including the light, the food and the time of testing, must be reported, since each can change the outcome. Cumming (2014) argues that the effects of sleep should be reported with their intervals across participants, since the response to sleep loss varies between people.
