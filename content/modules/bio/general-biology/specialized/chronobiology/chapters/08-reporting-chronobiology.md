A chronobiology report must allow another group to reproduce the rhythm and to compare it with others. The organism and its genotype come first, since different strains have different periods. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and for a rhythm study those means include the organism, the conditions and the sampling schedule. Takahashi (2017) showed that the clock differs between tissues and genotypes, so the tissue and the strain must be stated.

The conditions of the study should be given in full, including the light schedule, the intensity and the wavelength of light, the temperature, and whether the animals were held in constant conditions. The feeding schedule and its timing must be reported, since food is a powerful cue for the peripheral clocks. A rhythm measured in a light-dark cycle may be a masking effect, and the report must state whether an endogenous rhythm was tested in constant conditions.

The sampling times, the number of cycles and the number of samples per cycle should be stated, along with the time of day of every measurement. The method of rhythm detection should be given with the period, amplitude and phase, each with its interval. Cumming (2014) argues that the uncertainty of each estimate should be shown, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a rhythm detected in a small sample may not be found in a larger one.

Where chronotherapy is studied, the time of each dose must be reported with the outcome measured. The time series and the analysis code should be deposited with identifiers, so that the rhythm can be reanalysed and compared with others. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended keeping the inputs, steps and outputs of an analysis together, and this applies directly to the reanalysis of a rhythm.
