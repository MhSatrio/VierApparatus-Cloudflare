Chronotherapy gives a drug at the time of day at which it works best or causes least harm. The rationale is that the body's handling of a drug follows a daily rhythm: the rate at which a drug is absorbed, distributed, metabolised and excreted changes with the time of day, as do the activity of its target and the sensitivity of the tissues to its effects. A dose given at one time can therefore produce a different concentration and a different response from the same dose given at another time.

Examples exist in which the timing changes the toxicity of a drug, so that a dose given at a particular time is better tolerated, and in which the timing changes its efficacy. The rhythms of the disease itself also matter, since some conditions are more severe at certain times of day, and a treatment timed to coincide with the period of greatest risk may be more effective. These examples are the basis of the case for chronotherapy.

Clinical trials of dosing time must compare the timings directly, with the same drug and the same dose, and with random allocation of patients to the times. Without such controls, a difference in outcome may reflect the patients who were treated at each time rather than the timing itself. Ioannidis (2005) described how a benefit that is shown in a small or poorly controlled trial often fails to replicate, and the trials of chronotherapy have been affected by this problem.

Chronotherapy trials must report the time of each dose and the time at which the outcome was measured, since both determine the result. The pharmacokinetic rhythm should be measured in a subgroup where possible, and the variation between patients should be reported. Cumming (2014) argues that the difference between timings should be reported with its interval, since a small benefit may be real but uncertain.
