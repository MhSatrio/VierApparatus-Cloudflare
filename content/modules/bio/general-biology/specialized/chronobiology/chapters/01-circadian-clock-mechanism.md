The circadian clock is a feedback loop in which genes regulate their own expression. In mammals, the transcription factors CLOCK and BMAL1 bind to the DNA and switch on the period and cryptochrome genes. Their protein products accumulate, enter the nucleus and inhibit CLOCK and BMAL1, which shuts off their own transcription. As the repressors are degraded, the inhibition lifts and the cycle begins again. Takahashi (2017) described this architecture as a set of interlocked loops, in which several genes reinforce the rhythm.

The delay between the transcription of the repressor genes and the action of their protein products is the essential element. Without the delay the loop would settle to a steady state, but with it the system overshoots and oscillates. The length of the delay depends on the time taken for transcription, translation, modification and transport into the nucleus, and each step can be altered to change the period.

Post-translational modification sets the pace of the cycle. The repressor proteins are phosphorylated by kinases, which mark them for degradation, and the rate of this process determines how long they persist. A change in the stability of a repressor changes the period of the rhythm, and mutations that alter the kinases have been found to shorten or lengthen it. The period is therefore a property of the stability of the proteins as well as of the transcription.

Each cell carries its own clock, and the clocks of different tissues must be coordinated to produce a consistent rhythm in the whole organism. The coordination is provided by the central clock in the brain, which is entrained by light, and by signals such as hormones and the timing of feeding. The mechanism thus produces a rhythm that persists in constant conditions and is reset by external cues. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the genetic background, the conditions and the sampling times in studies of the clock, since each changes the observed period.
