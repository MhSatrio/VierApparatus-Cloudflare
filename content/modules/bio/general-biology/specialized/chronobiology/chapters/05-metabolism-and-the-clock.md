Metabolism follows a daily rhythm that is coordinated by the circadian clock. The liver, muscle and fat tissue handle glucose, lipids and amino acids differently at different times of day, and the enzymes that process these nutrients are regulated by the clock. Panda (2016) reviewed how the clock anticipates the feeding cycle, so that the metabolic machinery is ready for the food that the animal is expected to eat, and the metabolic rhythm is therefore a combination of the clock and the timing of food.

The timing of food is a powerful cue for the peripheral clocks. Animals fed only during the normally inactive period show shifts in the rhythms of their liver and other tissues, and their handling of glucose and lipid changes. The response depends on the time of feeding and on its composition, and a meal taken at a different time from usual may be processed less efficiently.

Chronic misalignment between the timing of food and the clock is thought to contribute to obesity and to diabetes, and studies in mice and in people have examined this link. The evidence in people is more limited than in animals, because the timing of food is difficult to control in free-living conditions, and the causal role of misalignment has not been fully established. Ioannidis (2005) described how a plausible mechanism may not hold when it is tested directly.

Metabolic measurements are sensitive to the time of food and the time of sampling, and studies that do not control these may give results that cannot be compared. The timing of each meal, the time of sampling and the fasting period should be reported, and the comparison should be made at matched times. Cumming (2014) argues that the metabolic response should be reported with its interval across animals or participants, since the response to the same timing varies between them.
