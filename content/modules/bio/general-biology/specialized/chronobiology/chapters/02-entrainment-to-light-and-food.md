The circadian clock must be set each day by cues from the environment, and light is the strongest of these. Light received by specialised cells in the retina reaches the central clock in the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the brain, and it shifts the phase of the clock depending on the time at which it arrives. A light pulse in the early night delays the clock, and a pulse in the late night advances it. The relation between the time of the pulse and the size of the shift is the phase response curve, which describes how the clock responds to light across the day.

Peripheral clocks in the liver, the gut and other tissues are set less by light than by the timing of food and by the hormones that accompany feeding. Panda (2016) showed that restricting food to a time of day can shift the clocks of the peripheral tissues, sometimes independently of the central clock. The peripheral clocks are therefore entrained by a different cue from the central clock, and the two can disagree.

When light and food cues conflict, as they do in shift work or in eating late at night, the clocks of different tissues can fall out of step with one another. This desynchronisation is thought to contribute to the metabolic disturbances seen in people who work at night. The degree of desynchronisation can be measured by the timing of rhythms in different tissues, and a measurement of one tissue alone does not show it.

Phase shifts must be measured in a way that can be reproduced. The intensity, the wavelength and the duration of the light, and the timing of the pulse relative to the previous dark period, all affect the shift. Cumming (2014) argues that the shift should be reported with its interval across animals, since individuals differ in their response. Ioannidis (2005) described how a shift observed under one protocol may not repeat under another, and the protocol should be fully specified.
