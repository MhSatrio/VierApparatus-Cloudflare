Chronobiology studies the rhythms that organise life in time. Most organisms show a circadian rhythm, a cycle of about twenty-four hours that persists in constant conditions and is set by the light and the other cues of the environment. The rhythm governs sleep, activity, hormone secretion, body temperature and the metabolism of food, and an organism that is out of step with it suffers. Takahashi (2017) described how the rhythm is generated within cells by a molecular clock built from a set of genes whose products regulate one another.

The topic begins with the clock itself, a set of transcriptional feedback loops in which activators drive the expression of repressors that later inhibit the activators, producing a cycle of about a day. The period of the cycle depends on the delays in the loops and on the stability of the proteins, and the clock can be reset by external cues. The chapters that follow examine the entrainment of the clock, its link with sleep and metabolism, its application in medicine, and the measurement of rhythms.

Panda (2016) showed that the clock in peripheral tissues is coordinated with the feeding cycle, and that the timing of food can alter the rhythms of metabolism. The interaction between the clock and metabolism means that the time of day at which a measurement is made can change its result, and this must be considered in any study.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a result obtained at one time of day may not hold at another, and Cumming (2014) argues that rhythmic measurements should be reported with their intervals across times and individuals. Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the time of sampling and the conditions of light and feeding, since these determine the result.
