A variant that is screened in a cell reports the activity of the cell, which depends on how much of the protein is present as well as on how active each molecule is. A mutation can change either or both, and a variant that appears more active may simply be produced in greater amounts. Bornscheuer et al. (2012) emphasised that the expression of an engineered enzyme must be measured to separate these effects, since a higher expression is not an improvement in the enzyme itself.

Codon usage influences the rate of translation and can alter the amount of protein produced without any change in the protein's sequence. A synonymous change in the gene may therefore change the apparent activity, and the codons should be considered when variants are compared. The host's preference for codons and the availability of its transfer RNAs should be stated in the description of the expression system.

Folding in the cell is assisted by chaperones, which prevent misfolding and aggregation. A variant that folds poorly may be degraded or may aggregate into inclusion bodies, which lowers the amount of soluble, active protein. The aggregation of a variant is a property of its sequence and of the cell, and it should be measured as the fraction of soluble protein.

The activity of a variant should be normalised to the amount of protein present to obtain the specific activity of each molecule. Cumming (2014) argues that the normalised estimate should be reported with its interval, since both the activity and the amount are measured with error. Ioannidis (2005) described how a comparison that is not corrected for a confounder can produce an apparent improvement, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the host, the vector, the conditions and the measurement method for expression.
