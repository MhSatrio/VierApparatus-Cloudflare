Many marine animals and algae are not alone. They carry symbiotic microbes that live within their tissues, and the microbes often produce compounds that the host uses or that were long attributed to the host itself. Sponges host dense communities of bacteria that make a large share of the bioactive compounds found in the sponge, and corals and other invertebrates carry symbionts of their own. Mora et al. (2011) noted that the diversity of the microbial world is far greater than that of the animals that host it, which makes these partnerships a large and largely unexplored resource.

A compound isolated from a sponge may therefore be made by its symbiont rather than by the sponge. Attributing the compound correctly matters for its supply, since a symbiont may be cultivated and a sponge may not, and for its regulation. The attribution is usually made by identifying the biosynthetic genes in the symbiont and by showing that they are the source of the compound. Ioannidis (2005) described how an attribution that rests on co-occurrence alone may not hold, and the genetic evidence should be direct.

Most marine microbes cannot be grown in the laboratory, so their products are known only from their genomes. The biosynthetic gene clusters can be found in metagenomic data and their likely products predicted from their sequences. A product predicted in this way has not been isolated, and its structure and activity must be confirmed before it is claimed. Single-cell methods can link a gene to the organism that carries it, which helps to resolve which symbiont produces a compound.

The microbes of the ocean also cycle nutrients and degrade pollutants, which makes them important to the health of marine ecosystems as well as to biotechnology. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the identity of each symbiont and the method used to link it to its product, and Cumming (2014) argues that the abundance of symbionts should be reported with its interval across hosts.
