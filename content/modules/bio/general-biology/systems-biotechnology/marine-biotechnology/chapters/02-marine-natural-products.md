Marine organisms produce a wide range of chemical compounds that are not needed for their primary metabolism, and many of these secondary metabolites have biological activity. Blunt et al. (2018) reviewed the compounds reported from marine sources and grouped them by their chemical classes, including polyketides, peptides, terpenes and alkaloids. The classes differ in their structures and their activities, and the chemistry of a compound often reveals the pathway that produced it.

Many marine compounds serve a function for their producer, such as defence against predators, competition for space with other organisms, or the signalling that coordinates the behaviour of a colony. The biological activity that makes a compound useful to a human, such as the ability to inhibit a cell or a microbe, is often the same activity that protects the producer from its enemies. This is one reason that marine natural products have been a productive source of drug leads.

Several compounds from marine sources have reached clinical use, and many more serve as leads for the development of drugs. The path from a natural compound to a drug is long, and it requires confirmation of the activity, the identification of the active part of the molecule and the optimisation of its properties. The reported activities should be confirmed in independent assays before the compound is taken further.

Many marine compounds are produced in very small amounts, sometimes milligrams from tonnes of organism. This makes the supply the main barrier to development, since a promising compound cannot be tested or developed without a reliable source. Synthesis, cultivation of the producing microbe and the cultivation of the animal or alga are the means of supply, and each has its limits. Cumming (2014) argues that the yield and the activity should be reported with their intervals, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a striking activity reported in one assay may not replicate in another.
