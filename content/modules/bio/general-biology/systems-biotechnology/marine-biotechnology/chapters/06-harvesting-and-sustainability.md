Many marine organisms used in biotechnology are harvested from the wild, and the harvest can deplete the populations on which the industry depends. A species that is collected for a compound may be reduced in its numbers, and its loss may affect the ecosystem to which it belongs. The sustainability of a harvest therefore depends on the size of the population and on the rate at which it can recover, and a harvest that exceeds this rate cannot be maintained.

The sustainable yield of a population is the harvest that it can support without declining over time. It depends on the growth rate of the population, its size and the mortality caused by the harvest, and estimating it requires data on abundance and productivity that are often scarce for marine species. Cumming (2014) argues that the sustainable yield should be reported with its interval, since it is estimated from populations that are poorly measured.

The alternatives to harvesting wild organisms reduce the pressure on populations. Cultivation of the organism, whether in tanks, ponds or farms, can supply the product without collecting from the wild, though the cultivated organism may not produce the same compound at the same level. Chemical synthesis can produce a compound from simple starting materials, and fermentation of a symbiotic microbe can supply the compound without the host. Each route must be assessed for its cost and its environmental effects.

Harvesting also affects the biodiversity and the genetic resources on which the industry relies. The loss of a population removes the genetic variation that might later be useful, and the regulation of access and of the benefits derived from marine genetic resources is a matter of international concern. Ioannidis (2005) described how a population that appears stable in a short survey may decline over a longer period, and the harvest should be monitored over time. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the location, quantity and method of every harvest so that its sustainability can be assessed.
