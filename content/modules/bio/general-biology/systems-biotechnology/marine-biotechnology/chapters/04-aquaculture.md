Aquaculture is the farming of aquatic species, and it now supplies a large share of the fish and shellfish eaten by people. Farmed species include finfish, crustaceans, molluscs and seaweeds, and each is grown in a different system, from ponds and tanks to sea cages and longlines. The industry depends on the biology of the species, on the water in which they are grown and on the feeds that nourish them, and biotechnology contributes to each of these.

Genetic improvement of farmed stocks uses selection and breeding to increase traits such as growth rate, disease resistance and feed conversion. A breeding programme must measure these traits accurately across many families, and the heritability and the genetic correlations between traits determine how fast the stock can improve. Cumming (2014) argues that the response to selection should be reported with its interval across generations, since the gains are estimated from measured populations.

The feeds used in aquaculture have been based on fishmeal and fish oil, whose supply is limited, and the search for alternatives has become a central concern. Plant proteins, insects and microbial biomass are being developed as replacements, and their effect on the health and the flesh quality of the farmed fish must be measured. The sustainability of a feed depends on its source as well as its performance, and the comparison should account for both.

Crowded cultures allow diseases to spread, and vaccines, husbandry and the control of water quality are used to limit them. Farms also affect the water and the wild populations around them through the nutrients and the medicines they release, and these effects must be monitored. Ioannidis (2005) described how a claim of low environmental impact that rests on a few measurements may not hold for the whole farm, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the production, the health and the environmental data so that the sustainability of a farm can be checked.
