A marine study can be checked only if the source of its material is known precisely. The collection location, the depth, the date and the method should be given for each sample, since the same organism may produce different compounds in different places and seasons. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and for a marine sample the means include the collection and the identification.

Each organism should be identified, with the method used to identify it. A morphological identification, a DNA barcode and a voucher specimen deposited in a museum give different levels of confidence, and the report should state which were used. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the identity of every input, and an unidentified or misidentified organism undermines the rest of the study.

The extraction and purification of a compound should be described with the yields at each step, and the bioassays should be reported with their conditions, their replicates and their controls. Cumming (2014) argues that the activity and the yield should be given with their intervals, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a striking activity from one extract may not replicate. The concentration tested and the solvent used should be given, since both affect the result.

The sequences should be deposited with identifiers, and the sustainability of the harvest should be reported with the quantity collected and its effect on the population. The uncertainty of each estimate should be given, and the data and the analysis code should be made available so that others can reproduce and extend the study.
