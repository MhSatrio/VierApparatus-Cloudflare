The number of species in the ocean is one of the most uncertain numbers in biology. Mora et al. (2011) estimated the total number of species on Earth and in the ocean by extrapolating from the rates at which new species have been described, and their estimates indicated that a large fraction of marine species remain undescribed. The uncertainty is wide, because the extrapolation depends on assumptions about how the rate of description will change and how many species are yet to be found in poorly sampled groups.

Diversity is concentrated in some places. Coral reefs host a large share of the species in a small area of the ocean, and the deep sea contains many species that are known from few specimens. The sampling effort is uneven, with shallow and accessible waters studied far more than the deep and remote ones, so that the apparent distribution of diversity partly reflects where scientists have looked.

Many marine organisms are small, rare or hard to culture, and they can be described only with difficulty by morphology. Microbes in particular are numerous and varied, and most cannot be grown in the laboratory. DNA barcoding, which identifies species by a short standard gene, and metagenomics, which sequences the DNA of an entire community, reveal diversity that morphology and culture miss. These methods have greatly increased the number of known lineages, although the identity of a sequence is not always equivalent to a described species.

The completeness of knowledge matters for biotechnology, because a compound or an enzyme can only be found in an organism that has been sampled. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the identity of each organism and the method used to identify it, so that the diversity that supports a discovery can be assessed. Cumming (2014) argues that the number of species in a sample should be reported with its uncertainty, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a diversity estimate from a limited survey may not represent the whole.
