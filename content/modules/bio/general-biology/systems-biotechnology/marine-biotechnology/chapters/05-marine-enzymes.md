Enzymes from marine organisms work in conditions that would inactivate many industrial enzymes, and this makes them valuable as catalysts. Cold-active enzymes from polar and deep-water organisms catalyse reactions efficiently at low temperatures, which saves energy and suits processes that must avoid heating. Halophilic enzymes from organisms that live in salt water or in salt lakes function at high salt concentrations, where most proteins precipitate. Blunt et al. (2018) noted that many of the bioactive compounds of marine organisms are matched by enzymes whose chemistry has adapted to the same environment.

Enzymes from deep-sea microbes tolerate the high pressures of the deep ocean, which can alter the folding and the volume changes of a reaction. The pressure adaptation shows in the flexibility and the packing of the protein, and it may give the enzyme properties that are useful at high pressure in industry. Marine polysaccharide-degrading enzymes break down the complex carbohydrates of algae, and they are used to process seaweed and to produce sugars and other chemicals from it.

Most of these enzymes are found by screening metagenomic libraries, in which DNA from uncultured marine microbes is cloned and expressed, and the clones are tested for an activity of interest. The screen identifies new sequences, which are then characterised. The hit rate depends on the library and the assay, and the number of clones screened must be stated.

The conditions of an assay must match the marine environment for the measurement to be meaningful. An enzyme that is assayed at room temperature in buffer may behave quite differently at the temperature and salt of its native habitat. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result obtained under the wrong conditions may not describe the enzyme in use, and Cumming (2014) argues that the kinetic parameters should be reported with their intervals and the conditions. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the habitat, the assay conditions and the screening method for each enzyme.
