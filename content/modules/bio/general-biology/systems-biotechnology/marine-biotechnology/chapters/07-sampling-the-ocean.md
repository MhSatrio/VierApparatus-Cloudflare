Sampling the ocean is expensive and difficult, and the methods used determine which organisms are seen. Trawls, dredges and nets collect larger animals and some microbes from the water column or the seabed, but they damage fragile organisms and miss those that escape the mesh. Grabs and corers sample sediments, and they reveal the organisms that live within them. Each method has a characteristic bias, and a survey that uses one method alone will give an incomplete picture of the community.

Water samples collected in bottles and filtered onto membranes provide the microbes and their DNA, from which the diversity of the microbial community can be estimated. The volume filtered and the size of the filter pores determine which cells are captured, and the samples must be preserved quickly to prevent changes in the community. Mora et al. (2011) noted that much of the diversity of the ocean is found in such microbial samples, which are the most difficult to describe in full.

The deep sea is reached by remotely operated vehicles and autonomous instruments, which can collect samples and make observations at depths that divers cannot reach. These methods are costly, and the number of samples that can be taken is limited, so the design of a deep-sea survey must make the most of each deployment. Autonomous instruments can record over long periods, which helps to capture events that a single visit would miss.

Marine life is patchy, so the number of samples needed to estimate a community is large, and a few samples may give a misleading picture. The location, depth and time of each sample must be recorded, since the same community may differ substantially between places and seasons. Cumming (2014) argues that the variation between samples should be reported with the estimates, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a survey with a few samples may give a result that does not hold for the wider area. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the method, the location and the time of each collection.
