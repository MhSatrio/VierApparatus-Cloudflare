The ocean covers most of the planet and contains a large share of its biological diversity, much of which has never been described. Its organisms have evolved in conditions of cold, salt, pressure and darkness, and they produce chemicals and enzymes that differ from those of land organisms. Marine biotechnology seeks to find these products and to use them in medicine, industry and agriculture, and the field depends on a reliable knowledge of what the ocean contains. Mora et al. (2011) estimated that a large fraction of the species on Earth, and a larger fraction in the ocean, have not yet been named.

The topic begins with the diversity of marine life and the sampling problems that limit its discovery. Marine natural products, which are the secondary metabolites of sponges, corals, algae, tunicates and microbes, have yielded drug leads and industrial compounds, and Blunt et al. (2018) reviewed the compounds reported in recent years. Many of these compounds are produced in small amounts by organisms that are difficult to grow or collect, and the supply of a promising compound is often the first barrier to its development.

The later chapters examine marine microbes and their symbioses, the enzymes that are adapted to the marine environment, the aquaculture of marine species, the sampling of the ocean and the reporting of marine studies. The sustainability of harvesting wild organisms, and the risk of depleting populations and ecosystems, is a central concern that the field must address. Cumming (2014) argues that the frequency and the abundance of species and compounds should be reported with their intervals, since many marine estimates are uncertain.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a claimed discovery that is reported from a few samples may not hold in a wider survey, and marine studies are particularly prone to this because of the cost and the difficulty of collection. Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the collection site, the identity of the organism and the method of extraction so that a marine result can be reproduced and its source traced.
