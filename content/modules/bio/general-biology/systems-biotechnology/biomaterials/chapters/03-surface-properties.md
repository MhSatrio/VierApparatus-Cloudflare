The surface of an implant is the first thing the body encounters. When a material is placed in blood or tissue fluid, proteins adsorb to its surface within seconds, forming a layer that determines what happens next. Williams (2008) described this adsorbed layer as the mediator between the material and the cells, since cells do not attach to the bare material but to the proteins that have adsorbed to it. The identity and the conformation of these proteins are therefore central to the biological response.

Adsorbed proteins change their shape on the surface, and the change can expose or hide the sites that cells recognise. A protein that unfolds on a hydrophobic surface may present a different face to the cells than one that stays folded on a hydrophilic surface. The conformation is difficult to measure directly, and its effect is often inferred from the cell response, which means that a claim about the protein must be supported by independent evidence.

Cell attachment to the adsorbed layer is mediated by integrins, receptors that bind specific sequences in adhesive proteins such as fibronectin. The presence of these sequences, and their accessibility, determines whether the cells attach and spread. A surface that presents the right sequence at the right density supports attachment, and one that presents none may provoke the cells to round up or to die.

Surface chemistry, roughness, wettability and charge influence how much protein adsorbs and what it does. Ioannidis (2005) warned that several of these properties often change together when a surface is modified, so an effect attributed to one may be produced by another, and the properties should be varied independently where possible. Cumming (2014) argues that the surface properties and the cell responses should be reported with their intervals, and the methods of measurement should be stated, since spectroscopy, microscopy and contact angle each measure a different aspect of the surface.
