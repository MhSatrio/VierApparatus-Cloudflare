A biomaterial is defined by its contact with living tissue and by the function it is designed to perform. A material used in a packaging or a building is not a biomaterial, even if it is made of the same substance as one that is, because the context and the function differ. Williams (2008) emphasised that the biological response is part of the definition, since a material is judged by how the body responds to it as well as by its properties in the laboratory.

Biomaterials are grouped into classes by their composition. Metals, such as titanium alloys and stainless steels, are strong and tough, and they are used for load-bearing implants such as joint replacements and fixation plates. Their typical failure modes include corrosion and wear, which release ions and particles into the tissue. Ceramics, such as alumina and calcium phosphates, are hard and chemically stable, and they suit bone contact, though they are brittle and may fracture under tension.

Polymers can be tailored in their chemistry and their degradation, and they are used for sutures, drug delivery systems and soft tissue supports. Their typical limits are their lower strength and their tendency to creep or to degrade in ways that change their properties. Composites combine materials to match a tissue more closely, as a polymer reinforced with ceramic particles can approximate bone, and their failure depends on the interface between the components.

The response that a material provokes classifies it further. Inert materials provoke little response, bioactive materials form a bond with the tissue, and resorbable materials dissolve and are replaced by tissue. Hutmacher (2000) showed that the same composition can fall into different categories depending on its surface and its structure, so the classification must consider the material as implanted. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the composition, the form and the processing of each material, since these determine its response.
