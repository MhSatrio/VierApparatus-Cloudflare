Ceramics are hard, stable and resistant to wear, which makes them suitable for parts of the body that bear load and that must not change. Alumina and zirconia are used in joints for these reasons, and their chemical inertness keeps them from reacting with the tissue. Their weakness is brittleness: a ceramic fails suddenly under tension, with little warning, and this limits the loads that a ceramic component can bear without a supporting structure.

The calcium phosphates are of particular interest for bone. Hydroxyapatite is chemically similar to the mineral of bone, and it forms a direct bond with bone tissue, which is the behaviour called bioactivity. Tricalcium phosphate is resorbed by the body over time and replaced by new bone, which is the behaviour called resorbability. Hutmacher (2000) described how these two behaviours can be combined in a single material, and each has a place in the repair of bone.

Composites combine a ceramic with a polymer, so that the ceramic supplies stiffness and bioactivity and the polymer supplies toughness and resistance to fracture. The result is a material that can approximate the mechanical behaviour of bone more closely than either component alone. The properties depend on the proportion of the phases and on how well they bond to one another.

The interface between the phases of a composite determines its strength, since a weak interface allows cracks to pass between them. The response of the body to the composite depends on the materials exposed at its surface, and a composite whose ceramic is partly exposed may provoke a different response from one that is fully covered. Williams (2008) argued that the response depends on the surface as well as on the bulk, and Ioannidis (2005) described how the apparent strength of a composite can depend on the test that is used, so the interface and the test should both be reported with the mechanical results.
