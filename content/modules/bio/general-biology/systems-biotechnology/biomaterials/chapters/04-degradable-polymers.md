Degradable polymers are used when a device should disappear after it has served its purpose. The polyesters, including polylactide and polyglycolide, are the most common, and they degrade by hydrolysis, in which water breaks the ester bonds of the chain. The breakdown products are small acids that the body metabolises and removes, which makes the material acceptable for many temporary uses. Hutmacher (2000) noted that the rate of degradation must be matched to the rate at which the tissue replaces the device.

The rate of degradation depends on several factors. The crystallinity of the polymer slows the entry of water, since the crystalline regions are more resistant, and the molecular weight determines how many bonds must be broken before the fragments are small enough to dissolve. The water uptake and the local acidity also control the rate, and the acidic products can accelerate the breakdown of the surrounding polymer.

This acceleration is autocatalytic. In a thick device the acidic products are trapped in the interior, where they cannot diffuse out quickly, and the interior degrades faster than the surface. The result is a device that loses its strength from the inside and may fragment abruptly, which is undesirable for a load-bearing function. The thickness and the geometry of the device must be considered in this respect.

The mechanical properties of the device change as its mass is lost, and the change must be measured. The mass loss and the decline in molecular weight should be measured over time under conditions that mimic the body, including the temperature, the water and the presence of enzymes. Cumming (2014) argues that these measurements should be reported with their intervals, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the conditions of the degradation test so that the rate can be reproduced.
