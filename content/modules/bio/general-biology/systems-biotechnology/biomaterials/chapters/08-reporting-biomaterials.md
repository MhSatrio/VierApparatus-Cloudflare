A biomaterial report must allow another group to reproduce the material and to test it in the same way. The composition and the form in which the material was tested should be stated first, since a material tested in powder, film or finished device may behave differently. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and for a biomaterial those means include the composition, the processing and the sterilisation.

Processing and sterilisation change the properties of a material, and they must be reported. A heat treatment can alter the crystallinity of a polymer, and a sterilising radiation can change its molecular weight, so a material that is tested before sterilisation may not behave as the sterilised product does. Williams (2008) emphasised that the response of the body depends on the material as implanted, and the report should describe the material in its final form.

The surface and the biological tests should be reported with their conditions and their intervals. The surface characterisation, the extraction conditions for the biocompatibility tests, the number of samples and the time points of the implantation should all be given. Cumming (2014) argues that each estimate should be shown with its precision, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a test with few samples or favourable conditions may not represent the device.

The degradation, mechanical and infection data should be given with the conditions under which they were measured, and the data and the specifications should be deposited with identifiers. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended keeping the inputs, steps and outputs of an analysis together, so that the performance of the material can be checked and compared with others.
