Biocompatibility is tested in stages, from simple tests in cells to implantation in animals. The first stage exposes cultured cells to extracts of the material or to the material itself and measures their viability and their function. Cytotoxicity tests reveal whether the material releases substances that kill cells, and they are quick and inexpensive, though they cannot represent the complexity of a living tissue. Williams (2008) argued that such tests must be interpreted with the mechanisms by which the body responds, and not treated as a complete account.

Sensitisation and irritation tests examine whether the material provokes an allergic reaction or an inflammatory one. They are performed in animals or in models that predict these responses, and they reveal reactions that cell culture cannot. The results must be read with the dose and the route of exposure, since a material that is harmless in a small amount may provoke a response in a larger one.

Implantation tests place the material in an animal and observe the tissue response over time. The response is classified as acute, if it occurs within days, chronic, if it persists, or granulomatous, if macrophages form a characteristic structure around the material. The time points at which the tissue is examined determine what is seen, and early and late responses may differ.

Extraction conditions must match the body. A material placed in a solvent that dissolves more of it than the body would will release more leachables than it does in vivo, and an extraction under mild conditions may miss a substance that is released only under stress. Cumming (2014) argues that the number of samples and the variation between them should be reported with the results, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a test performed on a few samples may not represent the material. The form of the material and the conditions of extraction should be stated.
