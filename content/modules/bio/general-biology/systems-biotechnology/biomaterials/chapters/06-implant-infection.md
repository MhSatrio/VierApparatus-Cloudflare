Infection is one of the most serious complications of an implanted device, and it is difficult to treat because bacteria on the implant form biofilms. A biofilm is a community of bacteria embedded in a matrix that they secrete, and it resists antibiotics, which cannot penetrate it well, and the immune response, which cannot clear it. Bacteria reach the implant during surgery or later from the blood, and their first step is adhesion to the surface, which is shaped by the protein layer that the body has already deposited.

The adsorbed protein layer mediates the initial adhesion of bacteria, since many bacteria bind to specific proteins such as fibrinogen or fibronectin. The surface that presents these proteins may be colonised more readily, and the properties discussed in the surface chapter therefore influence the risk of infection as well as the response of the tissue. Williams (2008) emphasised that the body and the bacteria respond to the same surface, and its design must consider both.

Several strategies reduce the risk of infection. An antibacterial coating can release a substance that kills bacteria at the surface, an antibiotic can be released from the implant over time, and a surface can be designed to resist attachment, so that bacteria are removed before they settle. Each strategy must be tested, since its effect may last only as long as the coating or the antibiotic remains.

A strategy must be tested against biofilms on the implant, not only against free bacteria in a laboratory culture. A coating that kills bacteria in a flask may fail against a mature biofilm, which is far more resistant. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result obtained in a simple model may not hold in the setting it is meant for, and Cumming (2014) argues that the reduction in bacterial numbers should be reported with its interval. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the bacterial strains, the test conditions and the form of the biofilm used, so that the test can be compared with others.
