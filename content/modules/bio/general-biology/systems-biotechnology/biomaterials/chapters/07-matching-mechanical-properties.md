A biomaterial must bear the loads of its function without failing and without disturbing the tissue around it. The most important mechanical requirement is that the stiffness of the material should be close to that of the tissue it replaces, since a mismatch changes how the load is shared. Hutmacher (2000) described how the mechanical properties of scaffolds for bone must be matched to the bone they support, and the stiffness is one of the central properties of the design.

A stiff implant in bone takes up much of the load that the bone would otherwise carry. The bone, no longer loaded, loses mineral and strength over time, a phenomenon called stress shielding, and the implant may then loosen. Stress shielding is a clear example of a biomaterial changing the tissue it is meant to serve, and it can only be detected by following the tissue over time.

A compliant implant, one that is much softer than the tissue, moves relative to it under load. The movement can provoke a fibrous tissue to form between the implant and the bone, which prevents a firm bond and can lead to failure. The stiffness that is best for the tissue lies between the extremes, and its determination depends on the tissue and the load.

Implants are subject to cyclic loads, and fatigue can cause failure at loads far below the static strength. The fatigue test must reproduce the frequency, the magnitude and the number of cycles expected in use, and the test method changes the result. Cumming (2014) argues that the strength and the fatigue life should be reported with their intervals, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a test that uses favourable conditions may overstate the performance of a device.
