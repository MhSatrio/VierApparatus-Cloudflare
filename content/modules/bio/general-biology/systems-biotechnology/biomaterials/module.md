A biomaterial is any material designed to act in contact with living tissue, whether as a permanent implant, a temporary support or a surface in a diagnostic device. Its job is to perform a mechanical or chemical function while the body responds to it. Williams (2008) described biocompatibility as the ability of a material to perform its intended function without eliciting an undesirable response, and this definition makes the material and the biology inseparable. A biomaterial is judged by what happens at its surface as much as by its bulk properties.

The topic begins with the classes of material. Metals are strong and tough and are used for load-bearing implants, ceramics are hard and stable and suit bone, polymers can be tailored in their chemistry and their degradation, and composites combine these to match a tissue. Each class has characteristic failure modes, and the choice of class follows from the tissue and the function. Hutmacher (2000) reviewed how these classes are used in bone and cartilage.

The surface of a material is the first point of contact with the body. Proteins adsorb to it within seconds of implantation, cells attach to the adsorbed layer, and the response of the tissue follows from the nature of the surface. The chemistry, the roughness and the charge of the surface therefore determine much of the response, and they must be measured and reported. Ioannidis (2005) described how a single surface property may be mistaken for the cause of a response when several are changed together.

The later chapters examine the testing of biocompatibility, the degradation of polymers, the matching of mechanical properties, and the threat of infection. Cumming (2014) argues that the responses should be reported with their intervals, and Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the materials, their preparation and their tests so that the performance can be reproduced.
