Fermentation is the oldest biotechnology. Microorganisms have been grown to make bread, beer, cheese and vinegar for thousands of years, and modern fermentation uses the same principle at a far larger scale and with far greater control. A culture is grown in a vessel, the microorganism converts a feedstock into a product, and the product is recovered. The field draws on the microbiology of the organism, the engineering of the vessel and the chemistry of the product, and each contributes to the success of the process.

The topic begins with the basic stages of a fermentation, from the preparation of the inoculum to the harvest. The inoculum is grown up in stages so that the production vessel receives a large and healthy population, and the quality of the inoculum affects the whole run. Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez (2009) described how the conditions of the vessel must be matched to the requirements of the organism through each stage.

Fermentations are run in batch or continuous modes, and each has its advantages. Batch operation is simple and robust, and each run is isolated from the last, which limits the spread of contamination. Continuous operation gives higher productivity over long periods, but it requires the culture to be held in a steady state, and any instability in the strain or the feed shows itself as a drift in the output. Keasling (2010) noted that industrial microbes are often chosen for their fitness in such processes, and the choice of mode depends on the product and the organism.

The later chapters examine the medium, the contamination control, the formation of product and by-products, the monitoring of the run and the reporting of results. Cumming (2014) argues that the results of each run should be reported with their intervals, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a result from a single run may not reproduce. Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording each fermentation so that it can be reproduced.
