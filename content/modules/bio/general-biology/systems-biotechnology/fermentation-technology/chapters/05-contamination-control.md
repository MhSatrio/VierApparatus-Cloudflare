Contamination is the growth of an unwanted organism in the production vessel. It competes with the production strain for nutrients, it may produce by-products or enzymes that degrade the product, and in some cases it poses a risk to the people who handle the process. Because a production vessel is large and its contents are valuable, a contamination can cost the output of an entire run, and the prevention of contamination is one of the most important parts of the process design.

Sterilisation of the medium and the vessel is usually achieved by heat, applied for a time and at a temperature that kill the microorganisms present. The gas supply is sterilised by filtration through membranes that retain bacteria, and the transfers of the inoculum and the sampling of the culture must be made aseptically. Each step must be verified, since a failed seal, a leaking valve or an incomplete sterilisation can introduce contaminants at any point.

Detection is made by microscopy, by plating samples onto selective media and by molecular methods that identify the organisms present. Early detection allows a run to be stopped before the product is lost, and a monitoring plan should specify how often samples are taken and what is measured. The sensitivity of the detection method limits how early a contaminant can be found, and this should be stated.

Each contamination event should be recorded with its cause and the corrective action taken, so that the source can be removed from the process. Ioannidis (2005) described how a source of error that is not recorded may recur unnoticed, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended keeping the record of each run with its events. Cumming (2014) argues that the frequency of contamination should be reported with its interval across runs, since a single contamination says little about the reliability of the process.
