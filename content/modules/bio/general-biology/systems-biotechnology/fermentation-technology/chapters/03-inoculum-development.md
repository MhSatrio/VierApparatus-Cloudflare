The production fermentation begins with an inoculum, a population of cells grown up from a stored stock. The stock is kept frozen or freeze-dried in a working cell bank, from which a flask is started, and the culture is then expanded through successively larger flasks and seed vessels until it reaches the volume needed to inoculate the production vessel. Each stage must produce a culture that is healthy, pure and in the right physiological state, since the production run inherits its condition from the inoculum.

The quality of an inoculum is defined by several criteria. Its cell density must be sufficient to start the run without a long lag, its viability must be high, its purity must be confirmed by microscopy or plating, and its physiological state must be that which the production run requires, which is often the exponential phase. An inoculum taken from a culture in stationary phase may show a long lag, which lengthens the run and changes its timing.

The age and the growth phase of the inoculum affect the reproducibility of the run. A culture that is transferred at a fixed time after inoculation may be at a different phase on each occasion if its growth varies, and a run started from it will begin in a different state. The inoculum should therefore be transferred at a defined density or phase, not at a defined time alone, and this criterion should be stated.

Each working cell bank and each inoculum should be recorded, so that a variation in a production run can be traced to its source. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the inputs of every analysis, and the inoculum is an input to the fermentation. Ioannidis (2005) described how a variation that is not traced to its cause may be attributed to the wrong factor, and Cumming (2014) argues that the variation between inocula should be reported with its interval.
