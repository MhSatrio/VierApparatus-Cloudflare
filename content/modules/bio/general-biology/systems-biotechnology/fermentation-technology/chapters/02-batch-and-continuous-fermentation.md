A batch fermentation is the simplest mode. The vessel is filled with medium and inoculated, and the culture then passes through a sequence of phases. A lag phase precedes growth, the exponential phase follows with the cells growing at their maximal rate, and the stationary phase begins when a nutrient is exhausted. Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez (2009) noted that the batch is closed to the outside, which makes it robust and easy to control, and it is the reference against which other modes are measured.

A continuous fermentation adds fresh medium and removes culture at the same rate, so that the volume is constant. In the simplest form, the chemostat, the culture reaches a steady state in which the cell density and the concentrations are constant, and the steady state is set by the dilution rate, the ratio of the flow to the volume. Because the state is steady, the productivity can be held for long periods, which is the main advantage of the mode.

Washout occurs when the dilution rate exceeds the maximal growth rate of the organism. The cells are removed from the vessel faster than they can divide, and the culture is lost. Below this rate the chemostat is stable, but close to it the steady state is sensitive to small disturbances, and the process can drift. The operating dilution rate must therefore be chosen with a margin below the washout rate.

Each mode has advantages and disadvantages. Batch is robust and isolated, but its productivity is limited by the time spent filling, emptying and cleaning the vessel. Continuous operation achieves higher productivity, but the steady state is vulnerable to contamination, to the instability of the strain and to changes in the feed. Cumming (2014) argues that the productivity of each mode should be reported with its interval over the run, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a steady state observed in one run may not persist in the next.
