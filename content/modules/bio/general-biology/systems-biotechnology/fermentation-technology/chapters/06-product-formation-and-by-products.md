The product of a fermentation is formed from the carbon that the cell does not use for its own growth, and the way this carbon is distributed determines the yield. Some products are made as the cells grow, and their formation is linked to the growth rate, while others are made when growth slows, and their formation depends on different signals. Keasling (2010) described how the two kinds of formation are managed in production, and the choice of strategy depends on which kind the product follows.

When a substrate is supplied faster than the cells can oxidise it, many organisms switch to overflow metabolism, in which part of the carbon is excreted as an incompletely oxidised by-product. In a bacterium growing on glucose, acetate is the common example, and in a yeast ethanol is formed under similar conditions. The by-product lowers the yield of the desired product and can inhibit growth, so its formation is undesirable in most processes.

The distribution of products also depends on oxygen and the redox state. A limitation of oxygen shifts the metabolism towards reduced products, while abundant oxygen favours complete oxidation to carbon dioxide and biomass. The redox balance of the cell therefore determines which products are formed, and it must be considered when the vessel is designed or the aeration is set.

By-products reduce the yield and complicate the recovery, since they must be separated from the product. The strategies to reduce them include controlling the feed so that the substrate does not accumulate, modifying the pathways that produce the by-product, and choosing a process condition that favours the product. Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez (2009) showed that the oxygen supply is among the conditions that most affect the balance, and Cumming (2014) argues that the yield should be reported with the by-products quantified and their intervals.
