A fermentation is the growth of a microorganism in which it converts a substrate into biomass and into the products it excretes or stores. The term has a precise meaning in microbiology, where it describes energy generation without an external electron acceptor, but in industry it is used more widely for any microbial process carried out in a vessel. The basic quantities are the amount of substrate consumed, the biomass formed and the product made, and the relations among them describe the performance of the organism.

Aerobic fermentation uses oxygen as the terminal electron acceptor, and it yields more energy per unit of substrate than anaerobic fermentation. The high energy yield supports rapid growth and high cell density, and it requires the oxygen supply that the vessel must provide. Anaerobic fermentation yields less energy, and its products are often reduced compounds such as organic acids or alcohols. Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez (2009) showed that the oxygen demand of aerobic processes is the principal constraint on their scale.

Products are classed as primary or secondary metabolites. Primary metabolites, such as amino acids, nucleotides and organic acids, are made during active growth and are essential to the cell's own metabolism, so their formation is linked to growth. Secondary metabolites, such as many antibiotics, are made after growth slows, and their formation is controlled by different regulatory signals. The class of product determines the strategy of the process, and the distinction should be stated in the report.

The physiology of the organism determines what can be made. A strain must have the enzymes for the product, the precursors and the cofactors to supply it, and a tolerance to its accumulation. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the organism, its physiology and the measurements of substrate, biomass and product, since the basic quantities define the performance of the fermentation.
