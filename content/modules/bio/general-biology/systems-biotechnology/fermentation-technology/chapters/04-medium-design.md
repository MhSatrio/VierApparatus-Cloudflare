A culture medium supplies everything the cell builds itself from. The carbon source provides energy and the carbon skeleton of the biomass and the product, and it is usually the largest component by mass. Nitrogen is needed for amino acids and nucleotides, phosphorus for nucleic acids and energy carriers, and sulphur for some amino acids and cofactors. Minerals such as magnesium, potassium and iron act as cofactors and in the maintenance of cell structure, and vitamins are needed by organisms that cannot synthesise them.

The carbon source is chosen by its cost, its conversion to product and the metabolism it supports. A cheap feedstock that the organism converts poorly is less useful than a dearer one that gives a high yield. Keasling (2010) emphasised that the cost of the medium can be a large part of the cost of production, and the choice of carbon source is a central economic decision.

The balance of nutrients determines whether the cells grow or make product. Excess nitrogen favours growth, and its limitation can shift the cells towards product formation, a strategy used for many products. Phosphate and other elements can be limited to control growth in the same way, and the balance should be designed to reach the desired phase of metabolism.

A complex medium, such as yeast extract or corn steep liquor, contains many components whose composition varies between batches, and a process using it is hard to reproduce. A defined medium, in which every component is known and controlled, is preferred for reproducibility, though it may be more expensive. Cumming (2014) argues that the effect of medium variation should be measured and reported with its interval, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a result obtained with an unrecorded component may not repeat. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the exact composition of every medium used.
