The performance of a production process is described by three metrics that answer different questions. Yield is the amount of product made per amount of substrate consumed, and it measures how efficiently the carbon is converted. Titre is the concentration of product at the end of the process, and it determines how much must be separated and purified. Productivity is the rate of product formation per unit volume and time, and it determines how much product a given vessel can make in a given period. Keasling (2010) described these as the measures that decide whether a metabolic process is economic.

The metrics can conflict. A process that consumes substrate slowly may give a high yield but a low productivity, and a process that runs fast may waste substrate into by-products and give a low yield. A high titre is attractive, but it may be reached only by a long process whose productivity is low. The choice of which metric to optimise depends on the process, and a strain that improves one may worsen another.

The three metrics combine to determine the cost of production, since the yield sets the raw material cost, the titre sets the cost of recovery and the productivity sets the capital cost of the vessels. An engineer should therefore report all three together, rather than optimising one in isolation, and the trade-off between them should be evaluated for the process as a whole.

Each metric must be reported under stated conditions, with the time and the volume over which it was measured. A yield measured at one time point may differ from the yield at the end, and a productivity computed over a short interval may be higher than the average over the process. Cumming (2014) argues that each metric should be reported with its interval, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a single favourable measurement can mislead. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the time points, the volumes and the calculation of each metric.
