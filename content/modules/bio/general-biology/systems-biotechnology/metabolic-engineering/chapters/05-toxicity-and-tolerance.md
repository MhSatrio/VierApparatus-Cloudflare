Many valuable products are toxic to the cells that make them. Alcohols and organic acids disturb membranes, and some intermediates inhibit enzymes or denature proteins at concentrations that a production strain can reach. As the product accumulates, growth slows and then stops, and the titre is limited by the toxicity rather than by the efficiency of the pathway. Keasling (2010) noted that tolerance is one of the main obstacles to the commercial use of engineered cells.

Tolerance is measured as the concentration at which growth is reduced by a given amount, and it is a dose-response quantity. The measurement should be made with replicate cultures, since growth varies between cultures, and the concentration at which the effect is observed should be reported with its interval. A host that tolerates a product at one concentration may fail at a slightly higher one, so the measurement must be made in the range the process will reach.

Cells use several mechanisms to tolerate harmful compounds. Efflux pumps remove the compound from the cell, and changes in the membrane reduce its entry or repair its damage. Chaperone proteins protect others from denaturation. Engineering tolerance can use the natural mechanisms by overexpressing pumps, or it can exploit adaptive evolution, in which cultures are grown in increasing concentrations of the product until they adapt.

Toxicity sets a ceiling on the titre, and the ceiling must be measured to guide the design. Ioannidis (2005) described how an apparent improvement in a strain may disappear when it is tested at higher concentrations, so the tolerance should be measured as well as the yield. Cumming (2014) argues that the ceiling should be reported with its interval, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the concentrations, the growth conditions and the adaptive history of each strain.
