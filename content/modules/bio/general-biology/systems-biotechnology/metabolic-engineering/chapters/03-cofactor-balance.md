Metabolism runs on cofactors, molecules that carry electrons and energy from one reaction to another. NADH and NADPH carry reducing power, ATP carries energy, and coenzyme A carries acyl groups, and each is continually regenerated by the host. A heterologous pathway draws on these pools, and if it consumes a cofactor faster than the host can regenerate it, or produces one that the host cannot use, the balance of the pool is disturbed.

The demand of a pathway for each cofactor follows from its stoichiometry. A reduction step consumes NADPH, and an oxidation step produces it, so a pathway that is net reductive places a load on the host's supply of reducing power. Orth et al. (2010) described how the stoichiometry of a network determines these demands, and the calculation for a candidate pathway should be made before it is built.

An imbalance limits the yield, because the pathway cannot run faster than the cofactor it needs can be regenerated. It also limits growth, since the host's own reactions depend on the same pools. A strain that has been pushed to produce a product may grow more slowly because its reducing power has been diverted, and the two effects must be measured together.

Strategies to rebalance the pools include choosing enzymes that use the cofactor the host has in excess, introducing transhydrogenases that interconvert NADH and NADPH, and changing the central metabolism to regenerate the cofactor. Cumming (2014) argues that the effect of each strategy on yield and growth should be reported with its interval, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the cofactor demand calculated for each pathway, so that the balance can be checked against the measurements.
