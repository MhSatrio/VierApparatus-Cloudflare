When a nanoparticle meets a biological fluid, proteins adsorb to its surface within moments, forming a layer called the protein corona. The corona is a dynamic mixture, and its composition depends on the particle surface, on the proteins in the fluid and on how long the particle has been in contact with them. Nel et al. (2009) argued that the corona gives the particle its biological identity, since cells and the immune system respond to the corona rather than to the bare particle.

The corona changes how a particle is recognised. Immune cells may bind proteins of the corona that mark a particle for clearance, and a corona that presents such proteins makes the particle circulate for a shorter time. A particle that adsorbs few opsonising proteins may circulate longer. The composition of the corona is therefore a key determinant of the fate of the particle in the body, and it must be characterised in the fluid of use.

Particles enter cells mainly by endocytosis, in which the cell membrane engulfs the particle, and the route and the rate depend on the size, the shape, the charge and the corona. Positively charged particles tend to be taken up more readily than negatively charged ones, and particles of a size near that of a virus may be taken up efficiently. The uptake determines both the delivery and the toxicity of the particle, and the factors should be measured together.

Clearance from the body occurs mainly through the liver, the spleen and the kidney, and the size and the surface determine which organ takes up the particle. Williams (2008) emphasised that the response of the body depends on the surface that the tissue encounters, and this is the basis of the design of particles for a chosen route. Cumming (2014) argues that the uptake and clearance should be reported with their intervals across animals or cell preparations, and Ioannidis (2005) described how the choice of cell type can change the apparent uptake of a particle.
