A nanoparticle carrier can deliver a drug to a site that the free drug would not reach, and it can reduce the dose that affects healthy tissue. The drug is encapsulated within the particle or attached to its surface, and the particle carries it through the circulation. Nel et al. (2009) described how the interaction of the particle with the protein corona and the cells determines whether it reaches its target, and drug delivery is one of the applications in which this interaction is most directly exploited.

Passive targeting relies on the physical properties of the particle and the tissue. Tumours have leaky blood vessels, so particles of a suitable size can accumulate in a tumour more than in healthy tissue, and particles that avoid clearance by the immune system circulate long enough to reach it. Active targeting adds a ligand to the surface that binds a receptor on the target cell, which may increase uptake in the desired cells, though the ligand may also alter the corona.

Controlled release allows the drug to be liberated in response to a trigger, such as a change in pH, a rise in temperature or the activity of an enzyme. A carrier that releases its drug in the acidic environment of a tumour or an endosome is designed for this purpose. The release profile must be measured under conditions that mimic the target, since release in buffer may differ from release in the body.

Drug loading and release must be measured, and they must be reported with the efficacy and the safety of the carrier. A carrier that improves delivery may also change the toxicity of the drug, and a carrier that is safe in itself may not deliver enough drug to be effective. Cumming (2014) argues that the loading, the release and the response should be reported with their intervals, and Ioannidis (2005) described how the apparent benefit of a carrier may be overstated when the comparison is made with a poor control. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the formulation, the release conditions and the comparator used.
