The nanoscale spans from about one nanometre to a few hundred nanometres, and it overlaps the sizes of many biological objects. A protein is a few nanometres across, a virus is tens of nanometres, and the pores of a nuclear membrane are about the size of the smallest nanoparticles used in medicine. A particle in this range can therefore interact with the molecules and structures of the cell in ways that a micrometre-sized object cannot, and this overlap is the reason that nanobiotechnology is a distinct field. Nel et al. (2009) placed the interactions of nanoparticles with biology at the centre of their analysis.

As a particle shrinks, its surface area grows in proportion to its volume, so a given mass of material presents a far larger surface. The surface is where the particle reacts and where biomolecules adsorb, and a smaller particle is therefore more reactive and more strongly coated by proteins than a larger one of the same composition. Size affects the biology through its direct geometry and also through the surface it exposes.

At the smallest sizes, quantum and optical effects appear, changing the colour, the fluorescence and the electronic behaviour of a particle. These effects depend sharply on size, so that a small change in diameter alters the properties of the particle. The use of such particles as labels or sensors depends on this sensitivity, and it also means that small differences in preparation can produce large differences in behaviour.

A nanoparticle is not described by a single diameter, because a preparation contains particles of many sizes. The size distribution, with its mean, its spread and the method by which it was measured, must be reported, since a preparation with a few large particles may behave very differently from one of uniform size with the same mean. Cumming (2014) argues that the distribution should be reported with its interval, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the method and the instrument used to measure size.
