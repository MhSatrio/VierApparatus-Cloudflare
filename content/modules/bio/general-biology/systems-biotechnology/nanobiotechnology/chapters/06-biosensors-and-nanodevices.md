A nanoscale biosensor detects a biological molecule by binding it and converting the binding into a signal. The conversion, or transduction, may be optical, as when gold nanoparticles change colour on aggregation, or fluorescent, as with quantum dots that emit light at a wavelength set by their size, or electrical, as when a nanowire changes its conductance when a charged molecule binds to it. Each transduction has its own sensitivity and its own susceptibility to interference, and the choice follows from the application.

Specificity comes from the recognition element, which is the part of the sensor that binds the target. Antibodies, aptamers and enzymes are the usual choices, and each binds its target with a characteristic selectivity. The recognition element must be attached to the nanostructure without losing its activity, and the density and orientation of the attachment affect the signal.

The limit of detection is the smallest concentration that the sensor reports reliably, and it is set by the ratio of the signal to the noise. A sensor that gives a large signal for the target but also a large background will have a poor limit, and the noise must be measured in the matrix of use. Cumming (2014) argues that the limit of detection should be reported with its interval, since it is estimated from repeated measurements near the threshold.

Complex samples, such as blood or food, contain many molecules that can bind the recognition element or alter the signal, and this interference reduces the accuracy of the sensor. The performance must therefore be tested in the real sample, and compared with a conventional assay. Ioannidis (2005) described how a sensor validated only in clean buffer may fail in practice, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the calibration, the matrix and the comparison assay used.
