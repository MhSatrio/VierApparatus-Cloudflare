Nanoparticles can be toxic, and their toxicity is a central concern for any application in medicine or consumer products. The mechanisms include oxidative stress, in which particles generate reactive oxygen species at their surface or through their interaction with cells, and the damage to membranes and proteins that follows. Inflammation is a further response, in which cells release signals that recruit immune cells, and chronic inflammation can cause lasting harm. Nel et al. (2009) described these mechanisms as products of the interaction at the nano-bio interface.

Toxicity depends on the properties of the particle. Its size, its composition, its surface chemistry and its tendency to dissolve all influence the response, and a particle that releases toxic ions as it dissolves may be more harmful than an insoluble one of the same composition. The dose is also critical, and the response may be non-linear, with a threshold below which no effect is seen.

The dose must be reported in several ways. Mass is the usual measure, but a given mass of small particles has a far larger number and a far larger surface area than the same mass of large ones, and the surface area often correlates best with the response. A report that gives only the mass cannot be compared with one that uses number or surface, and the same dose may appear large or small depending on the measure.

The tests of toxicity in cells and in animals each have limits. Cell cultures are simple and cheap, but they lack the clearance and the immune response of the body, and the particle may behave differently in the culture medium. Animal tests reflect the body more fully, but they are expensive and the results may not transfer to people. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result from a simple model may not hold in the setting it is meant for, and Cumming (2014) argues that the toxic responses should be reported with their intervals. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the dose measures, the particle properties and the exposure conditions.
