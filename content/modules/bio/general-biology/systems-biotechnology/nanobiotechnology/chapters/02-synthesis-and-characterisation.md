Nanoparticles are made either by building them from molecules, in bottom-up methods such as precipitation and chemical reduction, or by breaking down larger materials, in top-down methods such as milling and lithography. Bottom-up methods give good control of size and shape in solution, while top-down methods can produce particles with precise patterns but are often less uniform. The choice of method sets the size distribution and the purity of the particles, and the report should state it.

The surface of the particle is usually modified by a coating, a layer of a polymer or a ligand that prevents aggregation, controls the charge and presents a chosen chemistry to the biology. The coating determines how the particle is stabilised in solution and how it interacts with proteins and cells, and its density and its attachment must be measured. Nel et al. (2009) emphasised that the coating is often more important than the core in determining the biological behaviour.

Characterisation measures the size, the shape, the surface charge and the surface chemistry of the final particles. Size is measured by light scattering, electron microscopy or other methods, each of which measures a different quantity, and the methods must be compared before a size is reported. Surface charge is measured as the zeta potential, which depends on the medium and the concentration of ions.

A particle that is well characterised in pure water may change its size and charge in a biological fluid, since proteins adsorb and ions alter the surface. The characterisation must therefore be repeated in the medium of use, and its results reported with the medium. Cumming (2014) argues that the characterisation should be reported with its variation across batches, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a result from a single batch may not hold for the next. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the synthesis, the coating and the characterisation for each batch.
