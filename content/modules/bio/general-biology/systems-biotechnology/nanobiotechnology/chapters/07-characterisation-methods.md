No single method describes a nanoparticle preparation fully. Dynamic light scattering measures the hydrodynamic size, the diameter of a sphere that diffuses at the same rate as the particle, and it is quick and sensitive to the presence of a few large particles, which can dominate its signal. Electron microscopy measures the size and shape of individual particles in the dry state, and it reveals the distribution directly but requires preparation that can change the particles.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis follows the Brownian motion of individual particles and counts them, giving a number concentration as well as a size distribution. Zeta potential measures the surface charge through the electrophoretic mobility of the particle, and its value depends on the medium and the ionic strength. Spectroscopy and chromatography measure the surface chemistry and the composition, and they reveal the coating and any contaminants.

The methods give different sizes for the same preparation, and the differences are informative. A particle that is much larger in suspension than in the dry state may be aggregated or surrounded by a hydrated layer or a protein corona, and the difference shows the state of the particle in its medium. Nel et al. (2009) emphasised that the size in the medium of use is the one that matters for the biology.

An adequate description requires several methods, reported together, with their conditions. Cumming (2014) argues that each measurement should be reported with its interval, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a single method may give a misleading impression of a preparation. Peng (2011) argued that a measurement is reproducible only with its procedure, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the instrument, the medium and the settings used for each method.
