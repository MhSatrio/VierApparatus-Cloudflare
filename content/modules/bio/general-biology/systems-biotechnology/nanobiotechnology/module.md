Nanobiotechnology applies materials and devices with dimensions of a few to a few hundred nanometres to biology and medicine. The scale is that of proteins, viruses and many cellular structures, and a nanoparticle can enter cells, cross barriers and interact with biomolecules in ways that larger objects cannot. Nel et al. (2009) described how the interactions at this nano-bio interface depend on the size, the shape, the surface and the composition of the particle together, and this interdependence is the central difficulty of the field.

The topic begins with the synthesis and characterisation of nanoparticles. Their size, shape, surface charge and coating must be measured, and the measurements must be made in the conditions of use, since a particle that is stable in water may aggregate in a biological fluid. The characterisation is therefore a prerequisite for any biological result, and the chapters that follow build on it.

When a nanoparticle enters the body, proteins adsorb to its surface and form a layer called the protein corona. The corona determines how the particle is recognised by cells and how long it circulates. The same particle may behave differently in different fluids, and the corona must be characterised in the biological fluid of interest. Williams (2008) emphasised that the response depends on the surface that the cells actually meet.

The later chapters examine toxicity, drug delivery, biosensors and the reporting of nanobiotechnology. Ioannidis (2005) described how the early enthusiasm for a new class of material can produce findings that do not reproduce, and Cumming (2014) argues that the size and the response of particles should be reported with their intervals. Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the particle properties and the conditions so that the studies can be reproduced.
