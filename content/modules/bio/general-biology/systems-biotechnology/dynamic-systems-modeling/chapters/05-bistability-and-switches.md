A bistable system has two stable steady states, and the system can remain in either. Positive feedback is the usual source: a product that promotes its own production, with enough cooperativity, creates a low and a high stable state separated by an unstable threshold. Below the threshold the system returns to the low state, and above it to the high state. Such systems are the basis of cellular switches, in which a cell commits to one of two fates.

Hysteresis is the defining feature of a bistable switch. The threshold at which the system switches on when the input rises is different from the threshold at which it switches off when the input falls, so the state depends on the history of the input and not only on its current value. A graded response, by contrast, returns along the same curve in both directions. Hysteresis is therefore a test that distinguishes a switch from a dimmer, and it requires that the input be varied both up and down.

Gardner, Cantor and Collins (2000) built a genetic toggle switch in which two repressors inhibit each other, which produces two stable states in which one repressor is high and the other low. The switch could be flipped by an external signal, and it remained in the new state after the signal was removed. The construction showed that bistability can be engineered, and the toggle is now a standard element of synthetic biology.

A switch should be characterised by its thresholds and its hysteresis, not by a single on or off measurement. Ioannidis (2005) described how a single striking result can fail to replicate, and the bistability of a switch should be shown by varying the input and measuring the state across the range. Cumming (2014) argues that the thresholds should be reported with their intervals, since the switching point is an estimate that depends on the measurements.
