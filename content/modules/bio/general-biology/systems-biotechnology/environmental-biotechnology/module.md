Environmental biotechnology uses living organisms to address the contamination of soil, water and air. Many pollutants are transformed or destroyed by microorganisms, which have evolved enzymes that attack a wide range of compounds, and these capabilities can be harnessed, enhanced or monitored. Lovley (2003) argued that the genomics of the organisms that degrade pollutants could make bioremediation more predictable, since the genes and pathways responsible can be identified and followed in the field.

The topic begins with the principles of bioremediation, in which the activity of microorganisms is stimulated or supplemented to clean up a site. Bioremediation can be carried out in place, by adding nutrients or oxygen to the contaminated site, or ex situ, by excavating and treating the material. Each approach has its costs and its time scales, and the choice depends on the pollutant and the site.

The microbial pathways that degrade pollutants are the basis of the process, and the chapters that follow examine the genomics of the degraders, the sensors that detect pollutants, the plants that take up contaminants and the treatment of wastewater. Each of these depends on understanding which organisms are present and what they do, and on measuring whether the activity achieves the cleanup.

Engineered organisms may be proposed for bioremediation, and their release into the environment raises questions of containment and of ecological effects. The effectiveness of any treatment must be measured in the field, since laboratory degradation rates do not transfer directly to soil or water. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result obtained in controlled conditions may not hold in the environment, and Cumming (2014) argues that the degradation rates should be reported with their intervals. Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the site, the organisms and the conditions so that the results can be repeated.
