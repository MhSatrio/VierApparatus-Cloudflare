A whole-cell biosensor is a bacterium carrying a gene for a reporter, such as a fluorescent protein, under the control of a promoter that responds to a pollutant. When the pollutant is present, the regulator binds it, the promoter is switched on, and the reporter is produced. The specificity of the sensor comes from the regulator, which recognises a particular compound or class of compounds, and the signal is read by a fluorometer or a plate reader. The approach allows the bioavailable fraction of a pollutant to be measured, since only the compound that reaches the cell can activate the sensor.

Enzyme-based sensors respond to a pollutant by a change in the activity of an enzyme, which is measured electrochemically or optically. They can be more rapid than whole cells but are less robust, since the enzyme must be immobilised and kept active. Each type of sensor responds to a different property of the pollutant, and the choice depends on the question the measurement must answer.

The matrix of the sample alters the response. Soil particles and dissolved organic matter can bind the pollutant and reduce the amount available to the cell, and salts, pH and temperature can change the activity of the cell. A sensor calibrated in clean buffer will therefore report a different concentration from one in soil, and the calibration must be made in the matrix of use. Cumming (2014) argues that the response should be reported with its interval across matrices.

The limit of detection and the response time determine whether a sensor is useful in the field. A sensor that detects the pollutant only at high concentrations may miss a hazard, and one that responds slowly may not track a changing site. The performance should be compared with a standard chemical analysis, and the agreement reported with its interval. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the reporter, the regulator, the calibration and the matrix so that the sensor can be reproduced and its readings interpreted.
