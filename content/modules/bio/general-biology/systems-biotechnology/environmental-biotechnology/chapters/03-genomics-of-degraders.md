Genomics has changed the study of bioremediation by making it possible to identify the organisms that degrade a pollutant without culturing them. Lovley (2003) described how the genes for degradation can be sought in the genomes of isolated strains, and how the sequence of an isolate reveals the pathway it carries. The approach turns the search for degraders from a matter of chance culture into a systematic reading of the genes present.

Metagenomics sequences the DNA of an entire community at a contaminated site, and it reveals the organisms present and their catabolic genes, including those of organisms that have never been grown in the laboratory. The analysis requires assembly and binning of the sequences, and the results depend on the depth of sequencing and the completeness of the reference databases. The community profile should be reported with the method, since different pipelines can give different organisms.

Transcriptomics and proteomics show which genes and proteins are active during degradation. A gene may be present in the community but silent under the conditions of the site, and the expression data show which pathways are operating. Measurements of expression are closer to activity than the presence of genes, and they should be used to support claims about degradation.

Gene markers quantify the degraders in the field by measuring the abundance of a characteristic gene, usually by quantitative PCR, and these markers can track a bioremediation through time. Mobile genetic elements such as plasmids and transposons spread degradation genes between species, and this transfer can increase the degradation capacity of a community. Ioannidis (2005) described how a marker that is chosen after the results are known may fit the data without describing the process, and Cumming (2014) argues that the abundance should be reported with its interval. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the sequencing, the analysis pipeline and the reference databases used.
