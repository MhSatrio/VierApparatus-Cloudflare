Wastewater treatment is one of the largest applications of environmental biotechnology, and it relies on microbial communities to remove the organic matter, nitrogen and phosphorus that would otherwise damage rivers and lakes. The activated sludge process, the most common, aerates a tank of wastewater in which bacteria consume the organic matter and form flocs, and the flocs are then settled in a clarifier, with part of the sludge returned to the tank to maintain the community. Lovley (2003) noted that the community that carries out this treatment is itself a target for genomic analysis.

The sludge age, the average time that the biomass remains in the system, is the main control on the community. A long sludge age favours slow-growing organisms such as nitrifiers, which remove ammonia, while a short sludge age favours fast-growing organisms that remove organic matter. The sludge age must be chosen to give the removal required, and it should be reported with the performance.

Nitrogen is removed in two steps. Nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonia to nitrate under aerobic conditions, and denitrifying bacteria reduce the nitrate to nitrogen gas under anoxic conditions. Each step requires a distinct condition, and a plant must provide both the aerobic and the anoxic zones for the removal to be complete. Phosphorus is removed by the uptake of phosphate into the biomass, which is then removed with the sludge, and the process can be enhanced by a suitable sequence of conditions.

The process can be disrupted by bulking, in which filamentous organisms prevent the flocs from settling, and by foaming, in which surface-active organisms produce a stable foam. Both reduce the performance of the clarifier and may release the biomass in the effluent. The effluent must meet regulatory limits, and its measurement has an uncertainty that should be stated. Ioannidis (2005) described how a single sample may not represent the effluent over time, and Cumming (2014) argues that the effluent quality should be reported with its interval. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the operating conditions and the sampling for each measurement.
