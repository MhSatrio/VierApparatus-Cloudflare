Microorganisms degrade pollutants through pathways, which are sequences of enzymatic reactions that convert a compound step by step into smaller molecules. Aromatic compounds, such as those in fuels and industrial solvents, are attacked by oxygenases in the presence of oxygen, which add oxygen to the ring and open it so that it can enter central metabolism. Lovley (2003) described how the genes for these enzymes are often clustered, so that a degrading organism carries the whole pathway in a single region of its genome.

Under anaerobic conditions, different pathways operate. Chlorinated solvents can be reduced by microorganisms that use the compound as an electron acceptor, removing chlorine atoms one at a time, and this is the basis of some of the most effective treatments for groundwater. The organisms that carry out these reductions are often specialised, and their survival depends on the presence of the right electron donors.

Many different pollutants are funnelled into a few central intermediates, such as catechol or protocatechuate, which are then processed by common pathways. This convergence means that organisms with a common set of enzymes can degrade many compounds, and it also means that a bottleneck in one enzyme may limit the degradation of several pollutants at once.

Degradation is not always complete. A pathway may stop at an intermediate that is more toxic or more persistent than the parent compound, or it may transform the pollutant without mineralising it to carbon dioxide and water. The end products must therefore be identified, since a claim of degradation that counts only the disappearance of the parent compound may overstate the benefit. Cumming (2014) argues that the products should be quantified with their intervals, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the pathway and the measurements used to follow it.
