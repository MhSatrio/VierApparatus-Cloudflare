Bioremediation relies on microorganisms to remove or immobilise pollutants. Three approaches are common. Natural attenuation relies on the organisms already present, which degrade the pollutant over time without intervention, and it is the least costly but also the slowest. Biostimulation adds nutrients or an electron acceptor, such as oxygen or nitrate, to encourage the native organisms to degrade the pollutant. Bioaugmentation introduces organisms with a known capacity for the pollutant, and it is used when the native population cannot degrade it. Lovley (2003) described how the choice among these depends on what the site contains and what the pollutant needs.

The activity of microorganisms is limited by several factors. Oxygen is needed by many degraders and is often scarce in saturated soils, nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus may be missing, and temperature and moisture control the rate of metabolism. The pollutant itself may be unavailable to the organisms if it is strongly bound to soil particles, and bioavailability is then the limiting factor. A treatment must address the limit that actually applies at the site.

The time scale of treatment ranges from months to decades, depending on the pollutant, the site and the conditions. A treatment that appears to work in a short trial may slow as the easily degraded fraction is exhausted, leaving the more recalcitrant fraction, and the performance must be followed over the full period. The expected time scale should be stated when the treatment is planned.

A fall in pollutant concentration does not by itself show that microbes have degraded it. The concentration may fall because the pollutant is diluted by water, adsorbed to soil particles, or lost by volatilisation, and the biological contribution must be shown by additional evidence, such as the presence of degradation products or the isotopic signature of microbial breakdown. Cumming (2014) argues that the rate of loss should be reported with its interval, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a decline in one measure may not mean what it seems. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the site conditions and the evidence used to attribute the loss to biology.
