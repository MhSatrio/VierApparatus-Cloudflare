Releasing a microorganism into the environment is a different act from using it in a sealed vessel. An organism that is released may persist, multiply and spread beyond the site, and it may exchange genes with native organisms through mobile genetic elements. Lovley (2003) noted that the genes responsible for degradation can move between species, and a released organism may transfer its degradation genes to organisms that were never intended to carry them.

The effects of a released organism on native communities must be assessed. It may compete with native species, alter the microbial processes of the soil or water, or be a food for predators that then affect the rest of the food web. These effects are difficult to predict, since the interactions among species are complex, and the assessment must consider the community as a whole and not only the target species.

Containment strategies aim to restrict the released organism to the treatment site. Self-limiting designs, in which the organism requires a nutrient or a condition found only at the site, reduce the risk of survival outside it. Such designs can fail through mutation or through the availability of the required nutrient elsewhere, and their performance must be tested over many generations.

The detection of a released organism after release is essential, since the response to a problem depends on knowing where the organism is. Detection by genetic markers in the soil or water can show whether the organism persists and whether its genes have spread. Ioannidis (2005) described how a risk that is judged from a short study may not be the same over the long term, and Cumming (2014) argues that the detection limits and the rates of spread should be reported with their intervals. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the release, the monitoring and the containment design for each site.
