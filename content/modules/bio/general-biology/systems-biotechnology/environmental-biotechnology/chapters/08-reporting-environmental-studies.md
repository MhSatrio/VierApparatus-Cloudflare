An environmental study can be reproduced and compared with others only if the site and the methods are described in full. The site, its contamination history and its geology should be given first, since the same treatment behaves differently in different soils and waters. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and for an environmental study those means include the site description, the sampling and the analysis.

The organisms should be identified, with their genetic evidence, and the conditions of the site, such as oxygen, temperature, pH, moisture and the concentrations of nutrients, should be measured and reported. Lovley (2003) showed that the identity of the degrading organisms can be determined by genomics, and the report should state the method used to identify them. The pollutant concentrations should be given with the method of analysis and its detection limit.

The attribution of a loss to biology must be shown, and the evidence used should be stated, whether it is the appearance of degradation products, the expression of degradation genes or an isotopic signature. Where a plant or sludge is involved, the mass balance should be reported, including the disposal of the material. Cumming (2014) argues that each rate and each removal should be reported with its interval, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a decline in a single measure may not reflect what the study claims.

The monitoring of any release, and the containment used, should be reported, along with the deposition of the site data and the sequences with identifiers. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended keeping the inputs, steps and outputs of an analysis together, so that the study can be checked and compared with others.
