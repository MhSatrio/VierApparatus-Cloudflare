Plants can take up, concentrate and transform contaminants, and phytoremediation uses this ability to clean soil and water. Phytoextraction takes up metals from the soil into the shoots, which are then harvested and disposed of, and it works best for metals that the plant can accumulate without being killed. Phytodegradation uses enzymes produced by the plant to break down organic pollutants, and rhizoremediation relies on the microbes that live in the root zone, which degrade contaminants that the roots release their own enzymes to attack.

The choice of plant species matters. A species must tolerate the contaminant, grow fast enough to remove a useful amount, and produce enough biomass to carry the contaminant away. Hyperaccumulator species take up metals at high concentrations, but they are often slow-growing, and fast-growing species may remove more in total. Lovley (2003) noted that the microbes associated with plant roots can be as important as the plant itself, and the species should be chosen with both in mind.

The contaminant removed by a crop does not vanish. If the harvested plant material is burned or decomposes, the contaminant may return to the air or to the soil, and the disposal of the biomass must be included in the assessment. A mass balance that accounts for the contaminant in the soil before and after treatment, in the plant material and in its disposal is the only complete measure of removal.

Removal must be measured over the seasons of growth, since uptake depends on the stage of the plant and the weather. Cumming (2014) argues that the removal should be reported with its interval across plants and seasons, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a removal measured in one season may not hold for the next. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the species, the soil, the sampling and the mass balance used to calculate the removal.
