Genome engineering changes the DNA of a living cell at chosen positions. The modern methods use programmable nucleases, enzymes that cut DNA at a site specified by a short guide sequence. Jinek et al. (2012) showed that the bacterial CRISPR system could be directed to cut DNA at a site chosen by the experimenter, and this discovery made precise editing of many organisms practical. The cut is the starting point of an edit, since the outcome depends on how the cell repairs it.

The topic begins with the nuclease and its guide, and with the ways in which several sites can be edited at once. Cong et al. (2013) and Mali et al. (2013) showed that the system could be used in eukaryotic cells, including human cells, and that several guides could be used together. Doudna and Charpentier (2014) reviewed how the system became a general tool, and the chapters that follow examine the design, the delivery, the specificity and the verification of edits.

Each edit has risks. The guide may cut sites other than the intended one, the delivery may fail or be incomplete, and the repair of the cut may produce unintended changes. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result obtained with one method may not hold in another, and an edit must be checked by sequencing rather than assumed from the design. Cumming (2014) argues that the efficiency and the specificity of an edit should be reported with their uncertainty.

Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the guide sequences, the reagents and the verification, and Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it. The final chapter of the topic applies these principles to the reporting of genome edits, so that each change can be attributed to its cause and repeated by others.
