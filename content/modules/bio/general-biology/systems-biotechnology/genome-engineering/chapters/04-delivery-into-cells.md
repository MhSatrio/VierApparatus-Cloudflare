Editing components must reach the nucleus of the target cell, and the method of delivery determines how many cells receive them and for how long. Plasmid transfection is simple and cheap, but the plasmid may persist and continue to produce the nuclease for days. Viral vectors deliver the components efficiently into many cell types, and they carry their own constraints on size and on the immune response. Mali et al. (2013) showed that the system could be delivered into human cells, and the choice of method was central to their experiments.

Delivering the nuclease as a protein complexed with its guide RNA gives a short period of activity, since the complex is degraded within hours. This shortens the window in which off-target cuts can occur, and it reduces the chance that the cell retains the editing machinery. Physical methods such as electroporation and microinjection deliver the components directly, with efficiencies that depend on the cell type and on the conditions of the procedure.

The efficiency of delivery sets a limit on the fraction of cells that can be edited. If only a small fraction of cells receive the components, most of the population remains unedited, and the proportion of edited cells is bounded by the proportion that were transfected. The efficiency must be measured, usually with a reporter that marks the cells that received the components, and it should be reported with the method.

Selection markers can be used to enrich the cells that received the components, which increases the fraction of edited cells among those studied. The selection itself may affect the cells, and an edit that is enriched by a marker may be the result of the marker rather than of the edit. Cumming (2014) argues that the proportion of cells receiving the components should be reported with its interval, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the method, the reagent concentrations and the procedure, so that the delivery can be repeated.
