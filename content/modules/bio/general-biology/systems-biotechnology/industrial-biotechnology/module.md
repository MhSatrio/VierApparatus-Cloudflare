Industrial biotechnology uses living cells and their enzymes to manufacture products on a large scale. Keasling (2010) argued that microbes can be engineered to make many molecules that were once obtained from petrochemicals or from scarce natural sources, and the field now supplies chemicals, materials, fuels and food ingredients. Its central question is economic: a biological route must compete on cost, quality and reliability with the established alternatives, and the science must serve that competition.

The topic begins with the choice of host and feedstock. The host determines what can be made and how it can be grown, and the feedstock determines the raw material cost, which is often the largest component of the price of the product. Keasling (2012) described how the tools of synthetic biology made it possible to choose a host for its properties rather than for its convenience, and the feedstock must be chosen with the same care.

The economics of a biological product depend on the yield, the titre, the productivity and the cost of recovery, which are the metrics of the production process. A product that is cheap to make in the cell may be expensive to purify, and the cost of separation can dominate the total. The chapters that follow examine the trade-offs between the routes and the assessment of their sustainability.

Industrial processes are subject to regulation and to the requirements of biosafety, which constrain the organisms that may be used and the conditions under which they may be grown. Ioannidis (2005) described how a claim of advantage that is not checked against the full process may not survive, and Cumming (2014) argues that the economic and environmental claims should be reported with their uncertainties. Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the data and the assumptions behind each comparison so that it can be reproduced.
