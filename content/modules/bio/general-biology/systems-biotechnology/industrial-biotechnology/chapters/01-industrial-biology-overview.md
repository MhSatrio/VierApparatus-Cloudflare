Industrial biology uses cells as manufacturing units. A production organism, usually a bacterium, yeast or filamentous fungus, is grown in a large vessel on a defined feedstock, and it converts the feedstock into the product, which is then recovered and purified. Keasling (2010) described how the same principles of metabolic engineering are applied at each stage, from the design of the organism to the operation of the vessel. The process is a chain, and a weakness in any link limits the whole.

The stages are usually the preparation of the strain and its inoculum, the fermentation in which the product is formed, and the recovery in which the product is separated from the cells and the medium. Each stage has its own costs and failure modes. A strain that is unstable over many generations will lose its product during a long fermentation, and a fermentation that is not controlled will produce a variable product.

Some transformations are carried out by isolated enzymes rather than by whole cells. These biocatalytic processes use the enzyme alone, which simplifies the process and often increases its selectivity, although the enzyme must be produced and purified. The choice between a whole cell and an isolated enzyme is one of the first decisions in a process.

Industrial products must meet specifications for purity, consistency and safety, which a laboratory result does not guarantee. A process must produce the same product from batch to batch, and its variability must be measured and controlled. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the conditions and the measurements for every batch, since consistency can be demonstrated only with a record of what was done, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a result from one setting may not hold in another.
