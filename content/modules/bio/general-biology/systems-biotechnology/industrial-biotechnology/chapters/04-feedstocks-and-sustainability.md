A biological process begins with its feedstock, and the choice of feedstock governs its cost, its conversion and its environmental footprint. Sugars from food crops are readily used by many hosts but compete with food production for land and water. Lignocellulosic biomass, from agricultural and forestry residues, avoids this competition but must be broken down before it can be used, which adds cost and complexity. Waste streams and gaseous carbon sources offer further options, each with its own difficulties of consistency and conversion.

Keasling (2012) argued that the choice of feedstock should be matched to the host and the product, since a host that cannot use a feedstock efficiently will convert it poorly whatever its other merits. The efficiency of conversion is measured by the yield, and a feedstock that gives a high yield for the product of interest is preferred even if its price is higher.

Sustainability claims are often made for biological products, and they must be supported by evidence. The claim depends on the boundary of the accounting, the assumptions about land use and the energy used in the process, and different boundaries can give opposite conclusions. A product that appears low in emissions when the feedstock is counted as zero may appear high when the land it occupies is included.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a claim that rests on favourable assumptions may not survive a fuller analysis, and Cumming (2014) argues that sustainability estimates should be reported with their uncertainties. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the accounting boundary, the data sources and the assumptions, so that a sustainability claim can be checked and compared with others.
