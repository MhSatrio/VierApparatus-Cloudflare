A transformation can be carried out by an isolated enzyme, in a biocatalytic reaction, or by a living cell, in a fermentation. The isolated enzyme gives a cleaner reaction with fewer by-products, since the cell's other metabolism is absent, and it can be run under conditions that suit the enzyme rather than the cell. Bornscheuer et al. (2012) described the advantages of biocatalysis for selective reactions, and the choice between the two routes is often decided by these advantages.

The fermentation has its own strengths. The cell supplies the enzyme and its cofactors, so no enzyme needs to be purified, and the cell can carry out a multistep pathway in one vessel. Keasling (2010) described how the pathways of a whole cell can produce molecules that would be difficult to make with isolated enzymes, since the cofactors and intermediates are regenerated by the cell itself.

The cost of the enzyme is a major consideration for biocatalysis. An enzyme must be produced and purified, and its cost is spread over the number of times it can be used, so the stability and reusability of the enzyme determine the economics. The cofactor requirement is the reverse issue: an isolated enzyme that needs a cofactor requires a regeneration system, while the cell regenerates its cofactors automatically.

Whole cells have limits of mass transfer, since the substrate must reach the cell and the product must leave it, and by-products are formed by the cell's own metabolism. The choice of route depends on the transformation, and a comparison should measure selectivity, conversion, cost and recovery for both. Ioannidis (2005) described how a comparison that favours one route may not hold when all costs are included, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the data for each route with the conditions of the comparison.
