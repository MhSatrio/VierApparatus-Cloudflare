A life-cycle assessment accounts for the environmental impacts of a product across its whole life, from the production of its feedstock through its manufacture and use to its disposal. It is the systematic way of testing a sustainability claim, since it forces the accounting to cover the stages that a claim might leave out. The assessment is only as sound as its definition, and the definition determines the answer.

The functional unit defines what is being compared, such as a fixed mass of product or a fixed service it delivers. Two products can be compared only if they are compared on the same functional unit, since a product that is used in smaller quantities may appear to have lower impacts simply because less of it is needed. The functional unit must be stated first, and it governs every later calculation.

The inventory records the material and energy flows of each stage, and the impacts are calculated from these flows using factors for each emission and resource use. The inventory is the most data-intensive part of the assessment, and the sources of its data should be stated, since the quality of the data determines the quality of the result.

When a process yields more than one product, the impacts must be allocated between them, and the choice of allocation can change the conclusion. An allocation by mass, by economic value or by system expansion can give different results for the same process. Cumming (2014) argues that the sensitivity of the result to these choices should be reported with its interval, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a conclusion that depends on one assumption is fragile. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the functional unit, the boundary, the inventory and the allocation for every assessment.
