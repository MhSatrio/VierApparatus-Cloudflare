An industrial process report must allow its performance, costs and environmental effects to be compared with other processes and checked against the data. The organism should be described by its genotype and its origin, and the feedstock by its composition and source. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and for an industrial process those means include the strain, the feedstock and the operating procedure.

The conditions should be reported in full, including the scale of the vessel, the medium, the temperature, the pH, the oxygen supply and the duration. Keasling (2010) emphasised that the conditions of production determine its economics, and a process reported without them cannot be compared with another. The metrics of yield, titre and productivity should each be given with their time points and their intervals, and the recovery and the purity of the product should be stated.

The economic and life-cycle claims should be reported with their assumptions and their uncertainty. Cumming (2014) argues that each estimate should be shown with its precision, and the assumptions about feedstock price, oil price, allocation and system boundary should be given so that the claim can be tested. Ioannidis (2005) described how a claim that rests on undisclosed assumptions is difficult to assess, and the sensitivity of the claim to these assumptions should be reported.

The data, the models and the calculations should be deposited together, with identifiers. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended keeping the inputs, steps and outputs of an analysis together, so that the process can be reproduced and its claims examined by others.
