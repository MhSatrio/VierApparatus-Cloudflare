The production host is the organism in which the product is made, and its choice shapes every later decision. The host must tolerate the product and the conditions of the vessel, it must grow fast enough on the feedstock to make the process economic, and it must be genetically tractable so that its metabolism can be engineered. Keasling (2012) described the tools that make a host tractable, and the availability of these tools is one of the main criteria for choosing a host.

The host must also secrete or store the product in a form that can be recovered. A product that remains inside the cell requires disruption of the cells before recovery, which adds cost and may release contaminants, while a product secreted into the medium can be recovered more simply. The location of the product is therefore a design variable with consequences for the whole process.

Safety status and regulatory acceptance are decisive for many products. Hosts with a long record of safe use in food or pharmaceutical production are preferred, and a host that is not accepted by the regulators may make a product impossible to sell even if it performs well. These criteria are not scientific in the narrow sense, but they determine the feasibility of the process.

A host that is convenient in the laboratory may be unsuitable at scale. A laboratory strain grown in a flask may tolerate conditions that a vessel does not provide, and its growth may be limited by oxygen or mixing that the flask does not reveal. Cumming (2014) argues that the performance of a host should be measured at the scale of interest, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a choice based on a favourable small-scale result may not hold at scale. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the host, its origin and the conditions of each comparison.
