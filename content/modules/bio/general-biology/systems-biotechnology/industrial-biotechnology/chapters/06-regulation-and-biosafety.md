Industrial biological processes are regulated because the organisms and the products can affect health and the environment. The production organism may be an engineered strain that must be kept contained, and the approval required for its use depends on the jurisdiction and the application. A product intended for food, medicine or agriculture faces further requirements, including evidence of its safety, its composition and the absence of harmful residues.

Regulation applies to the product as well as to the process. A product made by a genetically modified organism may be subject to different rules from one made by a traditional route, even when the molecule is identical, and the regulatory status can depend on the method rather than the substance. Developers must consider this from the outset, since the status of a product determines the market it can enter.

Biosafety assessment examines the host and the traits that have been engineered into it, and it considers the risks of escape and of transfer of genetic material. The assessment requires evidence, and the evidence must be reproducible if it is to be accepted. Ioannidis (2005) described how claims that are not independently checked may not be accepted by those who must rely on them, and independent confirmation is a regulatory as well as a scientific requirement.

Traceability and record keeping are required by regulators, and they serve the scientific purpose of making a process reproducible. A record of the strain, the batches, the conditions and the tests allows a product to be traced to its origin, and the same record allows an investigation to be repeated. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended keeping the inputs, steps and outputs of an analysis together, and Peng (2011) argued that a process is reproducible only with the means of producing it.
