The economics of a biological product are determined by its costs at each stage, and these are the basis of any decision to pursue it. The feedstock is often the largest single cost, and the yield, which converts feedstock into product, sets how much of this cost is attributed to each unit of product. A process with a high yield on a cheap feedstock can compete with established routes, while one with a low yield on a costly feedstock cannot.

The capital cost of the vessels depends on the productivity, which determines how much product a given vessel can make in a given time. A low productivity requires larger or more numerous vessels for the same output, and this raises the capital cost per unit of product. The recovery cost depends on the titre, since a dilute product is expensive to concentrate and purify. Keasling (2010) described how these interacting costs determine whether a metabolic process can be commercial.

The price of the product is sensitive to the yield, the titre and the productivity, and the sensitivity differs from one process to another. A sensitivity analysis varies each assumption in turn and shows which ones determine the cost, so that effort can be directed to the most influential. Cumming (2014) argues that the estimate of cost should be reported with its interval, since many of the inputs are uncertain.

The comparison with a petrochemical route depends on the price of oil and of the feedstock, both of which change over time. A process that is competitive at one oil price may not be at another, and the comparison should be shown across a range of prices. Ioannidis (2005) described how an advantage that is shown under one assumption may disappear under others, and the assumptions of any techno-economic estimate should be stated. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the inputs, the assumptions and the calculation so that the estimate can be repeated and tested.
