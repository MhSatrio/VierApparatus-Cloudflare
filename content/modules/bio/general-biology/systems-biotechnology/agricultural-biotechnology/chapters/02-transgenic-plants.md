A transgenic plant carries a gene from another species, or a modified version of one, that has been inserted into its genome. The most widely used method delivers the gene by the soil bacterium Agrobacterium, which naturally transfers part of its DNA into plant cells, and the gene of interest is placed between the borders of this transferred DNA. Biolistic bombardment, in which DNA is coated onto particles and fired into cells, is an alternative for species that Agrobacterium infects poorly. Gasser and Fraley (1989) described these methods as the basis of plant genetic engineering.

The transformed cells must be regenerated into whole plants, and this regeneration is often the most difficult step, since many species respond poorly to tissue culture. Each regenerated plant is an independent transgenic line, and the lines differ in the site of insertion and in the copy number of the transgene. The number of lines and their origin should be stated, since the lines are the units of the evaluation.

The promoter controls where and when the gene is expressed, and a promoter that drives expression in every tissue may be needed for some traits and undesirable for others. The expression must be measured in the target tissue, since a gene that is highly expressed in the leaves may be silent in the grain. Gene silencing, in which the plant shuts down the transgene, can occur and must be monitored across generations.

Insertion of a transgene at a random site can disrupt a gene, and the disruption may have effects unrelated to the transgene. The insertion site, the copy number and the expression should be confirmed for each line by molecular analysis. Cumming (2014) argues that the expression should be reported with its interval across plants, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a line selected for a favourable phenotype may carry an unrecognised disruption that accounts for it.
