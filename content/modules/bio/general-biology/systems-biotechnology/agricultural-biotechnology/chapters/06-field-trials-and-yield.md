A field trial measures how a crop line performs under the conditions of farming, and its design determines whether the comparison between lines is valid. The standard design is the randomised complete block, in which each line appears once in each block and the blocks are placed to reflect the variation in the field. The randomisation of the lines within blocks prevents the position of a plot from being confused with the performance of the line, and the block structure removes the variation between parts of the field.

A single site and season is rarely enough, because the performance of a line depends on the environment. The genotype by environment interaction means that the ranking of lines can change from one site or season to another, and a line that performs best in one place may perform worst in another. Trials must therefore span several sites and seasons, and the interaction must be analysed and reported, since the average performance alone can conceal it.

Yield is measured on plots of a defined size, and the measurement is affected by the border effects of neighbouring plots and by the spatial variation of the field. The plot size and the treatment of borders should be stated, and the yield should be adjusted for the variation that the design does not remove. Cumming (2014) argues that the yield should be reported with its interval across plots and environments.

A mixed model treats the sites and the seasons as random samples of the environments, which allows the average effect of a trait to be estimated across the environments that could occur. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result from a few favourable sites may not represent the wider population of environments, and the trial should be designed to sample environments that represent the target area. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the design, the sites, the seasons and the analysis, and Gasser and Fraley (1989) noted that a trait must be evaluated under the conditions where it will be used.
