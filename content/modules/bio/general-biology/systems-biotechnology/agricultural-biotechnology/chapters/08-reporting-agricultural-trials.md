A report of an agricultural trial must allow the trial to be repeated and its result to be applied to another setting. The crop and the lines should be identified, with their genotypes and the method by which they were created, since the origin of a line affects its interpretation. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and for a trial the means include the lines, the design and the procedure. Gasser and Fraley (1989) described how the creation of transgenic lines is itself a source of variation that must be reported.

The trial design should be given with its blocks, its plots and the randomisation, along with the sites, the seasons and the management practices, such as fertiliser, irrigation and pest control. The management is part of the environment in which the trait is tested, and a result without it cannot be applied to a farm managed differently. The measurements and their methods, including the plot size and the handling of borders, should be stated.

Each line should be characterised molecularly, with its copy number, its insertion site or its edit, and its expression, so that the trait can be attributed to a known genetic change. The statistical model should be stated, with the environments treated as random where appropriate, and the effect of the trait should be reported with its interval across the environments. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval is the honest description of what the trial shows, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a result from favourable sites may not hold elsewhere.

The data, the genotypes and the analysis code should be deposited with identifiers, so that the trial can be reanalysed and combined with others. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended keeping the inputs, steps and outputs of an analysis together, which allows a field result to be checked and built upon by others.
