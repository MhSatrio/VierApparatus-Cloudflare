Feeding a growing population depends on producing more food from land that is increasingly constrained by water, soil and climate. Godfray et al. (2010) argued that the demand for food is expected to rise substantially in the coming decades, and that meeting it requires gains in productivity together with changes in how food is produced, distributed and wasted. The problem is one of systems, not of any single crop, and the contribution of genetics must be understood within it.

The yield gap is the difference between the yield that a crop could achieve under good management and the yield that it actually achieves in farmers' fields. In many regions the gap is large, and closing it through better water, fertiliser, pest control and planting practice can deliver large gains without any new genetic material. Genetic improvement contributes to closing the gap and to raising the ceiling, and the two approaches are complementary.

The traits that matter most vary by region. In dry regions tolerance of drought and heat is central, in saline soils tolerance of salt is needed, and in many places resistance to pests and diseases protects a harvest that would otherwise be lost. Nutritional quality, such as the content of micronutrients, matters in regions where diets are poor. Gasser and Fraley (1989) described the potential of genetic engineering to deliver such traits, and the choice of trait should follow the local problem.

A single improved trait rarely solves a food problem, because the crop is only one part of a system that also depends on the variety, the soil, the water and the farmer's ability to use the trait. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the context of each claim, since a trait's benefit depends on the conditions in which it was tested, and a claim that omits them cannot be applied elsewhere.
