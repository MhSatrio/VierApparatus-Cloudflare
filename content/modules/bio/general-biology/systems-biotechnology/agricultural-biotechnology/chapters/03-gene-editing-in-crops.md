Gene editing changes a crop's own genes with a programmable nuclease, and it can do so without leaving foreign DNA in the plant. This is its principal attraction over transgenesis, since the edited plant may be indistinguishable from one produced by conventional breeding. The editing components must be delivered into the plant cells, and the plant must then be regenerated, and the method of delivery determines whether the plant retains any foreign DNA. Gasser and Fraley (1989) described the earlier transgenic approach against which gene editing is now compared.

Editing components can be delivered as DNA, which may integrate into the genome and must be segregated away in later generations, or as a protein complexed with the guide RNA, which is degraded and leaves no DNA behind. The second route gives plants free of foreign DNA directly, but it is harder to achieve in many species. The method should be reported, since the regulatory status of the plant may depend on it.

The genes that are edited include those controlling disease susceptibility, the shape and size of grain, and tolerance of herbicides. A small change to a gene may alter a trait, while the loss of the gene may have more severe effects, and the consequences differ by the type of edit. The chosen edit must be characterised, since a small deletion or insertion may not produce the expected change in the protein.

The detection of edits and of off-target changes in the plant genome follows the same principles as in other organisms, but the genome of a crop may be large, polyploid and variable. Ioannidis (2005) described how a method developed in one system may not transfer directly to another, and Cumming (2014) argues that the frequency of edits and off-target changes should be reported with their intervals. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the editing method, the guide sequences and the verification for each line.
