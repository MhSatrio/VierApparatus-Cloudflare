Crops are attacked by insects and by pathogens, and genetic resistance is one of the most effective ways to protect them. Insect resistance has been achieved by expressing insecticidal proteins from soil bacteria, which kill specific pests when they feed. Pathogen resistance is often conferred by resistance genes that recognise the molecules a pathogen delivers into the plant and trigger a defence. Gasser and Fraley (1989) described the first transgenic plants made to resist insects, and the principle of resistance has since been applied to many crops.

Resistance is threatened by evolution. A pest or pathogen population that is exposed to a resistance trait will contain individuals that carry mutations allowing them to survive, and these individuals will multiply and spread until the trait fails. The rate of this evolution depends on the genetic variation in the population, the selection pressure and the generation time, and a trait deployed widely can be overcome within a few seasons.

The refuge strategy slows the evolution of resistance by maintaining a proportion of the crop without the trait, so that susceptible pests survive and mate with any resistant ones that emerge. The offspring of such matings are mostly susceptible, which dilutes the resistance allele in the population. The size and the placement of the refuge must be specified, since the effect depends on them.

Resistance can also affect organisms that are not the target, and their effects must be assessed. The monitoring of pest populations and the stewardship of the trait are needed to detect the first signs of resistance. Ioannidis (2005) described how a trait that appears effective in early trials may fail once it is widely used, and Cumming (2014) argues that the frequency of resistance should be monitored and reported with its interval. The monitoring data should be recorded so that the trait can be managed on evidence.
