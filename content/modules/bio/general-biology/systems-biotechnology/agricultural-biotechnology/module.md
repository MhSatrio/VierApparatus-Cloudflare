Agricultural biotechnology applies the methods of molecular biology to the crops and livestock on which human nutrition depends. The demand for food is growing, and the land and water available to produce it are limited. Godfray et al. (2010) argued that the challenge of feeding a growing population requires gains in productivity across many systems, and biotechnology is one of the tools that can contribute to those gains. Its value is judged by whether the improvements it produces are real, safe and sustainable in the fields where they are used.

The topic begins with the purpose of crop improvement and the routes by which it is achieved. Transgenic plants carry genes from other species, a method that Gasser and Fraley (1989) described as a means of introducing traits that conventional breeding could not reach. Genome editing changes the crop's own genes without adding foreign DNA, and marker-assisted selection speeds up conventional breeding by using genetic markers to identify the plants that carry a desired allele.

Resistance to pests and pathogens is among the most important traits, and the durability of resistance depends on how the target organism responds to it. Yield is measured in field trials, where the environment varies and the effect of a trait must be separated from the effects of weather, soil and management. The chapters that follow examine these routes, the resistance, the field evaluation and the regulation.

Cumming (2014) argues that the effect of a trait should be reported with its interval across environments, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a result from a single site or season may not hold elsewhere. Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the genotypes, the sites and the conditions of each trial so that the results can be reproduced and compared.
