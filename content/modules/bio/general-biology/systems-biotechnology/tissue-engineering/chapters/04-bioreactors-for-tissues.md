Tissue constructs are cultured in bioreactors that supply the cells with nutrients and oxygen, and remove their wastes. In a static culture, the medium reaches the cells only by diffusion, which limits the thickness of the tissue to a few hundred micrometres. Bioreactors overcome this limit by moving the medium, so that a thicker construct can be supplied and its cells can survive. Langer and Vacanti (1993) identified the supply of nutrients as one of the principal constraints on tissue thickness, and the bioreactor is the means of overcoming it.

Rotating wall and spinner flasks keep constructs suspended and in motion, which reduces the boundary layer around them and improves the supply of nutrients to the surface. They are simple and inexpensive, but the supply they give reaches only the outer part of a large construct, and the interior is still limited by diffusion.

Perfusion bioreactors pass the medium through the pores of a porous scaffold, so that nutrients and oxygen are carried into the interior. The flow rate is set to supply the cells at the required rate, and it must be controlled, since too low a flow starves the centre of the construct and too high a flow may damage the cells. Hutmacher (2000) noted that the pore structure of the scaffold determines the flow that can be imposed, and the two must be designed together.

Perfusion also imposes shear on the cells within the pores. The shear is estimated from the flow rate, the pore geometry and the viscosity of the medium, and it can be substantial in small pores. Cumming (2014) argues that the shear and the supply should be measured and reported with their intervals, since the cells respond to the local conditions rather than to the average of the construct. The scale-up of a perfusion system must maintain the flow and the shear, and the choices should be recorded.
