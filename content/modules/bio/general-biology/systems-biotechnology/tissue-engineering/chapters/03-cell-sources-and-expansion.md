Tissue engineering needs cells in large numbers, and the source of the cells is one of the first decisions of a project. Autologous cells are taken from the patient, which avoids rejection but requires a biopsy and limits the number available. Allogeneic cells come from a donor, which can be expanded for many patients but carry the risk of rejection and of transmitting disease. Cell lines proliferate indefinitely and are easy to obtain, but they may differ from the primary cells of the tissue. Pluripotent stem cells can in principle give rise to many cell types, and they raise their own questions of control.

Cells are expanded in culture by repeated passage until enough are available. During expansion the cells divide, and many lose the differentiated features of their tissue of origin, a loss that is often called dedifferentiation. The construct then contains cells that do not perform the function of the tissue, and the expansion must be designed to preserve the phenotype as far as possible. Langer and Vacanti (1993) described the expansion of cells as a central step of the process.

Variation between donors and between batches of cells is a major source of inconsistency. Two donors may give cells that expand and differentiate differently, and two batches from one donor may differ with their passage history. The variation should be measured and controlled, and the donor and the batch should be recorded for every construct.

Expanded cells must be characterised before they are seeded. Markers of identity confirm the cell type, markers of function show whether the cells perform their role, and tests of genetic stability show whether the cells have changed. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that rests on an unverified material may not hold, and Cumming (2014) argues that the variation in marker expression should be reported with its interval across donors and batches. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the source, the passage number and the characterisation of each cell batch.
