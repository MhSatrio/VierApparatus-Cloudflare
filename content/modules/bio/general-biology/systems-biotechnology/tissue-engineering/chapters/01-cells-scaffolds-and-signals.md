A tissue construct depends on three elements working together. The cells carry out the function of the tissue, whether that is the secretion of cartilage matrix or the contraction of muscle. The scaffold gives the construct its shape, provides surfaces to which the cells can attach and holds them in position. The signals, which include growth factors and mechanical stimuli, tell the cells what to do, when to divide, when to differentiate and when to deposit matrix. Langer and Vacanti (1993) set out these elements as the foundation of the field.

The cells must attach to the scaffold, survive the process of seeding, and differentiate into the cell types of the target tissue. A cell that is seeded but does not attach is lost, and a cell that attaches but does not differentiate may proliferate without forming the tissue. The choice of cell and the conditions under which it is expanded determine its suitability, and the cell type should be identified and its identity confirmed.

The scaffold must match the target tissue in its mechanical properties and its structure. A scaffold that is too stiff or too soft will alter the behaviour of the cells, and a scaffold without the right pores will prevent them from entering and forming tissue throughout. The scaffold is also a source of signals, since its chemistry and its surface influence how cells attach and what they do.

The signals are supplied as soluble factors in the medium or as mechanical loads applied to the construct. Their dose and timing determine the outcome, and a signal given at the wrong stage can drive the cells towards the wrong fate. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result obtained with one combination of elements may not hold with another, and each element should be measured and reported. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the cell source, the scaffold and the signals used, so that the construct can be reproduced.
