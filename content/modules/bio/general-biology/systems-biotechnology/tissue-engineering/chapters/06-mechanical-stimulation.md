Cells sense the mechanical environment in which they live, and many tissues are shaped by the loads they bear. Mechanical stimulation applies these loads to a construct in the laboratory to direct its development. Cartilage is grown under compression, which mimics the loads of a joint, muscle and vascular cells are stretched cyclically to align them, and bone constructs are exposed to fluid pressure that drives the formation of mineral. Hutmacher (2000) noted that the scaffold must transmit these loads to the cells without failing.

The cells sense stiffness and strain through their adhesions to the scaffold and the matrix they make, and they respond by changing their gene expression and their organisation. The response depends on the magnitude, the frequency and the duration of the load. A moderate load stimulates the formation of matrix, while an excessive load damages the cells and the construct, and the window between these is narrow for many tissues.

Overloading is a real risk. A construct loaded beyond its capacity may crack or lose its structure, and the cells within it may die. The loads should be chosen from the properties of the target tissue and the mechanical strength of the construct, and the construct should be monitored for damage during the application of the load. The safety margin should be stated.

The mechanical environment inside the construct is difficult to measure, because the cells experience local strains that differ from the applied load. Cumming (2014) argues that the response of the construct should be reported with its interval across constructs, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the loading protocol, including the magnitude, the frequency and the duration, so that the stimulation can be repeated.
