Oxygen diffuses only a short distance through living tissue, and cells farther than about two hundred micrometres from a supply of blood die from lack of it. A tissue thicker than this must therefore be vascularised, with vessels that carry blood through its interior. Langer and Vacanti (1993) recognised that the lack of a vascular supply was a central obstacle to engineering tissues of useful size, and the problem remains the main barrier to larger constructs.

Several strategies promote the formation of vessels. Endothelial cells, which line blood vessels, can be seeded into the construct together with the tissue cells, so that they organise into networks. Angiogenic factors released from the scaffold attract vessels from the host, and a construct can be prevascularised in culture before it is implanted, so that the vessels are already present when it joins the circulation.

The vessels that form must connect to the host circulation, a process called anastomosis, and they must carry blood. A network of vessels that does not connect is of no use, and its presence may give a false impression of the vascularisation of the construct. The functional connection must be tested by perfusion or by imaging the flow of blood through the vessels.

The density and the function of the vessels should be measured and reported with their intervals. Ioannidis (2005) described how a claim of vascularisation based on a few sections may not hold for the construct as a whole, and Cumming (2014) argues that the density should be given with its variation across constructs and across sections. The sampling of the construct, and the proportion of its volume examined, should be stated.
