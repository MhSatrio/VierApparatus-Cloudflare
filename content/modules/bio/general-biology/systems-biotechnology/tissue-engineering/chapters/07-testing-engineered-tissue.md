An engineered tissue is judged against the native tissue it is meant to replace. Histology shows whether the expected cell types are present and whether they are organised as in the native tissue, and immunohistochemistry shows whether the matrix proteins characteristic of the tissue are made. These tests reveal the composition and the structure of the construct, but they do not show whether it works, so they are used together with the mechanical and functional tests that follow.

Mechanical testing measures the stiffness and the strength of the construct and compares them with those of the native tissue. A construct that has the right cells but is much weaker than the tissue will not bear the loads placed on it, and its mechanics must be tested under the conditions it will experience. Functional tests show whether the tissue performs its role, such as the secretion of a product, the contraction of a muscle or the transmission of a signal.

The viability of the cells within the construct is measured by stains and by the metabolic activity of the tissue, and the distribution of viable cells across the construct shows whether the centre is supplied. The measurement must be made throughout the construct and not only at its surface, since the surface is usually the best supplied.

Animal models provide a test of the construct in a living body before any clinical use, but they have their own limits. The animal's immune system, its size and its mechanical loads differ from those of a patient, and a construct that works in an animal may fail in a person. Ioannidis (2005) described how results from a model may not generalise, and Cumming (2014) argues that the results of each test should be reported with the number of replicate constructs and their interval. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the tests and their conditions, so that the comparison with the native tissue can be checked.
