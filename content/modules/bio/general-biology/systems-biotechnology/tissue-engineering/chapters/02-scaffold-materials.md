A scaffold can be made from natural materials, which often carry the biological cues that cells recognise, or from synthetic materials, which can be controlled more precisely. Collagen and hyaluronan are natural components of the matrix, and they support cell attachment and signalling. Synthetic polyesters such as polylactide and polyglycolide are widely used because they degrade by hydrolysis into products the body can clear, and ceramics such as calcium phosphate are used for bone. Hutmacher (2000) reviewed the choice of these materials for bone and cartilage, and showed that each class has its own advantages and limits.

The structure of the scaffold controls how cells enter it and how nutrients and oxygen reach them. Pores must be large enough for cells to migrate into, and they must be interconnected so that the cells and the vessels that follow can pass through the whole structure. A scaffold with closed pores may support cells only at its surface, and the central tissue then dies. The porosity and the pore size are therefore design variables that must be measured and reported.

The degradation rate must match the rate at which the tissue forms. A scaffold that degrades too quickly collapses before the cells have made enough matrix to bear load, and one that degrades too slowly remains as a foreign body that may impair the tissue. The degradation should be measured under conditions that mimic the tissue, since the rate depends on the pH, the enzymes and the mechanical loading present.

Scaffolds must not provoke a harmful response, and Williams (2008) described the mechanisms by which materials interact with the body and determine their compatibility. Biocompatibility is tested by exposing cells and tissues to the material and measuring their response. Cumming (2014) argues that the response should be reported with its interval across replicates, and the tests should use the form of the material that will be implanted.
