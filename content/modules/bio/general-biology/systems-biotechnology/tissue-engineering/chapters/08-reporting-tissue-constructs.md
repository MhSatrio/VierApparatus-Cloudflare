An engineered tissue construct can be reproduced only from a full description of how it was made. The cells should be identified with their source, their donor if they are not from a cell line, and their passage number, since these affect how the cells behave. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and for a construct those means are the cells, the scaffold, the signals and the procedure of culture. Langer and Vacanti (1993) set out these elements as the basis of the field, and the report should give each.

The scaffold should be described by its material, its porosity, its pore interconnection and its degradation rate, and the signals and mechanical loads applied should be stated with their magnitude, frequency and timing. The culture conditions, including the medium and the bioreactor, should be given, since they determine the outcome. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended keeping the inputs, steps and outputs of an analysis together, and for a construct the inputs include all of these elements.

The outcomes should be reported against the native tissue, with histology, mechanics and function each given with the number of replicate constructs and the interval of each measurement. Cumming (2014) argues that a single construct is weak evidence, and the variation between constructs should be shown. The viability and the vascularisation should be reported throughout the construct, with the sampling that was used to measure them.

Animal and clinical data, where they exist, should be reported with their limits, and the protocols and data should be deposited with identifiers. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result from a limited number of experiments may not hold in another setting, and a full report allows others to judge whether a construct is ready for the use claimed.
