Tissue engineering aims to replace or repair damaged tissue by growing it from cells. Langer and Vacanti (1993) set out its principle as the combination of living cells, a supporting structure and the signals that guide the cells to form a functional tissue. The field has produced skin, cartilage, bone and other tissues for clinical and research use, and it draws on cell biology, materials science and engineering in equal measure.

The topic begins with the three elements. The cells provide the function of the tissue, the scaffold provides a structure on which they can attach and organise, and the signals, from soluble factors and mechanical cues, direct their behaviour. The construct is successful only when all three are right, and a failure of any one shows in the tissue that results. The chapters that follow examine each element and the processes that link them.

Scaffolds are made from materials whose porosity, degradation and mechanical properties must match the tissue being formed. Hutmacher (2000) reviewed the scaffolds used for bone and cartilage, and showed that the pore structure controls how cells can enter and how nutrients reach them. The scaffold should degrade at a rate that matches the formation of new tissue, so that it does not disappear before the tissue can support itself.

Thick tissues need a vascular supply, since cells more than a few hundred micrometres from a blood supply die from lack of oxygen and nutrients. The engineering of vessels within a construct is one of the principal challenges of the field. A construct that is tested only in small pieces may appear healthy and fail when it is made larger, so the testing must reflect the scale of the intended use.

Cumming (2014) argues that the function of a construct should be reported with its interval across replicate constructs, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a result obtained in a few constructs may not reproduce. Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the cells, the scaffold, the signals and the conditions of culture, so that a construct can be made again by others.
