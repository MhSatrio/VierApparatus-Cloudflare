In a batch culture, all the substrate is supplied at the start, and the cells grow until it is exhausted. Fed-batch operation adds the substrate during the run, so that its concentration is controlled. This allows the cells to reach much higher densities than a batch allows, because the substrate can be supplied without reaching the concentrations that inhibit growth or cause the cells to form by-products. Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez (2009) described fed-batch as the standard mode for high-density production.

The feed rate is chosen to control the growth. If the substrate is supplied faster than the cells can use it, it accumulates, and the cells may switch to overflow metabolism, forming acetate or other by-products that reduce the yield. If it is supplied more slowly, the growth is limited by the feed. The control aims to match the supply to the demand, so that the substrate concentration stays low and the growth rate stays at a chosen value.

An exponential feed profile holds the specific growth rate constant. As the biomass grows exponentially, the demand for substrate grows, and the feed rate must increase in the same way to keep the growth rate fixed. The profile is derived from the mass balance on the substrate and the kinetics of growth, and it requires an estimate of the biomass at the start of the feed and the yield coefficient. Its accuracy depends on these estimates, which should be reported with their uncertainty.

Monitoring confirms that the feed is working. The mass of the feed can be measured on a balance, and the substrate can be followed by a sensor or by sampling. A feed that fails or is mis-calibrated can starve the cells or flood them, and a long run increases the chance of contamination and of oxygen limitation. Cumming (2014) argues that the feed profile and the growth rate should be reported with their intervals, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a control that works in one run may fail in another, so runs should be replicated.
