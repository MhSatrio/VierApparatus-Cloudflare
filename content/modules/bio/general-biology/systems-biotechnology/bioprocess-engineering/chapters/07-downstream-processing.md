The product of a bioprocess must be separated from the cells, the medium and the by-products before it can be used, and this downstream processing often costs more than the fermentation that made the product. The first step removes the cells from the broth, usually by centrifugation or filtration. If the product is inside the cells, the cells must be disrupted first, and the disruption releases many contaminants that must then be removed. Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez (2009) noted that the choice of route depends on where the product is and how much of it there is.

The capture step concentrates the product from a large volume. It may use extraction into a second liquid, precipitation with a salt or a solvent, or chromatography, which separates molecules by their properties. Capture is often the largest single cost, and a high titre reduces its cost because less volume must be handled. A dilute product requires large equipment and large volumes of reagents for the same output.

Polishing steps remove the remaining impurities to reach the required purity. These steps are chosen for the impurities that remain, and each step has a yield, so the overall yield is the product of the yields of the steps. A process with several steps each losing a few per cent can lose a large fraction of the product overall, and the loss at each step should be measured.

Yield and purity should be reported at each step, with their intervals across runs, since the efficiency of separation varies. Cumming (2014) argues that these values should be given with their variability, and Ioannidis (2005) described how the yield from a single favourable run can mislead about the process as a whole. The final product must also meet its specification, and the methods used to measure purity should be stated with their limits of detection.
