A bioprocess run can be reproduced only if the report states how it was carried out. The organism, its origin and its inoculum should be described first, since the culture's history affects its performance. The vessel should be given with its geometry, its scale and its working volume, because the same conditions in a different vessel may not give the same result. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and for a run those means include the vessel and the procedure.

The medium, the feed profile and the setpoints should be stated, along with the recorded sensor data and the calibrations used to convert them. Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez (2009) showed that oxygen transfer and mixing depend on the vessel and the operating conditions, and their measurements should be reported with the run. The sensor data should be deposited, since they are the record from which the conditions can be checked.

The growth, the substrate and the product profiles should be reported with their yields and titres, each with its interval across replicate runs. Cumming (2014) argues that a single run is a weak basis for a claim, and the number of replicate runs should be stated. The recovery steps should be reported with their yields and purities, so that the process can be judged as a whole rather than at its most favourable stage.

The run data and the calculations should be deposited with identifiers, so that the performance can be recalculated and compared with other runs. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended keeping the inputs, steps and outputs of an analysis together, and this applies to a bioprocess run as much as to any computational analysis.
