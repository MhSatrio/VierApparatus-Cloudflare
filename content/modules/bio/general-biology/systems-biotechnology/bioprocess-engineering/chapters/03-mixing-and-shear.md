Mixing makes the conditions of a culture uniform. The substrate, the oxygen, the pH and the temperature must be the same throughout the vessel, and mixing carries them from where they are supplied to where they are consumed. The mixing time is the time needed for a tracer added at one point to become homogeneous, and it is measured by following the response of a probe to a pulse of tracer. A short mixing time indicates a homogeneous vessel, while a long one indicates gradients that cells can experience.

Gradients form in large vessels because the supply of substrate and oxygen is at one place and the consumption is everywhere. Cells near the feed or the gas inlet may encounter high concentrations, and cells far from it may be starved or hypoxic. The physiology of the culture then varies across the vessel, and the average of the measurements may describe no cell. Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez (2009) noted that these gradients are a major problem of scale-up, since they grow with the size of the vessel.

Shear is the stress that the cells experience from the flow, generated by impellers and by rising gas bubbles. Shear can damage the cell wall or the membrane, and it can denature proteins and alter the product. Sensitive cells, such as those of animals and plants, may be damaged by levels of shear that bacteria tolerate well, and the shear should be considered in the choice of reactor and operating conditions.

The power input per unit volume describes both the mixing and the shear, since the energy supplied by the impeller determines both. Increasing the power improves the mixing but also increases the shear, so the operating point is a balance. Ioannidis (2005) described how a single criterion can be chosen to produce a desired result, and the power and the mixing time should both be reported. Cumming (2014) argues that these measurements should be shown with their intervals, since they vary across the vessel.
