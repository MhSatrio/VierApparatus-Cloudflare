The bioreactor is the vessel in which cells are grown, and its design determines the conditions that every cell experiences. The stirred tank is the most widely used, since an impeller mixes the culture, baffles prevent swirling, and the gas and the temperature can be controlled. It suits a wide range of microorganisms, and its behaviour is well understood, which is why it is the default for many processes. Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez (2009) described the stirred tank as the reference against which other designs are compared.

The airlift reactor circulates the culture by the rise of gas bubbles in a draft tube, and it generates less shear than a stirred tank. It suits cells that are damaged by the impeller, such as some animal and plant cells, and it can give good oxygen transfer at lower energy input. Its mixing is less intense, so the homogeneity of the culture must be checked at the scale used.

A bubble column is a simple vessel in which gas rises through a liquid without mechanical agitation, and it has few moving parts. It is cheap and robust, but its mixing and oxygen transfer depend strongly on the gas flow and the height of the column. Packed bed reactors hold cells on a solid support, and they suit cells that grow as films or that are immobilised. Photobioreactors supply light to phototrophic organisms, and their design is governed by the penetration of light into the culture.

The choice of reactor follows from the organism's sensitivity to shear, its oxygen demand and its growth form, and the design should be justified by these requirements. A reactor that is convenient for a small flask may not reproduce the conditions of production, and the materials and sterility requirements add further constraints. The properties of the chosen design should be measured at the intended scale, since a reactor that works in one size may not work in another. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the reactor type, its dimensions and its operating conditions, so that a run can be repeated.
