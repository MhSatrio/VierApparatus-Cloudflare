Oxygen is poorly soluble in water, so an aerobic culture depends on a continuous transfer of oxygen from the gas to the liquid. The rate of transfer is the volumetric coefficient, which combines the interfacial area of the bubbles and the mass transfer coefficient, multiplied by the driving force, the difference between the oxygen concentration at saturation and the concentration in the liquid. Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez (2009) gave this relation as the basis of oxygen transfer in bioreactors, and each term must be measured for the vessel in use.

The coefficient is measured by the dynamic gassing-out method, in which the dissolved oxygen is depleted by stopping the gas flow in a culture or in water, and then its recovery is recorded when the gas is restored. The rise of the concentration follows an exponential curve whose rate gives the coefficient. The measurement depends on the probe response and on the conditions, and it should be made in the medium and at the gas flow that the process uses.

The oxygen uptake rate of the culture is the rate at which the cells consume oxygen, and it rises with the cell density. At low density the transfer rate exceeds the demand and the dissolved oxygen remains high, but as the density rises the uptake can exceed the transfer, and the dissolved oxygen falls. The density at which this occurs is the critical cell density, and beyond it growth is limited by transfer.

Oxygen transfer is scaled by keeping either the volumetric coefficient or the power input per unit volume constant, since these determine the transfer in the larger vessel. The choice of criterion affects the other conditions, such as the shear and the mixing, and the trade-offs must be considered. Cumming (2014) argues that the coefficient should be reported with its interval across replicate measurements, since it varies with the conditions, and the criterion used for scale-up should be stated with the result.
