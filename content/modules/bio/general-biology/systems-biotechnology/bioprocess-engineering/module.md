Bioprocess engineering makes the conditions of a culture uniform and controllable at the scale of a production vessel. A laboratory flask is shaken and open to the air, while a large vessel must supply oxygen, remove heat, mix the contents and maintain the nutrients, and each of these demands has consequences for the cells. Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez (2009) reviewed how the transfer of oxygen in particular limits the scale of aerobic processes, and the topic builds from the vessel to the control of the process.

The topic begins with the types of bioreactor and the organisms for which each is suited. The stirred tank is the most common, because it gives good control of conditions for a wide range of organisms, while other designs suit cells that are shear-sensitive or that grow as films. The choice of vessel is a design decision that affects the oxygen supply, the mixing and the cost, and it should be justified by the organism and the product.

Oxygen supply is central to aerobic cultures. Oxygen must pass from the gas to the liquid and then to the cells, and the rate of transfer is described by a volumetric coefficient multiplied by the driving force. At high cell density the demand for oxygen can exceed the supply, and growth is then limited by transfer rather than by the cell's metabolism. Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez (2009) showed that the transfer rate must be measured at the scale of the vessel, since the coefficient changes with the design and the operating conditions.

Mixing and shear determine the homogeneity of the culture and the damage to the cells. Poor mixing creates gradients of substrate and oxygen, so that cells in different parts of the vessel experience different conditions. High shear can damage cells and their products. The chapters that follow examine the mixing and the growth kinetics, the operation in fed-batch mode, the control and the recovery of the product.

Cumming (2014) argues that measurements of growth, oxygen transfer and product formation should be reported with their intervals, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a conclusion drawn at one scale may not hold at another. Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the vessel, the conditions and the data so that a run can be reproduced.
