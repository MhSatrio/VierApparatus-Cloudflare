Enzymes are the catalysts of biotechnology, and their natural forms are rarely suited to an industrial process. They may be too slow, too unselective, too unstable at high temperatures or unable to tolerate organic solvents. Enzyme engineering changes their properties by altering their sequence, and the field has become one of the most productive areas of biotechnology. Bornscheuer et al. (2012) described the move from natural to engineered biocatalysts as the third wave of biocatalysis, in which designed enzymes are used across many industries.

The topic begins with the link between structure and catalysis, which determines where changes can be made. An enzyme's active site, its binding pocket and the flexible regions that move during catalysis each affect activity, and changes far from the active site can alter it through the protein's dynamics. Two broad strategies are then used. Rational design changes residues chosen from structural knowledge, while directed evolution accumulates beneficial mutations through repeated rounds of diversification and selection. Packer and Liu (2015) reviewed the methods of directed evolution in detail.

The screening and selection methods that identify improved variants are a central constraint, since the power of directed evolution depends on how many variants can be tested. The computational design of new activities extends these methods, and the trade-off between activity and stability limits what can be achieved in a single step. Enzymes engineered for high activity are often less stable, and the balance must be chosen for the process.

Kinetic parameters, the rate and the affinity of an enzyme, depend on the conditions in which they are measured. Cumming (2014) argues that they should be reported with their intervals and the conditions, since a variant that appears better at one pH may be worse at another. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result selected from many variants can overstate the improvement, and Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording the sequences, the assays and the conditions for each variant.
