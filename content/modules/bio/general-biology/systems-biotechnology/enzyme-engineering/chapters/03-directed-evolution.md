Directed evolution imitates natural selection in the laboratory. A gene is diversified by introducing random mutations or by recombining related genes, the resulting variants are expressed and tested, and the best one becomes the template for the next round. Each round accumulates the mutations that improve the property of interest, and after several rounds the enzyme can have activity and stability far beyond those of its starting point. Packer and Liu (2015) reviewed the methods used to generate diversity and to select improved variants.

Diversity is generated by error-prone polymerase chain reaction, which introduces mutations at a controlled rate, or by DNA shuffling, which recombines fragments of related genes. Each method produces a different spread of variants, and the choice affects the mutations that can be found. The rate of mutation should be chosen so that most variants carry one or two changes, since a library with many changes per variant is hard to interpret and rarely improved.

Mutations in one gene interact. The effect of a combination of beneficial mutations is often different from the sum of their separate effects, a phenomenon known as epistasis. Two mutations that each improve activity may together reduce it, and two that are neutral alone may together have a large effect. This is why directed evolution finds improvements that rational reasoning would not predict, and why its results are hard to extrapolate.

The method works without knowledge of the mechanism, which is its strength. Cumming (2014) argues that the improvement at each round should be reported with its interval across replicate screens, since small improvements are easily confused with the variation of the assay. Each round should also report how many variants were screened, so that the size of the search is known.
