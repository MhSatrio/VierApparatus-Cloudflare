An engineered enzyme can be compared with others only if the report states exactly what the variant is and how it was measured. The parent sequence and every mutation in each variant should be given, since the function of a variant is a property of its sequence. Peng (2011) argued that a result is reproducible only with the means of producing it, and for an enzyme the sequence is the first and most important of those means.

The library and the selection should be described with the method of generation, the number of variants made and the number screened. Packer and Liu (2015) described how the method determines the spread of mutations, and the size of the library determines what can be found. The screen or selection should be reported with its throughput, its sensitivity and the rate at which hits were confirmed, since these determine how much of the search was successful.

The kinetic parameters and the stability data should each be reported with their conditions and intervals. Cumming (2014) argues that estimates should be given with their precision, and the conditions of pH, temperature and solvent determine the values obtained. A parameter reported without its conditions cannot be compared with another report, and a half-life reported without its exposure cannot be used to judge the enzyme.

Computational predictions should be reported with their calibration against the measured values, and the selection of variants should be disclosed. Ioannidis (2005) described how the best of many variants tends to overstate its improvement, and the full set of tested variants should be deposited, not only the best. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended depositing the sequences, the assays and the data together, so that each claim about a variant can be checked.
