An enzyme accelerates a reaction by binding its substrate in an active site and stabilising the transition state of the reaction. The active site is usually a small region lined with residues that position the substrate, and the chemistry happens there. Bornscheuer et al. (2012) emphasised that understanding the active site is the starting point for engineering, since changes to it most directly alter the reaction.

Activity is described by two kinetic parameters. The turnover number is the maximal rate of catalysis per enzyme molecule, and the Michaelis constant is the substrate concentration at which the rate is half maximal and gives a measure of affinity. An improvement may take the form of a higher turnover, a lower Michaelis constant or both, and the parameters must be reported separately because they describe different properties. The efficiency is the ratio of the two, which is often used to compare variants.

Specificity describes which substrates an enzyme acts on and which it ignores. An engineer may seek to broaden or narrow it, and changes to the binding pocket affect both the affinity and the selectivity. The changes often involve trade-offs, since a pocket that accepts a new substrate may bind the old one less well.

Residues far from the active site can influence catalysis through the flexibility and the dynamics of the protein. Motions that open and close the active site, or that transmit changes across the structure, may be altered by distant mutations, and this makes the effect of a mutation hard to predict from its location alone. Cumming (2014) argues that kinetic parameters should be reported with their intervals, since they are estimated from a few substrate concentrations. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the assay conditions and the substrate concentrations, so that the parameters can be reproduced.
