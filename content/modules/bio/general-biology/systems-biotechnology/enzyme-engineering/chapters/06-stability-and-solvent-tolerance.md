Industrial processes expose enzymes to conditions that natural enzymes do not withstand: high temperatures, extreme pH, high salt and organic solvents. An enzyme that loses its fold in such conditions is useless however active it is, and stability is often the property that most limits a biocatalyst. Bornscheuer et al. (2012) emphasised that engineering for stability is as important as engineering for activity in industrial use.

Stability is measured as the half-life of activity under a stated condition, which is the time for activity to fall to half its initial value, or as the melting temperature at which the fold unfolds. The two measures can differ, since an enzyme may unfold without losing all activity at once. The condition of exposure must be stated with the half-life, because a value at one temperature or solvent concentration does not describe the enzyme at another.

Activity and stability often trade against each other. The flexibility that allows an enzyme to change shape during catalysis can also make it unstable, while a rigid fold is stable but may catalyse poorly. Engineering one property can therefore reduce the other, and the balance must be chosen for the process in which the enzyme will be used. Cumming (2014) argues that the trade-off should be shown by reporting both measures with their intervals for each variant.

Two strategies are common. Consensus design builds an enzyme from the residues most frequent in an alignment of its relatives, which often improves stability, and stabilising mutations at the surface or in the core can increase the melting temperature. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the conditions of each stability test, including the temperature, the pH and the solvent, so that the half-lives can be compared.
