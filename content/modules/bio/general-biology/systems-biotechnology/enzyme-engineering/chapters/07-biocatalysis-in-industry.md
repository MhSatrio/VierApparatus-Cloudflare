Enzymes are used in industry to make pharmaceuticals, fine chemicals, food ingredients, detergents and fuels. Their advantages are considerable: they are selective, so they make fewer by-products, they work under mild conditions of temperature and pressure, and they reduce the use of harsh reagents and the waste they produce. Bornscheuer et al. (2012) described how these advantages have led to the replacement of many chemical steps with enzymatic ones, and the chapter examines the practical forms in which they are used.

An enzyme may be used free in solution, immobilised on a support so that it can be recovered and reused, or within whole cells that supply the enzyme and its cofactors. Each form has costs and benefits. Immobilisation allows reuse and simplifies separation, but it may reduce activity and adds the cost of the support. Whole cells avoid the purification of the enzyme but introduce side reactions and mass transfer limits.

The economics depend on the cost of the enzyme, the number of times it can be reused and the yield it achieves. An expensive enzyme that can be reused many times may be cheaper per product than a cheap one that is used once. The evaluation must include the stability of the enzyme over the reuse cycles, since a loss of activity after each cycle changes the economics completely.

Scale-up exposes problems that are not visible in small experiments. Substrate and product inhibition reduce the rate at high concentrations, and the enzyme may be inhibited by the solvent or by impurities. Ioannidis (2005) described how a process that works in one setting may fail in another, and the inhibition should be measured across the concentration range of the process. Cumming (2014) argues that the conversion and the selectivity should be reported with their intervals, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the number of reuses and the conditions of each cycle.
