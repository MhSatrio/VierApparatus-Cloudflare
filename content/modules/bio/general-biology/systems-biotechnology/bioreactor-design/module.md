A bioreactor design begins with the requirements of the organism and the process. The required oxygen transfer, the tolerable shear, the temperature to be held, the sterility needed and the working volume are the criteria, and each constrains the geometry of the vessel. Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez (2009) showed that the choice of geometry and operating conditions cannot be separated, since the same vessel gives different oxygen transfer at different agitation and aeration.

The stirred tank is designed around its height to diameter ratio, the number and the placement of its impellers and the position of its gas inlet. A tall vessel with several impellers mixes a deep culture and improves oxygen transfer, while a squat vessel has a larger surface area and is easier to cool. The proportions are chosen to suit the process, and they should be reported, since the geometry determines the behaviour of the vessel at every scale.

Baffles prevent the culture from swirling as a solid body, which would reduce mixing and oxygen transfer, and gas spargers distribute the air or oxygen in small bubbles that increase the interfacial area. Heat transfer surfaces, such as jackets or internal coils, remove the heat of metabolism, which can be large at high cell density. Each of these features must be designed for the process, and the design should show how each meets its criterion.

Materials and sterility features protect the culture from contamination and from the products of corrosion or leaching. The vessel must be cleanable and must withstand the sterilisation procedure, and the seals and the ports must be designed to keep the culture closed. Ioannidis (2005) described how a design that has not been tested under its conditions of use may fail in practice, and Cumming (2014) argues that the performance of each feature should be measured with its interval. Sandve et al. (2013) and Peng (2011) recommended recording every design decision so that the vessel can be reproduced.
