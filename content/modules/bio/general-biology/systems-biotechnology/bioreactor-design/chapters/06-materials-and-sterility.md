The materials of a bioreactor are chosen for their resistance to corrosion, to the products of the culture and to the sterilisation procedure. Stainless steel is the usual choice for large vessels, since it withstands steam and is easy to clean, while glass is used for small vessels where the culture can be seen. Single-use vessels made of polymer bags are increasingly used, since they remove the need for cleaning and reduce the risk of cross-contamination between runs, but they carry their own constraints on size and on the leaching of compounds from the plastic.

The surface finish matters because a rough or scratched surface can harbour cells and deposits that later contaminate the culture. A smooth finish is cleaned more easily and resists the adhesion of cells, and the finish should be specified in the design. A deposit that forms on the wall during a run may also release material into the culture, which can affect the product.

The seals, valves and ports must keep the vessel closed throughout the run. Each port is a potential route for contamination, and the number of ports should be minimised and each one should be designed for sterile operation. Steam sterilisation of the vessel in place kills the organisms on the internal surfaces, and the cycle must be validated for time and temperature at every point.

Clean-in-place procedures remove the residues of the previous run, using cleaning solutions circulated through the vessel. The procedure must reach every surface, and its effectiveness must be checked. Leak testing and integrity checks confirm that the vessel is closed before inoculation, and a failed check should stop the run. Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the sterilisation and cleaning procedures, and Cumming (2014) argues that the results of the checks should be reported with their sensitivity. Peng (2011) argued that such procedures must be recorded for a run to be reproduced.
