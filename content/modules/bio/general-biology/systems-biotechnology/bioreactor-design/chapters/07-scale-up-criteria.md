Scale-up extends a bioreactor design that works at small scale to a vessel that produces at the required volume. The difficulty is that the properties of the vessel do not scale in the same way, so no single rule keeps every property the same. Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez (2009) reviewed the criteria that have been proposed, each keeping one property of the culture constant as the vessel grows, and the choice among them is one of the most important decisions in the design of a process.

The constant volumetric oxygen transfer coefficient is a common criterion, since oxygen supply often limits the growth at high density. Keeping it constant ensures that the vessel can supply the oxygen the cells need at the same concentrations as in the small vessel. The constant power input per unit volume is another, and it keeps the mixing intensity and the shear per unit volume the same. The constant tip speed and the constant mixing time are others, each protecting a different aspect of the culture.

These criteria cannot all be kept constant at once. Keeping the power per unit volume constant on enlargement reduces the tip speed and lengthens the mixing time, while keeping the mixing time constant raises the power and the shear. The choice of criterion is therefore a judgement about which property matters most for the organism and the product, and that judgement should be stated.

A scale-down model reproduces the gradients of a large vessel in a small one, by exposing the cells in the small vessel to the variations they would meet in the large. It allows the consequences of a scale-up choice to be tested before the large vessel is built. Ioannidis (2005) described how a design that is tested only under favourable conditions may fail when the conditions change, and Cumming (2014) argues that the performance at each scale should be reported with its interval.
