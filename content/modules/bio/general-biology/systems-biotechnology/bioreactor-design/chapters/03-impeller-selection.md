Impellers are chosen by the duty they must perform. Radial-flow impellers, of which the Rushton turbine is the classic example, throw the liquid outwards from the shaft, creating a high shear zone around the blades and dispersing gas bubbles effectively. They give high oxygen transfer, but the shear they produce may damage sensitive cells. Axial-flow impellers, such as the pitched blade, push the liquid along the axis of the vessel, circulating the bulk with lower shear and less dispersion of gas. Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez (2009) described these two classes and their effects on oxygen transfer.

The size of the impeller relative to the tank is an important design choice. A larger impeller moves more liquid at a given speed and draws more power, while a smaller one must run faster to achieve the same flow. The diameter is usually expressed as a fraction of the tank diameter, and the standard fractions give geometric similarity when the vessel is enlarged.

The power drawn by an impeller depends on its speed, its diameter and its power number, which is a dimensionless quantity characteristic of the impeller type. The power input per unit volume follows from this, and it describes both the mixing and the shear. Choosing an impeller therefore fixes the power that will be dissipated in the culture, and the speed must be set to give the desired power.

Several impellers are combined on one shaft when the vessel is tall, with a radial impeller near the gas inlet to disperse the gas and axial impellers above it to circulate the culture. The combination should be chosen to match the shear tolerance and the oxygen demand of the process. Ioannidis (2005) described how a choice that favours one outcome may not serve another, and Cumming (2014) argues that the performance of each combination should be reported with its interval.
