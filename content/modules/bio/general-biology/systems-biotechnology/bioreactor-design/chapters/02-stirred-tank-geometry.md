The geometry of a stirred tank is defined by a few proportions that are kept constant across designs. The ratio of the liquid height to the tank diameter is the most important, since it determines how many impellers can be placed on the shaft and how far the culture is spread vertically. A height-to-diameter ratio near one suits a single impeller, and a larger ratio requires several, each spaced to mix its own zone. Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez (2009) described the proportions of standard designs and their effects on the mixing.

The bottom shape and the head of the vessel affect the flow and the dead zones. A rounded bottom avoids the corners where cells can settle and accumulate, while a flat bottom is simpler to build but may create zones with poor mixing. The position of the impellers relative to the bottom determines whether the lower region is well mixed, and the choice should be reported with the vessel.

Freeboard is the space above the liquid, and it prevents foam and droplets from carrying the culture out through the exhaust. Foam is common in fermentations with proteins or surfactants, and an insufficient freeboard leads to loss of the culture and contamination of the exhaust line. The freeboard should be designed for the foam expected, and the antifoam strategy should be stated.

Geometric similarity means that the proportions of a vessel are kept the same when it is made larger, so that the flow patterns and the mixing scale in a predictable way. Cumming (2014) argues that the effect of geometry on the measured performance should be reported with its interval, since a change in proportion alters the mixing time and the oxygen transfer, and the assumption of similarity should be tested rather than taken for granted.
