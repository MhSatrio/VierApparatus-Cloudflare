A bioreactor design is a set of requirements translated into a vessel. The first requirement is the working volume and the production rate, which set the size of the vessel and the number of vessels needed. The second is the maximal cell density, which determines the oxygen demand at its highest point, since the oxygen uptake rate is the product of the density and the specific uptake. The required oxygen transfer rate follows from this demand, and it becomes a target for the design of the aeration and agitation.

The shear that the organism tolerates limits the agitation. A bacterium may withstand intense mixing, while an animal or plant cell is damaged by it, and the design must choose impellers and speeds that keep the shear below the tolerance. The temperature range of the organism and the heat generated by the culture determine the heat transfer surface required, since the heat of metabolism rises with the cell density.

Sterility and cleanability are design criteria as well as operating ones. The vessel must withstand sterilisation, must be cleaned between runs, and must keep the culture separate from the environment through its seals and ports. The control of pH and of the gases requires sensors and inlets, and their positions influence the homogeneity of the culture.

The criteria trade against one another. Increasing the agitation to raise the oxygen transfer increases the shear, and increasing the heat transfer surface may reduce the working volume. A design is therefore a compromise, and the weighting of the criteria should be stated. Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez (2009) emphasised that the oxygen criterion is often the one that determines the feasible design at high density, and Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the criteria and their weights.
