A growing culture generates heat, and the amount rises with the cell density and the rate at which the cells metabolise their substrate. The heat of agitation adds to it, since the power put into the liquid is dissipated as heat. A high-density culture can generate more heat per unit volume than a laboratory flask would ever show, and the vessel must remove it to hold the temperature at its setpoint. The heat balance equates the heat generated with the heat removed, and it is the basis of the design of the cooling.

The heat is removed through the walls of the vessel by a jacket, which is a second shell through which a coolant flows, or by internal coils or plates immersed in the culture. The rate of removal is the product of the overall heat transfer coefficient, the area of the surface and the temperature difference between the culture and the coolant. The design must give enough area to remove the peak heat load at the desired temperature difference.

The heat transfer area limits the working volume. As the vessel grows in volume its heat generation grows with the cube of its dimensions, while its jacket area grows with the square, so large vessels have less area per unit volume to remove heat. This constraint is important in scale-up and often determines that large vessels need internal cooling or a different geometry.

The temperature is held by a control system that adjusts the coolant flow, and a surface that is colder than the bulk culture can stress the cells that touch it. Ioannidis (2005) described how a design that works on paper may fail when the actual heat load differs from the estimate, and the heat load should be measured in the vessel. Cumming (2014) argues that the heat transfer coefficient should be reported with its interval, and Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez (2009) noted that the agitation and the heat removal are linked, since the power that mixes the culture also heats it.
