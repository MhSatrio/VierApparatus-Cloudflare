Baffles are vertical plates fixed to the wall of a stirred vessel. Without them the liquid rotates as a solid body with the impeller, and the vortex that forms reduces mixing and the transfer of gas. Baffles break the rotation and convert it into the turbulent mixing that homogenises the culture. Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez (2009) described full baffling, in which the baffles are wide and numerous enough that the vortex disappears, as the normal condition for stirred tanks.

The number and width of the baffles are chosen so that the vessel is fully baffled at the speed used. Four baffles of a tenth of the tank diameter are a common standard, and they should be placed to avoid dead zones near the wall. A vessel that is partly baffled may show variable mixing when the speed or the level of liquid changes, and the design should be verified by measuring the mixing time.

The sparger introduces the gas, usually as a ring of holes below the lowest impeller or as a single pipe. The size of the holes sets the size of the bubbles, and small bubbles give a larger interfacial area for the same gas flow, which increases the oxygen transfer. The sparger design therefore affects the oxygen supply as directly as the agitation does.

At high aeration the gas can accumulate behind the impeller blades and reduce its pumping, a condition called flooding. The impeller then draws less power and mixes less, and the oxygen transfer falls. The aeration and agitation must be matched to avoid flooding, and the condition should be detected by a change in the power drawn. Cumming (2014) argues that the effect of each design on the measured transfer should be reported with its interval, and Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez (2009) showed that the transfer depends on the sparger and the impeller together.
