An emergent property is one that a system shows as a whole and that none of its parts shows alone. A single enzyme does not oscillate, but a set of enzymes linked by feedback can, and a single protein does not switch between two stable states, but a network of interacting regulators can. Kitano (2002) argued that such properties are the central concern of systems biology, because they explain the behaviour of cells that the study of individual components cannot.

Feedback is the principal source of switch-like and oscillatory behaviour. A negative feedback loop that delays its own response can generate sustained oscillations, and a positive feedback loop with a threshold can create two stable states between which a cell switches. The conditions for these behaviours depend on the strengths and delays of the interactions, and the same network can switch between behaviours as its parameters change. Alon (2007) described how the recurring patterns of feedback in biological networks carry out such functions.

Robustness, the maintenance of function despite perturbation, often arises from redundancy and from the distribution of a function over several components. If a function can be performed by more than one path, the loss of one path does not stop it, and the system is robust to the failure of individual parts. This is part of the reason that single-gene knockouts often have small effects, while the combination of several losses has large ones, and the interaction between genes is itself an emergent property.

The distinction between additive and non-additive effects is essential. If two perturbations have effects that add together, the parts behave independently, but if their combined effect differs from the sum, the parts interact. Cumming (2014) argues that an interaction should be estimated with its interval and not inferred from the visual pattern of two effects, since an interaction is a difference between differences and is less precise than either effect.
