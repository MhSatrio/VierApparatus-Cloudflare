A hydrocarbon is a molecule made only of carbon and hydrogen. Hydrocarbon chains form the tails of fatty acids and the hydrophobic cores of membranes. A chain is saturated when every carbon-carbon bond is single, so each carbon carries the maximum number of hydrogen atoms. It is unsaturated when one or more double bonds are present, and each double bond removes two hydrogens from the chain.

The difference in shape is large. Each single bond can rotate, so a saturated chain can adopt many conformations and packs tightly with its neighbours, in a straight arrangement. A double bond cannot rotate, and in the common cis configuration it introduces a kink of about thirty degrees into the chain. Lewis (1916) described the electron pairs that make these bonds, and the geometry of the double bond follows from them.

The kinks have a practical effect. Saturated fatty acids pack into compact arrays and solidify at relatively high temperatures, which is why butter is solid at room temperature. Unsaturated fatty acids pack less tightly and remain liquid, which is why vegetable oils are liquid. The same principle governs membranes: a membrane rich in unsaturated tails is more fluid than one rich in saturated tails, and cells adjust their lipid composition to keep the membrane at a suitable fluidity.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how cells regulate the saturation of their membrane lipids, for example by introducing double bonds with specific desaturase enzymes in response to temperature. Organisms adapted to cold environments often have more unsaturated lipids than organisms living in warm ones, which keeps their membranes fluid when temperatures fall.

Chain structure is determined by mass spectrometry of fatty acid derivatives, by gas chromatography, which separates fatty acids by length and saturation, and by infrared spectroscopy, which detects the double bonds. These methods are the standard analysis of membrane composition.
