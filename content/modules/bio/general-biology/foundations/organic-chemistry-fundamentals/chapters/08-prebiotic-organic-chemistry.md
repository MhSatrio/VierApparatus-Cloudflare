Before life existed, the Earth contained a range of simple organic compounds, and the question of how they became the molecules of living cells is one of the central problems of biology. Chemistry can address part of it. Organic molecules can form from simple inorganic precursors under energy inputs such as electrical discharge, ultraviolet light or heat, and laboratory experiments have shown that many of the building blocks of life are accessible in this way.

Miller (1953) passed an electric discharge through a mixture of methane, ammonia, hydrogen and water vapour, intended to model a reducing early atmosphere. After a week, the products contained several amino acids, including glycine and alanine, along with other organic compounds. The experiment showed that amino acids can form from simple gases under plausible conditions, and it became a landmark in the study of the origin of life.

The experiment also has limits. The atmosphere of the early Earth is not known precisely, and the composition used in Miller's experiment may not have been representative. The yields of amino acids were modest, and the experiment did not produce the polymers, membranes or information-carrying molecules that life requires. Organic chemistry can show that building blocks can form. It cannot, by itself, show how they were assembled into a living system.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the RNA world hypothesis, in which RNA molecules may have carried both genetic information and catalytic activity before proteins and DNA took on their present roles. The hypothesis is an active research area and is supported by the catalytic properties of RNA and by the central role of ribosomal RNA in modern cells, but it is not established.

The origin of life is studied by combining laboratory chemistry with the geological record, phylogenetic analysis and models of prebiotic environments. Each approach supplies a constraint, and the most convincing accounts are those that satisfy several constraints at once.
