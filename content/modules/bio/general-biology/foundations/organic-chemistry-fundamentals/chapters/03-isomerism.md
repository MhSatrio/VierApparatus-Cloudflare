Isomers are molecules with the same molecular formula but different structures. Structural isomers differ in the way their atoms are connected. Glucose and fructose share the formula C6H12O6 but have different arrangements of carbonyl and hydroxyl groups, so they are different sugars with different metabolic roles. Structural isomers can differ greatly in reactivity, because the position of a functional group determines which reactions it can undergo.

Stereoisomers have the same connectivity but differ in the three-dimensional arrangement of their atoms. The most important type in biology is the enantiomer, a pair of molecules that are mirror images of each other and cannot be superimposed. A carbon atom bonded to four different groups is called a chiral centre, and its arrangement determines whether a molecule belongs to one enantiomeric series or the other. Alpha carbon atoms of amino acids are chiral, and almost all amino acids in proteins have the same configuration.

Pauling and Corey (1951) built their model of the protein backbone on the geometry of the amide bond and the planar arrangement of the atoms around it. The regularity of protein structure depends in part on the consistent handedness of its building blocks, since mixing enantiomers would produce chains that cannot fold into the same shapes.

Biological recognition depends on shape. Enzymes and receptors are themselves chiral, so they bind one enantiomer of a molecule far more strongly than the other, and the two can have very different effects. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how many drugs are active as a single enantiomer, and how the inactive form may be harmless, ineffective or harmful.

Isomers are separated by chiral chromatography, which uses a stationary phase that interacts differently with each enantiomer, and by polarimetry, which measures the rotation of plane-polarised light by a solution of a chiral compound. Both methods are standard tools in analytical chemistry and in the pharmaceutical industry.
