A phosphate group consists of a phosphorus atom bonded to four oxygen atoms, and at physiological pH it carries two negative charges. The phosphate groups of nucleotides and sugars are linked by ester and anhydride bonds. ATP, adenosine triphosphate, carries three phosphate groups in a chain, and the two terminal phosphoanhydride bonds are often described as high-energy bonds.

The term high-energy is misleading if taken literally. The energy released by ATP hydrolysis is large because the products are more stable than the reactants. The negative charges on the phosphates repel one another, the products are stabilised by resonance and by increased solvation, and the released phosphate is in a favourable ionic state. The energy is not stored in a bond that is strong in itself; it reflects the overall change in free energy of the reaction, as discussed in the chapter on chemical reactions and energy.

Phosphate transfer is the mechanism by which the energy of ATP drives other reactions. A phosphate group is moved onto a substrate, which then becomes more reactive, and the reaction it undergoes is coupled to the release of the phosphate. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how kinases use this mechanism to activate proteins and metabolites, and how the same energetic logic underlies the synthesis of macromolecules.

Lane and Martin (2010) argued that the energetic budget of a cell constrains its complexity, and ATP is the currency of that budget. The number of ATP molecules a cell can produce per unit time determines how many proteins it can make and maintain.

Phosphates and their derivatives are measured by enzymatic assays, which couple the conversion of ATP to a detectable signal, by chromatography, and by nuclear magnetic resonance of phosphorus, which can distinguish the phosphate groups in a mixture.
