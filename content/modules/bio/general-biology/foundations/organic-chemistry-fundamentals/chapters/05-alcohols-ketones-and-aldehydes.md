An alcohol contains a hydroxyl group attached to a saturated carbon. The class of the alcohol depends on how many other carbons are attached to the carbon that bears the hydroxyl: primary alcohols have one, secondary alcohols have two, and tertiary alcohols have three. The classes behave differently in oxidation, because primary alcohols can be oxidised to aldehydes and then to carboxylic acids, secondary alcohols can be oxidised to ketones, and tertiary alcohols resist oxidation under ordinary conditions.

Aldehydes and ketones both contain a carbonyl group, a carbon atom double-bonded to oxygen. In an aldehyde the carbonyl carbon is at the end of the chain and carries a hydrogen. In a ketone it lies within the chain and is bonded to two other carbons. The carbonyl carbon is electron-poor and is attacked by nucleophiles, which makes aldehydes and ketones reactive intermediates in metabolism.

Sugars combine these groups. Glucose is an aldehyde in its open-chain form and a ring in solution, where one of its hydroxyl groups has reacted with the carbonyl to form a cyclic hemiacetal. Fructose is a ketone in its open form. In aqueous solution the ring and open forms interconvert, so both are present in equilibrium.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how many metabolic reactions involve the oxidation or reduction of alcohols and carbonyls. During glycolysis, for example, glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate, an aldehyde, is oxidised to 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate, a carboxylic acid derivative, in a step that also reduces NAD+. Each step depends on the functional group at the reacting carbon.

Alcohols and carbonyls are identified by their infrared absorption and by chemical tests, such as the reaction of aldehydes with silver salts. In a laboratory these tests help distinguish sugars and other metabolites in complex mixtures.
