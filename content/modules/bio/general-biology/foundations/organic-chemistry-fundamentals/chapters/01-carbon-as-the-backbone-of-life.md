Carbon atoms can bond to up to four other atoms, and they can bond to one another to form chains, branches and rings of almost any length. A carbon chain can be saturated, with each carbon carrying as many hydrogen atoms as possible, or unsaturated, with double bonds in the chain. Lewis (1916) described the shared electron pair that makes such bonds possible, and the carbon skeleton is built from these shared pairs.

The skeleton is the frame to which the reactive groups of a molecule are attached. Its length and shape influence the physical properties of the molecule, such as its solubility, its melting point and how it packs into a membrane or a crystal. A short chain of carbon atoms is volatile, while a long chain is waxy or oily, which is why the fatty acids of lipids are long and the sugars of metabolism are short.

Carbon skeletons come in three main forms. Linear chains, such as those of fatty acids, can be straight or bent at double bonds. Branched chains, such as those of some amino acid side chains, have carbon atoms bonded to three or four other carbons. Rings, such as those of glucose and of the bases of nucleic acids, are closed loops of carbon and sometimes nitrogen or oxygen. Each form has a characteristic geometry and reactivity.

Carbon's versatility is not unique among the elements, but its combination of bond strength and stability in water is. Carbon-carbon bonds are strong enough to survive in aqueous solution, yet they can be broken by enzymes, which makes them suitable for the reversible reactions of metabolism. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the carbon skeleton of a metabolite is transformed step by step as it passes through a pathway, with each enzyme acting on a specific part of the molecule.

Chemists determine carbon skeletons by nuclear magnetic resonance, which identifies the carbon atoms and their neighbours, and by mass spectrometry, which gives the size of the molecule and the pieces into which it breaks. Together these methods reveal the frame on which biological chemistry is built.
