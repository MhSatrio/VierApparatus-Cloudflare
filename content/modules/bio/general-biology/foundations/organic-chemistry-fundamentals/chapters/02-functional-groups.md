A functional group is a specific arrangement of atoms that behaves in a predictable way wherever it appears. The carbon skeleton is largely inert, but the functional groups attached to it are where reactions take place. Recognising them is the most useful skill in organic chemistry, because the same group behaves similarly in a sugar, a fatty acid and an amino acid.

The hydroxyl group, an oxygen atom bonded to hydrogen, is polar and can form hydrogen bonds. It is found in alcohols and in sugars, and it can be oxidised or used to form new bonds. The carbonyl group, a carbon double-bonded to oxygen, is reactive at the carbon atom. It appears in aldehydes, which have the carbonyl at the end of a chain, and in ketones, which have it within the chain. The carboxyl group combines a carbonyl and a hydroxyl on the same carbon, and it is acidic because it can release a proton.

The amino group, a nitrogen atom bonded to two hydrogens, is basic because the nitrogen can accept a proton. It is found in amines and in the alpha carbon of amino acids. The phosphate group, a phosphorus atom bonded to four oxygens, is highly charged at physiological pH and forms high-energy bonds when linked to other molecules.

Alberts et al. (2022) present these groups as the working vocabulary of cell chemistry. Enzymes recognise functional groups rather than whole molecules, so an enzyme that oxidises a hydroxyl group in one sugar will often act on the same group in another. The conservation of reactive groups is one of the reasons metabolic pathways can be understood by chemical logic.

Functional groups are identified by infrared spectroscopy, which detects characteristic vibrations of bonds such as carbonyl and hydroxyl, and by nuclear magnetic resonance, which shows the chemical environment of each hydrogen and carbon. These signatures allow the groups in an unknown molecule to be assigned.
