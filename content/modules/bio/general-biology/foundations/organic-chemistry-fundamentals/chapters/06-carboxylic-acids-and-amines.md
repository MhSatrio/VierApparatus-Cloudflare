A carboxylic acid contains a carboxyl group, which is a carbonyl with a hydroxyl attached to the same carbon. The hydroxyl proton is released in water, leaving a carboxylate ion. Carboxylic acids are weak acids, with pKa values usually in the range of four to five. Fatty acids, the acetate of metabolism and the citrate of the citric acid cycle are all carboxylic acids, and their ionisation state depends on the pH of their surroundings.

An amine contains a nitrogen atom bonded to one, two or three carbon atoms. The nitrogen has a lone pair of electrons that can accept a proton, so amines are bases. Their basicity depends on the substituents attached to the nitrogen, and the amino group of an amino acid is usually protonated at physiological pH, carrying a positive charge.

Amino acids contain both groups, so they are amphoteric: they can act as acids or bases. In solution an amino acid carries a carboxylate and a protonated amine, which gives it a net charge that changes with pH. At a particular pH, called the isoelectric point, the net charge is zero. Pauling and Corey (1951) built the protein backbone from the amide bond, which forms when the carboxyl of one amino acid reacts with the amino group of the next and releases water.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the ionisation of these groups controls the solubility and binding of amino acids and peptides. A peptide that is charged at one pH may be neutral at another, and this change can alter its interaction with membranes and receptors.

The acidity and basicity of these groups are measured by titration, and their pKa values are reported as part of the characterisation of any new amino acid derivative. The same measurements allow chemists to predict the charge of a molecule under specific conditions before testing it biologically.
