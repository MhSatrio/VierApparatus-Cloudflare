The cell is the smallest unit that is generally regarded as alive. It is bounded by a membrane, it carries genetic information, and it can carry out metabolism and reproduce. Hooke (1665) named these units after the small rooms of a monastery when he observed the structure of cork, and his name stuck even though the living contents of cells were not seen for another century. The cell theory that developed in the nineteenth century holds that all living things are made of cells and that cells arise from existing cells.

Cells fall into two broad groups. Prokaryotic cells, found in bacteria and archaea, lack a nucleus and most internal membranes. Their genetic material lies in a region of the cytoplasm called the nucleoid. Eukaryotic cells, found in animals, plants, fungi and protists, have a nucleus enclosed by a membrane and many membrane-bound organelles. The difference is large, and most biologists regard it as one of the deepest divisions in the living world.

Size also differs. Typical bacterial cells are a few micrometres in length, while many eukaryotic cells are an order of magnitude larger. The difference in size affects how molecules move inside the cell. In a large eukaryotic cell, diffusion alone is too slow to deliver materials across the whole cell, so transport is organised by membranes and the cytoskeleton.

Multicellular organisms contain many cell types. A neuron has long processes for signalling, a red blood cell has lost its nucleus and carries oxygen, and a plant guard cell changes its volume to open and close pores in the leaf. The cell types share the basic architecture of their domain but are specialised for their functions. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how these specialisations arise from differences in gene expression during development, even though the cells of one organism carry the same genome.

Biologists classify cells using microscopy, staining, flow cytometry and genomic markers. These methods show where the boundaries between cell types lie, and they reveal the continuum of forms that lies between the familiar examples.
