An ecosystem is a community together with the physical and chemical environment in which it lives. Its biotic components are the organisms, and its abiotic components are the water, air, light, minerals and temperature that they depend on. Energy enters most ecosystems as sunlight captured by photosynthesis, and it is passed through food chains as organisms eat one another. Matter is recycled: nutrients taken up by plants return to the soil through decomposition, and carbon moves between the atmosphere, the oceans and living things.

An ecosystem is defined by its flows of energy and matter as much as by its species. A lake, a forest and a coral reef each have different producers, consumers and decomposers, but each exchanges energy and nutrients with its surroundings. Ecologists measure these flows by tracking the uptake and release of carbon and nitrogen, and by estimating the production of biomass at each trophic level.

The biosphere is the sum of all ecosystems on Earth, together with the region of the atmosphere, land and ocean in which life exists. It is the largest level of biological organization. Its properties include the global cycling of gases, the distribution of biomes across latitudes and altitudes, and the composition of the atmosphere, which has been changed by the metabolism of living organisms over billions of years.

Lane and Martin (2010) considered energy as a constraint that shapes life at the cellular level, and the same kind of energetic constraint applies to ecosystems. The amount of energy captured by producers limits how many consumers an ecosystem can support, and the efficiency of energy transfer between trophic levels is typically low.

Biosphere-level questions are studied by combining satellite observations, long-term monitoring networks and computer models. These approaches allow ecologists to ask how ecosystems respond to climate change, pollution and land use, and to connect local processes to the global system they belong to.
