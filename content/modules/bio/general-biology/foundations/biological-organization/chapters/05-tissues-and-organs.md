A tissue is a group of cells that share a common origin and structure and work together to carry out a function. Animal bodies are built from four principal tissue types. Epithelial tissue forms linings and covers, such as the skin and the lining of the gut, and it controls what passes between the body and the outside. Connective tissue supports and binds other tissues, and includes bone, cartilage, fat and blood. Muscle tissue contracts, and nervous tissue conducts electrical signals. Plants have their own tissues, including the vascular tissues that carry water and sugars.

Tissues are held together by cell adhesion molecules, by the extracellular matrix that cells secrete, and by junctions that link neighbouring cells. These connections allow the tissue to resist mechanical stress and to coordinate the behaviour of its members. Epithelial sheets, for example, are held together by tight junctions that limit the passage of molecules between cells.

An organ combines several tissues into a structure with a specific function. The stomach contains epithelium that secretes acid and mucus, smooth muscle that churns the contents, and connective tissue and nerves that support and regulate it. The organ does more than the sum of its tissues. Its function depends on the arrangement of the tissues, the way they are supplied with blood and nerves, and the way they respond to signals from the rest of the body.

Koshland (2002) described compartmentalisation as one of the principles of living systems. Organs illustrate the same principle at a larger scale, since each organ isolates a particular set of activities within the body and regulates its exchange with the rest of the organism.

Biologists study tissues by histology, in which thin sections are stained and examined, and by immunolabelling, which reveals the distribution of specific proteins. Organs are studied by physiology, measuring the function of an organ in intact animals or in isolated preparations, and by imaging techniques that show their internal structure.
