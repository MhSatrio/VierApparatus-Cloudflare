A population is a group of individuals of the same species living in the same place at the same time and able to interbreed. Population biologists measure its size, density, age structure, birth rate and death rate. These are properties of the group rather than of any member. An individual has an age, but only the population has an age distribution. An individual may die, but only the population can decline.

A community is a group of populations of different species that live in the same area and interact. Predators and prey, plants and their pollinators, and parasites and their hosts are all community relationships. Community properties include species richness, the number of species present, and the diversity that combines richness with the relative abundance of each species. These measures describe the community as a whole, and no single population can display them.

Dobzhansky (1973) argued that the diversity of organisms cannot be understood without recognising the populations in which genetic variation is shaped and sorted. Populations are the units in which evolution occurs, because selection acts on the traits of individuals but changes the frequencies of traits in populations over generations.

Interactions within communities produce effects that no single species produces alone. The removal of a predator can allow its prey to increase and to reduce the plants on which it feeds. Such trophic cascades show that community structure depends on the connections among populations, not only on the properties of each one.

Ecologists study populations by counting individuals, marking and recapturing animals, and measuring growth rates. They study communities by surveying species, sampling their abundance and mapping their interactions. The methods differ, but both aim to describe a level of organization that is larger than the individual and smaller than the ecosystem.
