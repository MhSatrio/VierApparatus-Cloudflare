An organelle is a specialised structure within a cell that carries out a particular function. Eukaryotic cells contain many organelles, most of them bounded by membranes. The nucleus holds the genome and is the site of transcription. Mitochondria produce much of the cell's ATP through oxidative phosphorylation. The endoplasmic reticulum synthesises lipids and many proteins, and the Golgi apparatus modifies, sorts and packages them. Lysosomes break down material taken into the cell or recycled from within it. Peroxisomes carry out oxidative reactions that produce and destroy hydrogen peroxide.

Membranes divide the cell into compartments, and each compartment can hold a different chemical environment. The lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum has a different composition from the cytosol, and the interior of a lysosome is acidic. Koshland (2002) included compartmentalisation among the principles of living systems, because it allows incompatible reactions to run side by side without interfering with each other.

Some organelles are not bounded by membranes. Ribosomes, which build proteins, are large assemblies of RNA and protein. Stress granules and other condensates form transiently by the aggregation of proteins and RNAs. These structures show that membranes are not the only way to organise a cell's chemistry, although membrane-bound compartments remain the dominant strategy in eukaryotes.

Organelles are inherited and divide. Mitochondria and chloroplasts contain their own DNA, which is thought to reflect their origin from once free-living bacteria, a view supported by their genetic and biochemical similarity to bacteria. The idea is central to the endosymbiotic theory and to much of modern cell evolution.

Investigators identify organelles by light and electron microscopy, by fluorescent labelling of specific proteins, and by fractionation, in which cells are broken open and their components separated by centrifugation. Each method reveals a different aspect of the organelle's identity and function.
