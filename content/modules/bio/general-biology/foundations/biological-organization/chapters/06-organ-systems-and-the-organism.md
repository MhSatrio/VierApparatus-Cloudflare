An organ system is a set of organs that together perform a major function of the organism. The human body has about a dozen recognised systems. The digestive system breaks down food and absorbs nutrients. The circulatory system distributes oxygen, nutrients and signalling molecules. The respiratory system exchanges gases with the air. The nervous and endocrine systems coordinate the others, the musculoskeletal system provides movement and support, the immune system defends against pathogens, and the urinary system regulates fluid and waste.

Systems are linked by exchange. The digestive and circulatory systems deliver nutrients to tissues, and the respiratory and circulatory systems deliver oxygen. The urinary system removes waste that the other systems produce. These links make the organism an integrated whole, in which a failure in one system affects the others. Kidney failure, for example, raises the concentration of potassium in the blood and disturbs the function of the heart and nerves.

Coordination depends on signalling. Nerves carry rapid electrical messages, and hormones carry slower chemical messages through the blood. Feedback loops, which are discussed in the Characteristics of Life module, keep the systems working within ranges that the whole body can tolerate.

Organisms are themselves units of evolution. Dobzhansky (1973) argued that the structure of an organism reflects its history, and that the organ systems of different animals are variations on shared designs. The lungs of a mammal and the gills of a fish solve the same problem of gas exchange, and the comparison of their structures reveals the evolutionary origin of each.

Physiologists study organ systems by measuring blood pressure, heart rate, breathing and kidney function in intact animals, and by isolating organs and recording their responses in controlled conditions. The integrated function of the organism is the outcome that all of these measurements are ultimately trying to explain.
