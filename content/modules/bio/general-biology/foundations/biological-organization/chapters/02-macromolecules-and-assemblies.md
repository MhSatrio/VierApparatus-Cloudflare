Four classes of large molecules dominate living systems: polysaccharides, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids. Polysaccharides are chains of sugar units, such as the starch stored by plants and the glycogen stored by animals. Proteins are chains of amino acids. Nucleic acids are chains of nucleotides. Lipids are not polymers in the strict sense, but they form the hydrophobic sheets that make membranes possible. Each class is built by linking smaller units, usually with the loss of a water molecule at each step.

The shape and chemistry of a macromolecule determine its function. A protein that binds oxygen must have a pocket of the right size, and a nucleic acid that stores information must have a sequence that can be read reliably. Kendrew and colleagues (1960) determined the three-dimensional structure of myoglobin, the oxygen-binding protein of muscle, and showed that its fold created a pocket for the haem group. Their work demonstrated that a macromolecule's function could be understood from its atomic arrangement.

Macromolecules rarely act alone. They assemble into complexes that carry out coordinated tasks. Ribosomes are complexes of ribosomal RNA and proteins that build other proteins. Membranes are assemblies of lipids and proteins that separate the cell from its surroundings. Cytoskeletal filaments are polymers of protein subunits that can be assembled and disassembled to change cell shape.

Assembly is governed by the same interactions that stabilise individual molecules, including hydrogen bonds, electrostatic attraction, hydrophobic effects and van der Waals contacts. The strength of these interactions is usually modest, so assemblies can form and dissolve in response to changing conditions. This reversibility is one reason cells can change their structure quickly.

Biologists study assemblies by a combination of methods. Crystallography and electron microscopy reveal their structure, mass spectrometry identifies their components, and genetic experiments test which parts are required for function. Together these approaches show how a collection of molecules becomes a machine.
