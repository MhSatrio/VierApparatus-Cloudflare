The bulk of every living organism is made from a small group of elements. Hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus and sulfur account for the great majority of the atoms in cells, with smaller amounts of sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, chlorine and iron, and traces of many others. Carbon is the most important, because it can form stable bonds with itself and with the other common elements, producing the long and branched structures on which biology depends.

Atoms combine into molecules, which are the smallest stable units of most chemical compounds. Water is made from two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. Glucose contains six carbon atoms, twelve hydrogen atoms and six oxygen atoms. Ammonia, carbon dioxide, and phosphate ions are other common small molecules. Their size and shape determine how they interact with one another and with larger molecules.

Lewis (1916) developed a model of the chemical bond in which atoms share or transfer electrons to complete their outer shells. Although later theory refined the picture, the idea that molecules form through the rearrangement of electrons remains the basis for understanding how small biological molecules behave.

Small molecules serve as building blocks. Amino acids are joined into proteins, nucleotides into nucleic acids, and sugars into polysaccharides. Each of these reactions releases or uses energy and removes or adds water. The small molecules therefore set the vocabulary of the cell: the rest of biochemistry is largely the chemistry of combining and separating them.

Biologists identify small molecules by mass spectrometry, nuclear magnetic resonance and chromatography, and they determine their atomic composition by elemental analysis. These methods give a precise inventory of the chemical parts from which the cell is built.
