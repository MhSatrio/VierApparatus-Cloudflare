Every reaction must pass through an energy barrier, called the activation energy, before reactants can become products. Molecules must collide with enough energy to distort their bonds, and the fraction of collisions that succeed rises steeply with temperature. Arrhenius showed that the rate constant of a reaction depends exponentially on the ratio of activation energy to temperature, and this relationship explains why reactions in a warm cell proceed far faster than in a cold one.

A catalyst lowers the activation energy of a reaction without changing its free energy change. It therefore speeds the reaction in both directions, and it does not alter the equilibrium position. Enzymes are biological catalysts, most of them proteins, that can increase reaction rates by many orders of magnitude. They do so by binding substrates in a precise arrangement that stabilises the transition state, the unstable intermediate on the path to products.

Enzyme structure shows how this works. Kendrew and colleagues (1960) determined the structure of myoglobin, a protein that binds a small molecule in a pocket, and their work demonstrated that the function of a protein can be understood from the shape of its binding site. Enzymes have similar pockets, lined with residues that position substrates and provide the chemical groups needed to break and form bonds.

Enzymes are not consumed by the reactions they catalyse. A single enzyme molecule can process many substrate molecules per second, and this turnover is one of the features that makes metabolism economical. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how cells regulate enzyme activity by controlling their concentration, their location and their chemical modification, which allows the rate of a pathway to respond to the needs of the cell.

Reaction rates are measured by following the disappearance of substrates or the appearance of products over time. Enzyme kinetics, the study of these rates, characterises how fast a catalyst works and how its speed depends on the concentration of substrate, which is one of the most useful descriptions in biochemistry.
