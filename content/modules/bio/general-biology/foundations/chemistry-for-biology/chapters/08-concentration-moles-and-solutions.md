Chemists count atoms and molecules in moles. One mole is 6.022 times 10 to the power 23 particles, a number called Avogadro's constant. A mole of glucose has a mass equal to its molar mass in grams, which is about 180 grams because glucose is C6H12O6. The mole allows a chemist to relate the mass of a substance to the number of molecules it contains, and it is the basis of every quantitative reaction in biochemistry.

Concentration expresses how much solute is dissolved in a given volume of solution. Molar concentration is the number of moles per litre, written as M. A solution of 0.1 M glucose contains 0.1 moles of glucose in each litre. Other units appear in biology, such as micrograms per millilitre for proteins and nanomoles per litre for hormones, but molarity is the common reference, because it links directly to reaction rates and equilibrium constants.

Many experiments require dilution. A stock solution is diluted by adding solvent, and the concentration after dilution is the original concentration multiplied by the fraction of the stock that remains. A ten-fold dilution of a 1 M solution gives 0.1 M. Serial dilution repeats this step to produce a series of concentrations spanning several orders of magnitude, which is how the response of a cell to a drug is often measured.

Lewis (1916) described the molecule as the unit of chemical identity, and the mole translates that identity into measurable quantities. Alberts et al. (2022) note that the concentrations of molecules inside a cell are often in the micromolar to millimolar range, and that these concentrations determine whether a binding interaction is likely to occur under physiological conditions.

Accurate solutions require accurate balances, volumetric glassware, and correct calculation of the molar mass. Errors in these steps propagate into every subsequent result, so experienced investigators check their stock concentrations and record the preparation of each solution in the laboratory notebook.
