The electrons of an atom occupy shells, and the outermost shell determines the chemistry of the element. Hydrogen has one electron and needs one more to fill its shell, so it forms a single bond. Carbon has four valence electrons and forms four bonds. Nitrogen has five and forms three bonds in neutral molecules, oxygen has six and forms two, and phosphorus can form five bonds in the phosphate groups that carry energy in cells. Lewis (1916) gave the principle that underlies these counts: atoms tend to acquire the stable arrangement of a full outer shell by sharing or exchanging electrons.

The number of bonds an atom can form gives it a shape. Carbon's four bonds can point toward the corners of a tetrahedron, so chains of carbon atoms can bend, branch and form rings. A chain of carbon atoms with hydrogen attached can therefore take many forms, and the variety of such forms is the basis of organic chemistry. Oxygen's two bonds create the bent shape of water, and nitrogen's three bonds create the planar groups found in amino acids and nucleotide bases.

Carbon is especially suited to life because its bonds are strong and stable, but not so strong that they cannot be broken by biological catalysts. Carbon-carbon and carbon-hydrogen bonds are strong enough to hold molecules together in an aqueous environment, and they can be cleaved or formed by enzymes at useful rates. Silicon lies in the same group of the periodic table and forms four bonds, but its bonds to oxygen are very stable, which limits its use in the reversible reactions that living systems require.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the central role of carbon in building the molecules of cells. The consequence for the student is that a diagram of a biological molecule is best read by first counting the bonds on each atom. If the count is wrong, the diagram is wrong, and most biochemical errors begin with an unexpected valence.

Chemists represent electron arrangements with Lewis structures, which show valence electrons as dots or lines between atoms. The representation is a model, not a picture of reality, but it reliably predicts the number and arrangement of bonds, and it is the notation used throughout biochemistry.
