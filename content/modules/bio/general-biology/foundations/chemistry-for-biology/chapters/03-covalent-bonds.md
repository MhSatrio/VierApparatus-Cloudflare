A covalent bond forms when two atoms share one or more pairs of electrons. Each shared pair is held between the two nuclei, and the attraction of both nuclei for the shared electrons holds the atoms together. The bond between two carbon atoms is strong, and the bond between carbon and hydrogen is similarly strong. These bonds form the framework of nearly every molecule in a cell, including sugars, fatty acids, amino acids and nucleotides.

Covalent bonds differ in how equally the electrons are shared. When two atoms have the same attraction for electrons, as with two carbon atoms, the bond is nonpolar and the electrons are shared evenly. When the atoms differ, the electrons spend more time near the more attractive atom, and the bond is polar. The oxygen-hydrogen bond in water is polar, with a partial negative charge on the oxygen and a partial positive charge on the hydrogen. Lewis (1916) gave the basic picture of shared electron pairs, and the notion of polarity was developed from it.

Polarity determines how a molecule interacts with water. Polar and charged groups form favourable contacts with water, so they are described as hydrophilic. Nonpolar groups, such as long hydrocarbon chains, do not interact well with water and tend to cluster together, which is the basis of the hydrophobic effect that organises membranes and folds proteins.

Covalent bonds are not normally broken by the weak thermal energy of a cell at body temperature. Breaking them usually requires an enzyme or a chemical reaction that supplies energy, which is why covalent bonds give biological molecules a durable structure. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how cells build macromolecules by forming covalent bonds between monomers and how they break those bonds during digestion.

Chemists determine covalent structures by mass spectrometry, which gives the molecular mass and fragments, and by nuclear magnetic resonance, which reveals which atoms are bonded to which. Together these methods allow a structure to be assigned with confidence.
