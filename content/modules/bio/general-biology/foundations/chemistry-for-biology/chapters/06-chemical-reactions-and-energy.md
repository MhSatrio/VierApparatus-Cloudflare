A chemical reaction changes one set of molecules into another. Whether it proceeds spontaneously depends on the change in free energy, written as delta G. A reaction with a negative delta G releases free energy and can proceed without an external supply; it is called exergonic. A reaction with a positive delta G requires energy to proceed and is called endergonic. Free energy combines the change in enthalpy, which is the heat released or absorbed, with the change in entropy, which is the change in disorder, weighted by temperature.

The sign of delta G says whether a reaction can proceed, but not how fast it will proceed. Glucose can be oxidised to carbon dioxide and water with a large negative delta G, yet a solution of glucose in air does not burn appreciably at room temperature. The reaction is thermodynamically favourable and kinetically slow. Living cells exploit this distinction: they keep fuels stable until enzymes are present to release their energy in controlled steps.

Cells make endergonic reactions proceed by coupling them to exergonic ones. The hydrolysis of ATP releases a large amount of free energy, and when the phosphate is transferred to another molecule, that molecule becomes more reactive and can take part in a reaction that would otherwise not occur. Alberts et al. (2022) describe this coupling as the central mechanism by which cells transfer energy from catabolism to anabolism.

Energy budgets constrain cell biology. Lane and Martin (2010) argued that the energy available to a cell limits how many proteins it can maintain, which in turn constrains its complexity. Reactions that release little energy must be used sparingly, and the metabolic network is shaped by the need to get the most work from each unit of fuel.

Biochemists measure free energy changes by calorimetry, by equilibrium constants, which relate delta G to the ratio of products and reactants, and by electrochemical methods for reactions that transfer electrons. These measurements allow the energetic feasibility of a pathway to be assessed before it is studied experimentally.
