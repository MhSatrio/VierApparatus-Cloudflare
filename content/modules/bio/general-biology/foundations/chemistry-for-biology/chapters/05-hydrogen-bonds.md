A hydrogen bond is an attraction between a hydrogen atom that carries a partial positive charge, usually one already bonded to oxygen or nitrogen, and a nearby oxygen or nitrogen atom that carries a partial negative charge. It is not a true covalent bond, and it is considerably weaker, but it is directional and it forms in large numbers in living systems. A single hydrogen bond is easily broken by thermal motion, while many together can hold a structure firmly.

Water provides the clearest example. Each water molecule can form up to four hydrogen bonds with its neighbours, and these bonds give liquid water a high boiling point, a high heat capacity and a density that falls on freezing, which is why ice floats. Ball (2008) argued that the hydrogen-bonded network of water is not a passive background for cell chemistry but an active participant that influences the shape and behaviour of biological molecules.

Proteins depend on hydrogen bonds to form their secondary structures. In an alpha helix, hydrogen bonds link the backbone of one turn to the next, and in a beta sheet they link adjacent strands. Pauling and Corey (1951) described the pleated sheet as a layer configuration that allows backbone hydrogen bonds to form between strands, and their model helped establish the principles of protein structure.

Nucleic acids use hydrogen bonds to pair their bases. Watson and Crick (1953) showed that adenine pairs with thymine through two hydrogen bonds and guanine pairs with cytosine through three. The specificity of these pairs allows the two strands of DNA to be separated and copied, and it is the chemical basis of heredity.

Because hydrogen bonds are weak, they can be broken and reformed quickly. This reversibility allows proteins to change shape and nucleic acids to open and close during replication and transcription, and it is an essential feature of biological function. The measurement of hydrogen bonds uses X-ray and neutron diffraction, along with infrared spectroscopy, which detects the stretching of bonded hydrogen atoms.
