An atom consists of a nucleus, which holds protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons. The number of protons defines the element: every atom with six protons is carbon, and every atom with eight is oxygen. The number of neutrons can vary without changing the element. Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons are called isotopes. Carbon-12 has six neutrons, carbon-13 has seven, and carbon-14 has eight. Carbon-14 is radioactive and decays slowly, while carbon-12 and carbon-13 are stable.

Electrons are arranged in shells around the nucleus. The electrons of the outer shell, called valence electrons, determine how an atom bonds with others. Lewis (1916) proposed that atoms tend to share or transfer electrons so that their outer shells are filled, and that the shared electrons form the chemical bond. This idea is still the starting point for understanding the molecules of life.

Isotopes are valuable in biology because they behave chemically almost identically to the common form of the element, but they can be detected. A molecule that contains carbon-14 can be followed through a metabolic pathway by measuring its radioactivity in each product. Stable isotopes such as carbon-13 and nitrogen-15 can be detected by mass spectrometry or nuclear magnetic resonance without radiation, which makes them suitable for studies in humans.

The method has a long history. Early tracer experiments revealed the routes by which nutrients enter and leave cells, and the same logic is used today in metabolomics and in measurements of protein turnover. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how labelled precursors are used to follow the synthesis of macromolecules in cells, and how the rate of incorporation reveals the rate of production.

Working with isotopes requires care. Radioactive compounds must be handled with appropriate shielding and disposal, and the dose and duration of exposure must be controlled. In studies of living organisms, the isotope must be introduced in a way that does not disturb the system being studied, since a labelled molecule that changes the reaction it is supposed to reveal gives misleading results.
