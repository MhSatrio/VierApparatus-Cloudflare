A eukaryotic cell is larger and more complex than a prokaryotic cell. Its defining feature is the nucleus, a compartment bounded by a double membrane that contains the chromosomes. The nuclear envelope has pores that regulate the traffic of molecules between the nucleus and the cytoplasm, and the nucleolus within it is the site where ribosomal RNA is made and ribosomes are assembled.

The cytoplasm contains a network of protein filaments called the cytoskeleton. Microtubules form tracks along which motor proteins carry vesicles and organelles, and they form the spindle that separates chromosomes during division. Actin filaments lie beneath the plasma membrane and drive changes in shape and movement. Intermediate filaments provide mechanical strength. Koshland (2002) included compartmentalisation among the organising principles of living systems, and the cytoskeleton adds a dynamic layer of organisation on top of the membranes.

Eukaryotic cells carry many membrane-bound organelles, including the endoplasmic reticulum, the Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, peroxisomes, mitochondria and, in plants and algae, chloroplasts. Each organelle has its own set of enzymes and transporters, and the cell moves materials between them through vesicles and membrane contact sites.

Eukaryotic genomes are packaged into linear chromosomes that are bound to histones, forming chromatin. The packaging regulates which genes can be read, and it allows very long DNA molecules to fit inside the nucleus.

The structure of eukaryotic cells is studied by electron microscopy, by fluorescent labelling of organelles and cytoskeletal proteins, and by genetic tools that remove or tag specific components. These methods reveal how the parts relate to one another in living cells.
