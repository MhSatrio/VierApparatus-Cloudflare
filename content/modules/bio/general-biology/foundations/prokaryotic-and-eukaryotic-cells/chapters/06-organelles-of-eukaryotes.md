The organelles of a eukaryotic cell divide its metabolism into distinct compartments. The endoplasmic reticulum is a network of membrane sheets and tubules that synthesises lipids and many proteins, and it removes toxic compounds in some cells. The Golgi apparatus receives proteins from the endoplasmic reticulum and modifies, sorts and dispatches them. Lysosomes contain hydrolytic enzymes that digest material delivered to them. Peroxisomes carry out oxidation reactions that generate and destroy hydrogen peroxide.

Mitochondria carry out respiration and produce most of the cell's ATP in animals and fungi. Each mitochondrion has an outer and an inner membrane, and the inner membrane is folded into cristae that increase its surface. Plant and algal cells also contain chloroplasts, which perform photosynthesis within a system of internal membranes called thylakoids.

Koshland (2002) described compartmentalisation as one of the principles of living systems, and eukaryotic organelles are its most elaborate expression. Each compartment can hold a different chemistry, and the enzymes inside it can work without interference from reactions elsewhere.

The compartments are connected. Vesicles bud from one organelle and fuse with another, carrying proteins and lipids between them. Membrane contact sites allow direct exchange of lipids and ions without vesicles. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how these routes are guided by specific tags on the membranes and by the machinery that recognises them.

Organelles are identified by their contents, by fluorescent markers, and by fractionation, in which cells are broken open and their components separated by centrifugation. Each method reveals a part of the picture, and together they define what each organelle does.
