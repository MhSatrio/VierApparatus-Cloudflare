Mitochondria and chloroplasts contain their own DNA, have their own ribosomes resembling those of bacteria, and divide by a process similar to binary fission. These features suggest that both organelles were once free-living bacteria that were taken into a host cell. This is the endosymbiotic hypothesis, and it is one of the most widely accepted explanations for the origin of eukaryotic organelles.

The genetic evidence is strong. Sequencing of mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes shows that they are most closely related to specific groups of bacteria, and phylogenetic analysis places mitochondria within the alphaproteobacteria and chloroplasts within the cyanobacteria. Much of the genetic material of the original bacteria has been transferred to the host nucleus over time, which is why modern organelles carry only a small genome.

The origin of the host itself is less clear. Woese and colleagues (1990) established that eukaryotes are related to archaea in the ribosomal RNA tree, which suggests that the host lineage had an archaeal character. Hug and colleagues (2016) reported a broader survey of microbial genomes, which revealed lineages that share some features with eukaryotes. Their work suggests that the eukaryotic cell may have emerged within a more diverse microbial world than was previously recognised.

Lane and Martin (2010) argued that the energetic consequence of acquiring mitochondria was central to the origin of complex cells, since the new energy supply allowed a larger genome and more complex organisation. Their hypothesis links the endosymbiotic event to the energetics of cell size, as described in the previous chapter.

The origin of the eukaryotic cell is still debated. The steps by which the host acquired its membranes, its nucleus and its cytoskeleton are not fully known, and different scenarios remain under test. The question is one of the most important open problems in the evolution of cells.
