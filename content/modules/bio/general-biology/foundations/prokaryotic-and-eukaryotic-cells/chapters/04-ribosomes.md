Ribosomes are the machines that translate messenger RNA into protein. Each one consists of a large and a small subunit, and each subunit is made of ribosomal RNA together with ribosomal proteins. The small subunit reads the messenger RNA, matching each three-base codon with the transfer RNA that carries the corresponding amino acid. The large subunit catalyses the formation of peptide bonds between the amino acids as the chain grows.

Ribosomal RNA does most of the catalytic work. The peptidyl transferase reaction that joins amino acids is carried out by RNA in the large subunit, which is a striking fact because it implies that an RNA molecule can act as an enzyme. Woese and colleagues (1990) used the sequences of ribosomal RNA to classify life into domains, and that choice reflects the central and conserved role of the ribosome in every cell.

Prokaryotic ribosomes are smaller than eukaryotic ribosomes, and their sizes are usually described by sedimentation coefficients, measured in Svedberg units. The structural differences are enough that many antibiotics, including some widely used ones, bind bacterial ribosomes and block protein synthesis without affecting the ribosomes of eukaryotic cells. This selectivity makes the bacterial ribosome an important drug target.

In eukaryotes, ribosomes are found free in the cytoplasm, attached to the endoplasmic reticulum, and inside mitochondria and chloroplasts, which have their own ribosomes resembling those of bacteria.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how ribosomes are assembled in the nucleolus and in the bacterial cytoplasm from RNA and proteins that fold and bind in a defined sequence. Ribosomes are studied by cryo-electron microscopy and by biochemical fractionation, which separates the subunits and their complexes.
