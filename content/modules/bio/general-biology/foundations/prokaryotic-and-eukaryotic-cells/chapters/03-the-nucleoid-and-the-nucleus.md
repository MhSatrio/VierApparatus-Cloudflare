The genetic material of every cell is DNA, but its arrangement differs between prokaryotes and eukaryotes. In a prokaryote, the chromosome is a single circle that occupies a region of the cytoplasm called the nucleoid. Proteins and supercoiling compact the DNA, but nothing separates it from the ribosomes that translate its messages. Transcription and translation can therefore occur at the same time, with a ribosome beginning to make a protein while the RNA is still being produced.

In eukaryotes the chromosomes are kept inside the nucleus, enclosed by the nuclear envelope. Transcription takes place inside the nucleus, and the resulting messenger RNA must be processed and exported through nuclear pores before it can be translated in the cytoplasm. The separation adds a step of control, since the cell can regulate which RNAs leave the nucleus and when.

Watson and Crick (1953) described the double helix whose strands can be separated and copied, and in both cell types the helix must be unwound during transcription and replication. The difference lies in how the helix is packaged and how the processes are compartmentalised. In eukaryotes, DNA is wrapped around histone proteins to form nucleosomes, which are then folded into higher-order chromatin.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the nuclear envelope also protects the genome from the cytoplasm, which contains enzymes that could damage DNA, and how the nucleus is reorganised during division when the envelope breaks down and reforms.

The nucleoid and the nucleus are studied by fluorescent staining of DNA, by electron microscopy and by chromosome conformation methods that reveal which regions of the genome lie close together in space.
