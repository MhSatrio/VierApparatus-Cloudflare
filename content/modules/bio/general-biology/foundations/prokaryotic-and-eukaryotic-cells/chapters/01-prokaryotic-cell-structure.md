A typical prokaryotic cell is enclosed by a plasma membrane and usually by a rigid cell wall. In bacteria the wall is built from peptidoglycan, a mesh of sugar chains cross-linked by short peptides. Archaea have walls of different chemistry, and their membranes contain lipids that differ from those of bacteria and eukaryotes. The wall resists the internal pressure of the cell, which is often several times that of the atmosphere, and it gives the cell its shape.

The interior holds the genetic material in a region called the nucleoid, which is not enclosed by a membrane. The nucleoid contains a single circular chromosome in most species, along with proteins that help to fold and organise it. Many prokaryotes also carry small circular pieces of DNA called plasmids, which can be transferred between cells.

Ribosomes are scattered through the cytoplasm. They are smaller than eukaryotic ribosomes, and their differences are the basis of several antibiotics that selectively block bacterial protein synthesis. Some prokaryotes contain internal membrane systems, such as the photosynthetic membranes of cyanobacteria, but these are not organised into the organelles found in eukaryotes.

Woese and colleagues (1990) used the sequence of ribosomal RNA to show that the prokaryotes form two distinct groups, bacteria and archaea, which differ fundamentally in their molecular machinery. The structural similarity of their cells masked a deep evolutionary difference.

Prokaryotic cells are studied by staining, by microscopy and by genomic analysis. Their small size and simple structure make them convenient for experiments, and many of the basic mechanisms of cell biology were first discovered in bacteria.
