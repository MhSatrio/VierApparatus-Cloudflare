Many prokaryotes and some eukaryotes surround their plasma membrane with a cell wall. The wall resists the osmotic pressure that would otherwise cause the cell to swell and burst, and it gives the cell a defined shape. In bacteria the wall is built from peptidoglycan, a polymer of sugars cross-linked by peptides, which forms a mesh that encloses the whole cell.

Bacteria are divided into two broad groups by the structure of their envelope. Gram-positive bacteria have a thick peptidoglycan layer and no outer membrane. Gram-negative bacteria have a thin peptidoglycan layer surrounded by an outer membrane that contains lipopolysaccharide. The outer membrane is an additional barrier, and it is one reason that many Gram-negative bacteria resist antibiotics. The distinction is named after the staining method that Hans Christian Gram introduced, which reveals the difference in wall structure.

Archaea have walls that are chemically different from those of bacteria. Many archaeal walls contain pseudopeptidoglycan or proteins, and some archaea have no wall at all. Woese and colleagues (1990) showed that the differences between archaeal and bacterial cells reflect deep evolutionary separation, which the wall chemistry illustrates.

Plant cells have walls made mainly of cellulose, and fungi have walls of chitin. These walls are not made from peptidoglycan, and they are built by different enzymes. The wall of each organism is an example of a structure whose chemistry reflects its lineage.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how walls are synthesised and remodelled as the cell grows, and how antibiotics that inhibit wall synthesis act selectively on bacteria because the target enzymes are absent from animal cells. Wall structure is studied by electron microscopy, by chemical analysis of its components and by staining.
