Prokaryotic cells are typically a few micrometres across, while many eukaryotic cells are ten to a hundred times larger in diameter and a thousand or more times larger in volume. The difference is partly a matter of geometry, as discussed in the chapter on cell size in the Introduction to Cells module, and partly a matter of internal organisation. Eukaryotic cells divide their interior into compartments, which allows them to exceed the transport limits that constrain a single open space.

Lane and Martin (2010) proposed that the energetic capacity of a cell is the key constraint. They estimated that eukaryotic cells have roughly two hundred thousand times more energy available per gene than prokaryotes. The argument is that the membrane system of the eukaryote, including the mitochondrial membrane, provides the energy supply that a large genome and a large cell require. Their hypothesis is debated, but it links cell architecture to energy in a testable way.

Compartmentalisation also allows different reactions to proceed at the same time in different places. In a prokaryote, metabolic enzymes may be crowded together in a single cytoplasm, and their reactions can interfere with one another. In a eukaryote, the cytosol, the mitochondria and the lysosomes can hold different chemistry, and their enzymes do not compete for the same space.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the consequences of this organisation for cell biology. A eukaryotic cell can grow in size while maintaining a high rate of transport through its internal membranes, and it can change its composition by altering the balance of its compartments.

Cell size and compartment number are measured by microscopy, by counting organelles and by estimating volumes from three-dimensional reconstructions. These measurements allow the relationship between architecture and size to be compared across species.
