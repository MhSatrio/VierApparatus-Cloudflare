# Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells

All cells fall into two fundamental types. Prokaryotic cells, found in bacteria and archaea, have no nucleus and few internal membranes. Eukaryotic cells, found in animals, plants, fungi and protists, have a membrane-bound nucleus and many organelles. The difference is one of the deepest divisions in biology, and it is the reason the tree of life is usually drawn with two branches for cell types.

The module begins with the structure of the prokaryotic cell and the envelope that protects it. It then describes the eukaryotic cell and its nucleus, its ribosomes, and its organelles. The following chapters cover the way the genome is organised in each type, the membrane and wall systems, the division of labour inside eukaryotic cells, and the question of how the complex eukaryotic cell arose from simpler ancestors.

The three domains of life, archaea, bacteria and eukaryotes, provide the frame. Woese and colleagues (1990) showed from ribosomal RNA that archaea and eukaryotes are more closely related to each other than either is to bacteria, which means the prokaryote is not a single natural group. Hug and colleagues (2016) extended the picture with genomic data, which revealed a far larger diversity of microbial lineages than was previously known.

Readers should keep in mind that the prokaryote and the eukaryote are two cell types, not two evolutionary groups. This distinction matters for the later discussion of the origin of eukaryotes.
