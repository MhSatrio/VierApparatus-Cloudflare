Life is divided into three domains: archaea, bacteria and eukaryotes. Woese and colleagues (1990) proposed this division after comparing the sequences of ribosomal RNA from many organisms. The sequences formed three groups that were far more distinct from one another than organisms within each group were from one another. Their analysis overturned the older division of life into prokaryotes and eukaryotes, because the prokaryotes proved to contain two deeply separated lineages.

Bacteria and archaea are both prokaryotes, but they differ in fundamental ways. Their membrane lipids are built differently, their ribosomes and transcription machinery have distinct components, and their cell walls differ in chemistry. Archaea share some features with eukaryotes, including aspects of their replication and transcription, and this relationship is one of the reasons that the genomic study of archaea has been important for understanding the origin of eukaryotes.

Bacteria are the most diverse and widespread prokaryotes. They live in soil, water, the bodies of other organisms and the most extreme environments on Earth. Many are harmless or useful, while some cause disease. Their metabolism covers almost every chemical possibility, from photosynthesis to the reduction of metals.

Eukaryotes are defined by the nucleus and by the membrane-bound organelles described in the previous module. The domain includes animals, plants, fungi and a large group of single-celled protists, and it is the lineage to which humans belong.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular differences that distinguish the domains, and they note that the central machinery of replication, transcription and translation is similar across all three. The conservation of this core is the reason the domains are seen as parts of one tree rather than three separate origins of life.

The domains are a framework, not the final word. Genomic data have revealed additional lineages that do not fit neatly into one domain, and the next chapter describes how the tree is now being revised.
