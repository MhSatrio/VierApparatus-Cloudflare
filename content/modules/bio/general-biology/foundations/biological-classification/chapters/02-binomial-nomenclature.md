Each species has a scientific name made of two parts, the genus and the specific epithet. This system is called binomial nomenclature and was introduced in the eighteenth century by Carl Linnaeus. The genus is written with a capital initial letter, the epithet with a lower-case letter, and the whole name is italicised when printed. The bacterium Escherichia coli, for example, belongs to the genus Escherichia and is named after Theodor Escherich.

The purpose of the system is to give each species a single name that does not depend on language. Common names vary between countries and between languages, and a single common name can refer to several species. A scientific name is intended to be unambiguous, so that a researcher reading a paper in one language can identify the organism described in another.

Names are governed by international codes. Once a name is published according to the rules, it is generally kept, unless a senior name is later found or a change is needed to preserve stability. Biologists sometimes disagree about the correct name of a group, and revisions follow the rules rather than personal preference.

Darwin (1859) noted that naming and classification are not the same. A name identifies a species, and a classification places it in a group. Names can change when classifications change, and this is one of the sources of confusion for students who learn both at once.

Alberts et al. (2022) use scientific names when discussing model organisms, which allows the reader to know exactly which species is meant. The practice is standard in research papers and in the databases that store sequence and phenotype information.
