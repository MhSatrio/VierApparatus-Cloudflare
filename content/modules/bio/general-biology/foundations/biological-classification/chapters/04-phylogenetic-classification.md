A phylogenetic tree is a diagram of evolutionary relationships. Its branch points represent common ancestors, and its tips represent living organisms or sequences. The tree is an inference from data, usually molecular sequences or morphological characters, and it is tested by asking whether the data support its branching pattern better than the alternatives.

A monophyletic group, also called a clade, consists of an ancestor and all of its descendants. Phylogenetic classification aims to define only monophyletic groups. A group defined by a shared resemblance that is not inherited from a recent common ancestor is called polyphyletic if it includes organisms from different lineages. Darwin (1859) argued that the natural system should contain only such groups, because only they reflect genealogy.

Trees are built by comparing characters that change over time. A derived character is one that appeared in an ancestor and was passed to some of its descendants. Shared derived characters provide evidence of common ancestry, while shared primitive characters or convergent features do not. Algorithms search for the tree that requires the fewest changes, or that maximises the likelihood of the data under a model of evolution.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how phylogenetic comparison of genes reveals their evolutionary history, and how a gene found in one lineage can be traced to its relatives. The principle is applied to whole genomes and to individual protein families, and it underlies much of modern comparative biology.

Trees are uncertain. Different genes can give different trees, and some branches are supported more strongly than others. Biologists report the support for each branch and test their trees with additional data, which keeps the classification open to revision.
