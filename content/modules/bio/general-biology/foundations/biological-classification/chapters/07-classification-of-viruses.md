Viruses are not included in the three domains of life. They do not have cells, they cannot carry out metabolism on their own, and they cannot reproduce without a host cell. Each virus consists of a genome, which may be DNA or RNA, enclosed in a protein coat, sometimes with an outer envelope. Once inside a host, the virus directs the host's machinery to make copies of its genome and coat proteins.

The status of viruses as living or non-living is debated, as discussed in the Characteristics of Life module. For classification, the practical question is how to group them. Viruses are commonly classified by their genome type, such as double-stranded DNA or single-stranded RNA, by the structure of their capsid and by the host they infect. These criteria do not always agree, and a virus that resembles another in genome organisation may infect a very different host.

Hug and colleagues (2016) surveyed the genomes of cellular organisms, and their work did not place viruses in the tree of life. The omission reflects a real difference: viruses cannot be assigned to a domain because they do not have the ribosomal RNA that the domain classification uses. Some researchers have proposed that viruses are an independent realm of biological diversity, while others consider them descendants of cellular lineages that lost their independence. The question is not settled.

Virus classification has been revised as sequencing has grown. Large numbers of viruses are now known from environmental samples, and many of them are identified only from their genome sequences. Classification in this field is therefore based heavily on sequence similarity of key genes.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how viruses use the machinery of their hosts, and how this dependence shapes their evolution. The classification of viruses must take this into account, because the host often determines which features are conserved.

The lesson for students is that classification reflects the evidence available. Viruses show where the boundaries of the tree of life are uncertain, and they remind the biologist that a system built for cellular organisms may not map cleanly onto every form of life.
