Most microorganisms cannot be grown in the laboratory. For many years, the tree of life was built mainly from organisms that could be cultured, which left a large part of microbial diversity out of the picture. Genomic methods changed this. Environmental samples can be sequenced directly, and genomes can be reconstructed from the mixed DNA of a community, so that organisms that have never been isolated can still be placed in the tree.

Hug and colleagues (2016) used this approach to survey a large number of genomes from across the microbial world. Their analysis added many new lineages and showed that the diversity of bacteria and archaea is far greater than the cultured organisms suggested. They proposed a revised tree in which several groups that had been considered minor were shown to be substantial lineages in their own right.

The revision also raised questions about where eukaryotes fit. Woese and colleagues (1990) established eukaryotes as a separate domain, with archaea and bacteria as the other two. The genomic survey by Hug and colleagues (2016) added many archaeal and bacterial lineages whose relationships to eukaryotes are still being studied. The position of the eukaryotic branch is therefore an active research question, and the broader tree now contains more intermediate lineages than the earlier diagrams showed.

The new tree also raised questions about the boundaries of the domains. Some groups do not fit clearly into any one domain, and the classification of these organisms must be revised as more genomes are sequenced. The tree is therefore best understood as a provisional map, which improves with each new dataset.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the conservation of the central machinery of cells supports the view of one tree of life rather than many. The new genomic data extend that picture, showing that the diversity of the microbial world is much larger than was once thought.

Biologists studying the tree of life use metagenomics, the sequencing of community DNA, and phylogenomics, which analyses many genes at once. Each method has limits, including the difficulty of assembling genomes from incomplete data, and the results are judged by their consistency across independent genes.
