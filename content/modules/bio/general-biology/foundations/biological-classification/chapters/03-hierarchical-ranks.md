The classification of living things is organised in nested ranks. The principal ranks, from the most inclusive to the least, are domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species. Each rank contains the ranks below it, so every species belongs to a genus, every genus to a family, and so on up to the domain. The nesting reflects the idea that groups within groups represent successive branching in the history of life.

The ranks are conventions rather than natural categories. The number of ranks is not fixed, and biologists add intermediate ranks, such as subfamily or superorder, when they are needed. The rank of a group is a judgement about how much difference it represents, and this judgement has been revised as knowledge has grown. Darwin (1859) noted that the distinctions between species, genera and families are matters of degree, and this is why the boundaries between ranks are sometimes debated.

Domains are the highest rank in current use. Woese and colleagues (1990) proposed three domains, archaea, bacteria and eukaryotes, based on the sequences of ribosomal RNA. The domain is above the kingdom, and the kingdoms of eukaryotes, such as animals, plants and fungi, sit within the eukaryotic domain. Many of the older kingdom systems were built before molecular data were available, and some groupings within them have since been found not to reflect ancestry.

Species is the rank at which the most detailed work takes place. A species is commonly defined as a group of populations that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring, although this definition does not apply to asexual organisms, and several other species concepts are used in practice.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how ranks are used in comparative biology, for example when a finding in one species is tested in a related species at a particular level of the hierarchy. The choice of rank determines how far a conclusion is expected to generalise.
