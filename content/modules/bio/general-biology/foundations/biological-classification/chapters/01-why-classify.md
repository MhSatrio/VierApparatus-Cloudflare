Biologists classify organisms for three main reasons. A shared name lets researchers communicate about the same organism, and a system of names makes it possible to find information about a species. Classification also summarises knowledge: knowing that an organism belongs to a group tells the biologist a great deal about its cell type, metabolism and likely relatives without further study. Finally, classification is a way of testing ideas about history, since a grouping that fits the data supports a claim about common ancestry.

Darwin (1859) argued that the best classification is the one that reflects the genealogy of organisms, so that a group contains an ancestor and all of its descendants. He used the term natural system for such a classification, and he contrasted it with schemes based on superficial resemblance. A resemblance can arise from shared environment rather than shared history, and two unrelated animals may look alike because they live in similar conditions.

The principle is still the basis of modern practice. Biologists now use molecular sequences, especially of genes that change slowly, to infer relationships. Two organisms that share a derived sequence feature are more likely to share a recent ancestor than organisms that share only a similarity that can arise independently.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how sequence comparison allows biologists to build phylogenetic trees, and how the same comparisons reveal the functions of genes shared among lineages. Classification and molecular biology are therefore closely linked.

The principle has limits. Evolution can produce convergent features, and a group defined by one character may not include all descendants of its ancestor. Biologists handle these problems by using many characters and by testing the groups against independent data.
