Classifications are revised. A group that seemed natural can be shown to contain organisms from distant lineages, and a group that seemed unrelated can be found to share a recent ancestor. The revisions are not a sign that biology is unreliable. They are the normal result of gathering new evidence and testing old hypotheses against it.

Convergent evolution is one of the most common causes of error. Organisms that live in similar conditions often evolve similar features independently, so resemblance can mislead. Darwin (1859) discussed how the external forms of organisms are shaped by their way of life, and he argued that classification must look beyond such adaptations to the underlying relationships. Molecular data help because they are less likely to converge on the same sequence by chance.

Incomplete data are another difficulty. Fossils are rare, many species have not been described, and most microbial species have never been cultured. A classification built only from organisms that can be studied in the laboratory will be biased toward those organisms. Hug and colleagues (2016) showed that the sampling of microbial genomes changed the view of the tree, and that the inclusion of previously unknown lineages altered the relationships among groups that were assumed to be well understood.

Revisions are argued through evidence. A proposed change must explain the data better than the existing classification, and it must make predictions that can be tested with new data. Other investigators check the analysis, sometimes using different methods, and the change is accepted only when it survives that scrutiny.

Alberts et al. (2022) note that the conservation of molecular machinery across many organisms provides a stable reference for these arguments. Shared features of the core machinery can be used to test whether a proposed grouping is plausible.

The result is a classification that is provisional but disciplined. It improves as evidence accumulates, and the reasons for each change are open to examination.
