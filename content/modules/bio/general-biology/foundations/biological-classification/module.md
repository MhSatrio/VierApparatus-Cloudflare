# Biological Classification

Classification is the organisation of living things into named groups. It is more than a filing system. Good classifications are hypotheses about evolutionary relationships, and each group is meant to contain an ancestor and all of its descendants. Darwin (1859) argued that the natural arrangement of organisms reflects their common descent, and this idea has guided classification ever since.

The module covers the purpose of classification, the conventions of naming, and the hierarchy of ranks from species to domain. It then turns to the modern tree of life, which is based on molecular sequences rather than on visible features. The three domains of archaea, bacteria and eukaryotes were defined by Woese and colleagues (1990), and genomic surveys since then have revealed far more microbial lineages than were known at the time.

The module also discusses the limits of classification. Groups are defined by people, and boundaries between them can be arbitrary. Many organisms do not fit cleanly into a single category, and the classification of viruses in particular raises questions that have not been settled.

The chapters can be read in sequence. The later ones assume the vocabulary of the earlier ones, and the reference list gives the primary sources for the claims about the tree of life.
