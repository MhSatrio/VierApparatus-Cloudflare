Ecosystems provide benefits that people depend on. Provisioning services include food, fibre, timber, fresh water and genetic resources. Regulating services include the purification of water, the control of floods and pests, the pollination of crops and the regulation of climate. Cultural services include recreation, inspiration and the spiritual value that many societies place on natural places. These services are produced by ecological processes, and they can be lost when those processes are disturbed.

Human activity alters ecosystems in many ways. Habitat loss, through the conversion of forests and wetlands to farmland or cities, removes the organisms that provide services and fragments the populations that remain. Pollution introduces chemicals that can harm organisms directly, and it can alter nutrient cycles, for example by adding excess nitrogen and phosphorus to water. Climate change shifts the temperature and rainfall that organisms depend on, and it can change the timing of life cycles.

Paine (1966) showed that the loss of a keystone species can transform an entire community, which means that the services provided by a community may depend on a few species that are easily overlooked. The removal of such a species can reduce diversity and alter the flow of energy and nutrients.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the physiology of organisms sets their tolerance to environmental change, and how that tolerance determines which species can persist. Understanding the cellular limits of organisms helps ecologists predict which species will be affected by a given change.

Human impacts are measured by monitoring species, water quality, soil chemistry and climate records, and by comparing disturbed sites with reference sites that have been less affected. The measurements allow managers to judge which changes threaten services and which can be reversed.

The lesson of this chapter is that ecosystems are not infinite sources of benefit. They are productive systems whose functions depend on their structure, and the people who depend on them have a direct interest in understanding how they work.
