Species in an ecosystem affect one another in many ways. Each interaction can be classified by its effect on each partner. Competition harms both species when they share a limited resource. Predation benefits the predator and harms the prey. Parasitism benefits the parasite and harms the host. Mutualism benefits both partners, and commensalism benefits one partner without harming or helping the other. These categories are simplifications, since an interaction can change with the environment and with the stage of life of the organisms involved.

Competition is one of the most studied interactions. When two species use the same resource, the one that uses it more efficiently may exclude the other from part of its range. Ecologists have documented this in plants competing for light and in animals competing for food. Competition can therefore limit the distribution of a species, even when the environment would otherwise suit it.

Predation drives many of the patterns of ecology. Predators can regulate prey populations, and prey species have evolved defences such as camouflage, toxicity and rapid escape. Dobzhansky (1973) noted that such interactions have shaped the evolution of both partners, and the evolutionary consequence of predation is one reason species are adapted to the threats they face.

Parasitism is widespread. Many species of animals, plants, fungi and microbes are parasites, and most species are parasites of something. Parasites can reduce the fitness of their hosts, and they can alter host behaviour and population dynamics. Mutualism is equally important. Pollinators and flowering plants, and the bacteria that live in the guts of animals, provide services that each partner could not obtain alone.

Paine (1966) studied the effects of predators on the species composition of a rocky shore community. His experiments showed that the removal of a predator changed which species dominated the community, which established that interactions can have strong effects on diversity.

Interactions are studied by observation, by experimental removal or addition of species, and by measuring the performance of each partner with and without the other. The design of these experiments is discussed in the Scientific Method module.
