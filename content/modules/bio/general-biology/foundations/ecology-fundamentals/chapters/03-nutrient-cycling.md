Energy flows through an ecosystem in one direction, but nutrients cycle. Carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and other elements are taken up by organisms, passed along food chains and returned to the environment by respiration, excretion and death. The movement of these elements forms cycles that link living things to the atmosphere, the oceans, the soil and the rocks.

The carbon cycle begins with photosynthesis, which fixes carbon dioxide from the air into sugars. Respiration by producers and consumers returns carbon dioxide to the atmosphere. Dead material is broken down by decomposers, and some carbon is stored for long periods in soils, sediments and fossil fuels. The balance between uptake and release determines the concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere.

The nitrogen cycle is more complex, because most of the nitrogen in the atmosphere is in a form that organisms cannot use. Certain bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia, which other bacteria convert into nitrate. Plants absorb these forms, and animals obtain nitrogen by eating plants or other animals. Denitrifying bacteria return nitrogen gas to the atmosphere, and the cycle is closed.

Decomposers are central to nutrient cycling. Fungi and bacteria break down dead matter and release nutrients in forms that plants can absorb. Without decomposition, nutrients would be locked in dead tissue, and the ecosystem would run out of the elements needed for growth. Paine (1966) studied the interactions of species in a rocky intertidal community and showed that the removal of a single predator changes which species dominate. The connections he documented are part of the same web of interactions that moves nutrients through the system, although his study measured community composition rather than nutrient fluxes.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the enzymes that carry out the key steps of the nitrogen cycle, including nitrogen fixation, which is catalysed by a single enzyme complex in many bacteria. The chemistry of these reactions determines the rate at which nutrients become available.

Nutrient cycles are measured with isotope tracers, which follow the path of an element through the system, and with measurements of soil, water and air chemistry over time. The cycles show how the living and non-living parts of the ecosystem are connected.
