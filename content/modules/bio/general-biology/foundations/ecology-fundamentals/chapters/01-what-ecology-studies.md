Ecology is the scientific study of the distribution and abundance of organisms and of the interactions that determine them. The word comes from the Greek oikos, meaning house or dwelling, and it describes the study of organisms in their home environment. Ecologists work at several levels: the individual organism and its responses to its surroundings, the population of one species, the community of many species, the ecosystem of communities and their physical setting, and the landscape and biosphere that contain them all.

Three questions are central to the field. Why do species live where they do and not elsewhere? Why are some species abundant and others rare? How do organisms affect one another and their environment? Each question has many answers, and the answers depend on the scale and the organisms studied. A single explanation for the distribution of a plant may combine climate, soil, competition and dispersal.

Dobzhansky (1973) argued that ecology and evolution are inseparable, because the environment shapes selection and selection shapes how organisms fit their environment. Ecological interactions are therefore an important part of the evolutionary history of every species.

Ecology is also a practical discipline. The management of fisheries, forests, farmland and protected areas depends on ecological knowledge. Ecologists use field surveys, experiments, long-term monitoring and models to predict how ecosystems respond to change, and they must communicate their findings to people who make decisions.

Alberts et al. (2022) note that the cell and the organism provide the foundation for ecological understanding, because the traits of organisms determine how they interact with their environment. Ecology builds on cell biology and physiology and extends them to the scale of the whole ecosystem.

The chapters that follow examine each of the central processes of ecology in turn, starting with the flow of energy through food chains.
