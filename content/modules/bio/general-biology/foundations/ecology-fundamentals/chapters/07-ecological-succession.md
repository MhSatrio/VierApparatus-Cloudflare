Ecological succession is the process by which the species composition of a community changes over time after a disturbance. A fire, a flood, a landslide or the retreat of a glacier can remove most of the organisms in an area, and the community that follows passes through a series of stages. Early colonisers, called pioneer species, arrive first. Over years or centuries, they are replaced by species that depend on the conditions the pioneers created.

Primary succession begins on a surface that has never supported life, such as bare rock or new volcanic ground. Lichens and mosses establish first, breaking down rock and accumulating organic matter until soil forms. Secondary succession begins on a site where soil remains after a disturbance, such as an abandoned field or a burned forest. It is usually faster, because the soil already contains nutrients and seeds.

The pioneers change the environment. They add organic matter, provide shade, alter the chemistry of the soil and change the moisture regime. These changes make the site suitable for later species, which may outcompete the pioneers. The process is therefore not a fixed sequence of independent species but a series of interactions in which each stage prepares the next.

Paine (1966) showed that the removal or addition of species can change the course of community development. Disturbances that remove a dominant species can allow others to establish, which is one of the mechanisms that maintains diversity in a community.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how organisms modify their environment through their metabolism and growth, a principle that applies in cell culture and in the wider world. The cells of an organism change their surroundings, and the same is true of ecological communities.

Succession is studied by revisiting sites over decades, by permanent plots that are surveyed repeatedly, and by chronosequences, in which sites of different ages are compared to infer the stages of development.
