A food chain describes a single line of feeding, from a producer through a sequence of consumers. Real ecosystems are more complicated, because most species eat several kinds of food and are eaten by several kinds of predator. The network of all feeding relationships in a community is called a food web. It captures the many routes by which energy and nutrients move through the system.

Paine (1966) examined the complexity of food webs and their relation to species diversity. He compared communities with different numbers of feeding links and observed how the loss of species affected the rest of the web. His work proposed that complex webs with many links may tend to be more stable, because a loss of one species can be compensated by the others. The argument is subtle, and the relationship between web complexity and stability remains an active topic.

Paine later introduced the term keystone species, in work on the same kind of rocky shore. His experiments showed that a single predatory starfish controlled the abundance of a dominant mussel. When the starfish was removed, the mussels spread across the shore and other species were displaced. The starfish had a disproportionately large effect relative to its abundance, which is the defining property of a keystone species.

Dobzhansky (1973) argued that the study of the interconnections of species gives insight into the history of life, since each relationship reflects the evolutionary history of the partners. The structure of food webs therefore carries historical information as well as ecological information.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the properties of individual cells are integrated into organisms, and how the connections among organisms form the networks of an ecosystem. The concept of a network applies at both levels.

Food webs are studied by gut content analysis, by stable isotope analysis, which reveals the diet of consumers, and by mathematical models that represent the links among species.
