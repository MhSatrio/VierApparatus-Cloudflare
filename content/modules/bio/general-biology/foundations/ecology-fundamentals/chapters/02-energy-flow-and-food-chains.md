Energy enters almost every ecosystem as sunlight. Photosynthetic organisms, such as plants, algae and cyanobacteria, capture a small fraction of the light and convert it into chemical energy stored in sugars. These organisms are the producers. Animals and other consumers obtain their energy by eating producers or other consumers, and the sequence of who eats whom is the food chain. Each step in the chain is a trophic level.

Energy moves in one direction through the chain. It is not recycled, and it is lost at each transfer. A large part of the energy in food is used in respiration, which powers the consumer's own metabolism, and much of the rest is lost as heat or as material that is not digested. As a result, only a small proportion of the energy at one level is available to the next. Ecologists often estimate this efficiency at around ten per cent, although the value varies widely among systems.

The loss has large consequences. It explains why food chains are short, why top predators are rare, and why an ecosystem supports far more biomass at the bottom of the chain than at the top. Lane and Martin (2010) argued that energy limits the complexity that a cell can maintain, and a similar energetic constraint applies to the number of trophic levels an ecosystem can support.

Energy flow is measured by harvesting biomass, by tracking the consumption of food by animals and by estimating respiration with the uptake of oxygen or the release of carbon dioxide. Energy budgets combine these measures to show how much energy each level receives, uses and passes on.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular processes that capture and release energy, which underlie the flow at the level of the ecosystem. The efficiency of those processes sets the efficiency of the ecological chain.
