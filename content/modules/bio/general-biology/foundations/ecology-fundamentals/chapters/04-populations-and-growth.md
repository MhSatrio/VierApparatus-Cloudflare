A population grows when births exceed deaths and shrinks when the opposite holds. When resources are unlimited and each individual reproduces at a constant rate, the population grows exponentially. The number of individuals in each generation is a constant multiple of the number in the previous one, so growth accelerates over time. Bacteria in a fresh medium show this pattern for a short period, and it is also a useful model for the early phase of many introduced species.

In nature, resources are limited, and growth slows as the population approaches a maximum. The logistic model describes this pattern. The rate of growth falls as the population increases, and the population levels off at a value called the carrying capacity. Carrying capacity is the largest population the environment can sustain over time, and it depends on food, space, water, predators and the diseases that spread among individuals.

Dobzhansky (1973) argued that population dynamics are shaped by the same processes that produce evolution, since the survival and reproduction of individuals determine both the size of the population and the frequency of its traits. Population growth is therefore an ecological question and an evolutionary one at the same time.

Populations can fluctuate in ways that the simple models do not capture. Delays in response to density, such as the time needed for predators to increase after their prey, can produce cycles. Environmental variation, such as drought or a harsh winter, can cause sudden declines. Random events can drive small populations toward extinction, which is one reason conservation biologists are concerned with population size.

Alberts et al. (2022) note that the regulation of population growth by food supply has parallels in cell biology, where the availability of nutrients controls the rate of cell division. Both systems show how limited resources set a ceiling on growth.

Population size is measured by counts, by mark and recapture methods in which individuals are captured, marked and recaptured to estimate the total, and by monitoring over many years. These measurements allow ecologists to fit growth models and to test whether a population is limited by resources, predators or disease.
