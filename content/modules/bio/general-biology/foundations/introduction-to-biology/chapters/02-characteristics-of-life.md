A list of the characteristics of life is a useful starting point, but it has to be read carefully. The list does not define life in a single sentence. It names properties that living systems share, and it asks the reader to judge whether an object has them. Koshland (2002) gave one influential version, describing life in terms of a few organising principles that he called pillars. Other textbooks use different groupings, and the differences are instructive.

The first property is organisation. Living systems are built from parts that are arranged in specific patterns, and the pattern matters as much as the parts. A cell contains the same kinds of molecules as a dead cell, but the living cell maintains them in an ordered state that changes in predictable ways.

The second is metabolism, the set of chemical reactions by which an organism takes in matter and energy, converts them, and releases waste. Metabolism is the reason living systems are never in equilibrium with their surroundings while they are alive.

The third is growth and development. Organisms increase in size and complexity according to information stored in their genetic material. Growth requires a supply of matter and energy, and it is controlled rather than random.

The fourth is reproduction and heredity. Living systems produce new systems that carry on their organisation. The information passed on from one generation to the next is stored in chemical form, and it is copied with a degree of fidelity that makes heredity possible.

The fifth is response to the environment, and the sixth is adaptation through evolution. Organisms detect changes around them and alter their behaviour or physiology. Populations change over generations as heritable variation is filtered by the environment.

The point of combining these properties is that each has exceptions. Fire grows, consumes fuel and responds to wind, yet it has no heredity. Crystals grow in an ordered way, yet they do not metabolise. A list of properties therefore works best as a set of questions that a biologist asks about a system, not as a single test that settles the matter.
