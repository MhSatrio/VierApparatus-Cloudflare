Biology has a small number of principles that organise a very large body of facts. The first is cell theory: all living things are made of cells, and cells arise only from pre-existing cells. This principle is the reason cells are used as the unit of study in nearly every branch of the subject. Its modern form also recognises that some organisms, such as bacteria, consist of a single cell, while others, such as animals, are built from billions of cells that cooperate.

The second principle is evolution by descent with modification. Dobzhansky (1973) argued that the diversity of life and the adaptations of organisms make sense only in light of their shared history. Populations change through variation, heredity and selection, and the relationships among species can be read from the similarity of their genes and anatomy. The principle explains patterns across all of biology, from antibiotic resistance in bacteria to the shared bone structure of mammalian limbs.

The third principle is that heritable information is stored in chemical form. Watson and Crick (1953) showed that the structure of DNA allows information to be copied accurately, because each strand specifies the sequence of its partner. Later work established that this information is read through RNA and proteins, and that the mapping from sequence to function is the central problem of molecular biology.

The fourth principle concerns energy. Living systems maintain order by using energy, and the energetic costs of building complex cells constrain what is possible. Lane and Martin (2010) argued that the energy available per gene in prokaryotes and eukaryotes differs greatly, and that this difference helps explain why eukaryotic cells are so much more complex. Their hypothesis remains debated, which illustrates how principles are themselves subject to revision.

Principles are not laws in the physical sense. They are generalisations supported by many lines of evidence, and they remain open to correction. Their value lies in organising knowledge so that a new observation can be placed in context and tested against what is already known.
