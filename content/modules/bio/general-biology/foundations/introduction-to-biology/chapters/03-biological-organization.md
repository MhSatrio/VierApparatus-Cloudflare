Biology is organised in levels. At the smallest scale, atoms such as carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen combine into molecules. Some molecules, such as sugars, lipids and amino acids, are simple. Others, such as proteins and nucleic acids, are very large and carry information. Molecules assemble into structures such as membranes, ribosomes and the cytoskeleton, which are called organelles when they sit inside a cell. The cell is the smallest unit that is generally regarded as alive.

Cells group into tissues, tissues into organs, and organs into systems such as the digestive or nervous system. In an organism the systems work together to maintain the body. Above the organism come populations of one species living in one place, communities of many species, and ecosystems in which living things interact with the non-living environment. The biosphere is the sum of all ecosystems on Earth.

The idea that living things are made of cells was first made visible by Hooke (1665), who examined thin slices of cork under a microscope and described small compartments he called cells. Later observers established that cells are the basic units of plants and animals. Much of modern biology still rests on that discovery, which is why cells are the usual starting point for the study of organization.

The levels are not simply stacked boxes. Each level has properties that do not follow directly from the level below. A single neuron cannot think, but a network of neurons can. A single water molecule is not wet, yet large numbers of water molecules form a liquid with that property. Alberts et al. (2022) describe this as the emergence of new properties through interactions among components, and the concept recurs throughout biology.

Organization also explains how biologists choose their methods. A structural biologist works at the level of molecules and uses X-ray crystallography or electron microscopy to resolve their shapes. A physiologist measures organs and whole animals. An ecologist counts populations and communities. Each approach answers questions at its own level, and the most productive research often links several levels in a single study.
