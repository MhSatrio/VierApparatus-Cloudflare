Biology has no single founding moment, but its history is shaped by a few turning points. Ancient writers such as Aristotle catalogued animals and plants and tried to classify them by their structure and habits. For many centuries that descriptive tradition dominated, and it produced large collections of observations without a unifying explanation of how organisms are related.

The invention of the microscope changed what could be seen. Hooke (1665) examined thin sections of cork and described the small compartments he called cells. The observation was not yet a theory. It took more than a century and a half for botanists and zoologists to establish that all plants and animals are built from cells and that cells arise from other cells. That generalisation, known as cell theory, is one of the foundations of modern biology.

The publication of *On the Origin of Species* by Darwin (1859) provided the second great turning point. Darwin argued that populations contain heritable variation, that more individuals are born than can survive, and that those with advantageous variants leave more descendants. The theory explained the diversity of life and the fit between organisms and their environments, and it gave biology a single explanatory framework. Its full implications took decades to develop, after the mechanisms of heredity were understood.

The third turning point came in the middle of the twentieth century, when biochemistry and genetics converged. Watson and Crick (1953) proposed a double helix structure for DNA in which two complementary strands carry information that can be copied. Their model suggested how genetic information is stored and replicated, and it opened the molecular era of biology.

Each turning point depended on tools. The microscope revealed cells, the statistical analysis of populations supported Darwin's argument, and X-ray diffraction provided the data on which the DNA model rested. The history of biology is therefore also a history of instruments, and new instruments continue to change what the subject can explain.
