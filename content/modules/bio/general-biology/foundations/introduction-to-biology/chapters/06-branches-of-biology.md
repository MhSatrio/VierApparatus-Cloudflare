Biology is divided into branches so that each can concentrate on a level of organization or a group of organisms. Molecular biology asks how genes, RNA and proteins carry out their functions. Cell biology studies the structure and behaviour of cells and their compartments. Genetics examines heredity and the variation that inheritance transmits, and genomics extends that work to whole genomes. Biochemistry describes the chemical reactions of living systems. Physiology asks how organs and organisms maintain their functions, while anatomy describes their structure.

Other branches are defined by the organisms they study. Microbiology covers bacteria, archaea, viruses, fungi and protists. Botany studies plants, and zoology studies animals. Mycology is devoted to fungi. These groupings are practical, since techniques and textbooks tend to follow the organism, but they are not natural kingdoms of the living world. Woese, Kandler, and Wheelis (1990) showed from ribosomal RNA sequences that the traditional split between prokaryotes and eukaryotes hides a deeper division into three domains, which means that a grouping by organism can cut across evolutionary relationships.

Some branches are defined by their relationship with the environment or with time. Ecology studies interactions between organisms and their surroundings. Evolutionary biology studies change in populations over generations. Developmental biology asks how a single cell becomes a complex organism. Each of these branches shares methods and concepts with the others, so a question about how a fish develops a particular fin may draw on genetics, cell biology and evolutionary theory at once.

Applied branches such as medical biology, agricultural biology and biotechnology take the knowledge produced by basic research and apply it to particular problems. Their questions are often shaped by the needs of medicine, food production or industry, and they can feed back into basic research by exposing gaps in understanding.

The branches are best seen as a map of emphasis rather than a set of walls. Alberts et al. (2022) organise their account of cell biology around molecular mechanisms, but they draw freely on genetics, evolution and physiology. Students who learn the branches as separate subjects should expect to find that the most interesting questions lie between them.
