Biology is the scientific study of living organisms and of the processes that keep them alive. The word combines the Greek *bios*, meaning life, with *logia*, meaning the study of something. It entered common scientific use in the early nineteenth century, when naturalists began to treat the living world as a subject with its own methods and its own questions, separate from medicine and natural history.

The definition is simple to state and difficult to apply. Every biologist can name living things, such as bacteria, oak trees and humans, but the boundary between living and non-living matter is not always sharp. A virus can be crystallised and stored like a chemical compound. Once it enters a host cell, however, it directs that cell's machinery to make copies of itself. Some researchers count viruses as living, others do not, and the disagreement is partly about definitions and partly about evidence. This is why biology cannot simply take life as given. It has to decide which properties matter and why.

Working biologists therefore approach the question through a set of observable properties. Koshland (2002) argued that living systems share a small number of features, among them a genetic program that stores information, the capacity to respond to changing conditions, compartmentalisation of chemistry inside membranes, and the conservation and use of energy. No single property is decisive on its own, since crystals grow and flames consume fuel. Taken together, however, the properties separate living systems from the inanimate world in a way that can be tested.

Modern biology studies life at many scales. A biochemist may ask how an enzyme changes shape when it binds its substrate. An ecologist may ask how predator and prey populations rise and fall across a forest over decades. Between these extremes lie cell biology, physiology, genetics, developmental biology and evolution. Each subdiscipline uses its own instruments, but all share a commitment to explaining observations through mechanisms that can be tested.

The definition also has a practical side. Decisions about conservation, medicine and agriculture depend on knowing what counts as an organism, when a population is viable, and when a biological process has stopped. A clear definition of the subject is therefore a working tool, not only a classroom exercise.
