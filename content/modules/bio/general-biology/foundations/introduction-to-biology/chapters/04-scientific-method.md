The scientific method is not a single recipe. It is a family of practices that share one commitment: claims about the natural world should be tested against observations that could, in principle, prove them wrong. In biology the cycle usually begins with an observation, such as a mutant bacterium that grows more slowly than normal, or a population of birds that has changed its beak size. The observation prompts a question, and the question leads to a hypothesis, which is a statement that can be tested and that predicts a specific outcome.

Platt (1964) described a disciplined version of this cycle, which he called strong inference. The method asks the investigator to formulate several alternative hypotheses at once, to design an experiment whose outcomes will distinguish between them, and to carry out that experiment without letting any single hypothesis become a favourite. Platt argued that fields progressing rapidly tend to practise this discipline, while fields that test only one idea at a time make slower progress.

Controls are central to the practice. A control is a condition that is identical to the experimental condition except for the factor being tested. If a drug is expected to slow the growth of cultured cells, the control cells receive the same medium and handling without the drug. Without a control, a change in the cells could come from the warmer incubator, a new batch of medium or the time of day, and the investigator could not attribute the change to the drug.

Replication is the second safeguard. A single measurement can be misleading because of random variation, contamination or a mistake in handling. Repeating the experiment, and ideally repeating it with independent samples, allows the investigator to estimate how much variation to expect and whether an observed difference is larger than that variation.

Biology is also a science of complex systems, so a clean experiment is sometimes impossible. Field studies cannot control the weather, and human studies cannot randomise every factor. In those cases biologists rely on statistical design, natural experiments and careful comparison. The underlying logic is the same: a hypothesis is accepted only to the extent that it survives tests that might have falsified it.
