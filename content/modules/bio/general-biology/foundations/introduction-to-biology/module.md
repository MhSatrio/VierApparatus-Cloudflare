# Introduction to Biology

Biology is the study of living things. This module is the entry point to the whole curriculum. It explains what the subject covers, how biologists decide that something is alive, how the discipline is organised from molecules to ecosystems, and how biological knowledge is produced and checked. Each chapter treats one part of that introduction in enough depth to support the more demanding modules that follow.

The eight chapters are meant to be read in order. The first two chapters establish the vocabulary of the subject: what biology is and which properties define a living system. The third chapter describes the levels of organization that give the subject its structure. The fourth chapter explains how biologists reason from evidence, and the fifth gives the historical background that explains why current ideas took the form they have. The sixth chapter describes the major branches, the seventh sets out the principles that connect them, and the eighth considers why the subject matters to medicine, food, the environment and society.

Introductory material is sometimes treated as a list of facts to memorise. That approach misses the point. The facts in this module are the outcome of arguments, measurements and revisions, and the reasons they are accepted matter as much as the facts themselves. Where the evidence is incomplete, the chapters say so.

Readers should expect no prior knowledge of chemistry or cell structure. Those topics are introduced in the chapters that follow this module in the Foundations level, and they are needed for the later chapters here only in simplified form.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module, a learner should be able to define biology, recognise the properties of living systems, place a biological structure at its correct level of organization, describe a basic experimental argument, outline the history that produced modern biology, and give examples of how biological principles are used in practice.
