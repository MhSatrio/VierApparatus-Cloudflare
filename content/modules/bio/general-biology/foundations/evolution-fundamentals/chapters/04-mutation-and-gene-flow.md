Mutations are changes in the DNA sequence. They arise from errors in replication, from damage to the bases and from the activity of mobile genetic elements. Watson and Crick (1953) showed that DNA carries information in the order of its bases, so a change in the sequence changes the information. Most mutations are neutral or harmful, but a small proportion can be beneficial, and these provide the new variation on which selection can act.

The rate of mutation is low per base, but a genome contains a large number of bases and a population contains many genomes. The total number of new mutations in each generation is therefore substantial. The rate varies among species and among regions of the genome, and it is influenced by the efficiency of repair systems.

Gene flow is the movement of alleles from one population to another, usually through the migration of individuals or the dispersal of gametes or seeds. It is a counterforce to divergence. When two populations exchange migrants, they share alleles, and the differences between them are reduced. When gene flow stops, the populations can diverge through drift and selection.

Gene flow can also introduce new variation into a population, because the migrants may carry alleles that are absent or rare locally. This can speed adaptation, for example when a pathogen spreads a resistance allele from one region to another.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how mutations are detected by sequencing, by comparing the DNA of individuals and by tracking the frequency of specific variants. Gene flow is measured by genetic markers that differ between populations and by tracing the movement of those markers across landscapes.

Together, mutation and gene flow set the supply of genetic variation available to a population, and they determine how far its genetic makeup is shared with others.
