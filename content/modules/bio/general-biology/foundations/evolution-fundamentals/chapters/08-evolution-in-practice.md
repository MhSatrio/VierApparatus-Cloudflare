Evolution is not only a historical process. It acts in populations today, and its effects can be seen in medicine, agriculture and conservation. Antibiotic resistance is the most important example in medicine. A bacterial population exposed to an antibiotic contains some cells that carry a mutation or gene that allows them to survive. Those cells reproduce, and within a few generations the population may consist mainly of resistant bacteria.

The mechanism is simple. Resistance can arise by mutation in the target of the drug, by the acquisition of genes that inactivate the drug, or by the pumping of the drug out of the cell. Resistance genes can spread between bacteria through plasmids and other mobile elements, which is a form of gene flow among microbes. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how these mechanisms are studied in cell biology, and how the molecular target of an antibiotic is identified.

Pests show the same pattern. Insects exposed to a pesticide can evolve resistance, and the speed of resistance depends on the number of generations per year, the genetic basis of resistance and the strength of selection. Management strategies such as rotating pesticides, maintaining refuges of untreated plants and monitoring populations are designed with these processes in mind.

Dobzhansky (1973) argued that understanding evolution is necessary for solving practical problems, and the examples here show why. A treatment that ignores evolution can fail quickly, while one that anticipates it can last longer.

Conservation biology uses the same principles. Small populations lose genetic variation through drift, and fragmented populations may stop exchanging genes. Managers aim to maintain population size and connectivity to preserve the variation that populations need to adapt to change.

Biologists monitor evolution in practice by sequencing resistant organisms, by tracking the frequency of resistance alleles over time and by testing the effectiveness of different management strategies in field trials.
