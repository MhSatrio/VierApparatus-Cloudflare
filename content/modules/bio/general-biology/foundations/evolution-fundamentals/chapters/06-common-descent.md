Common descent is the principle that all living organisms are descended from a common ancestor, and that the diversity of life arose through branching. Darwin (1859) proposed the idea and illustrated it with a branching diagram, in which lineages diverge from shared ancestors and some become extinct. The image of a tree, rather than a ladder, is the correct picture: no living species is the ancestor of another living species, but all share ancestors in the past.

The evidence for common descent comes from many sources. Anatomical homologies, such as the similar bone pattern in the forelimbs of humans, bats, whales and horses, are explained by inheritance from a common ancestor even when the structures have different functions. Embryological similarities reflect shared developmental programmes. Molecular homologies are the strongest evidence of all: the genetic code is nearly universal, the ribosome is conserved across the tree of life, and the same core genes are found in distant lineages.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the universality of the genetic code points to a common origin, since a code shared by all cells is unlikely to have arisen independently many times. Many of the enzymes and structural proteins of cells are similar in organisms as different as bacteria and humans, and the sequences of these proteins can be compared to infer their ancestry.

Common descent also explains patterns that would be unexpected under independent creation. Vestigial structures, such as the reduced hind limbs of some whales, are inherited from ancestors that used them. Organisms on islands often resemble the mainland species nearby more closely than they resemble species on other islands, which reflects the path of colonisation and descent.

Dobzhansky (1973) argued that common descent is the framework within which the diversity of organisms can be understood. The principle is tested by building trees from independent data sets and checking whether they agree.
