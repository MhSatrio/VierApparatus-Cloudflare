No two individuals in a population are identical. They differ in size, colour, physiology and behaviour, and many of these differences have a genetic basis. Darwin (1859) recognised that this variation is the raw material of evolution. Without it, a population could not change in response to a changing environment, because every member would respond in the same way.

Only inherited variation can drive evolution. A tall person who became tall through good nutrition has not passed on a genetic advantage, because the trait was not inherited. A plant that grows larger in full sun has adapted its growth during its life, which is a response, not an evolutionary change. The distinction is between the phenotype that an individual shows and the genotype it carries.

Variation arises from two main sources. Mutation creates new alleles, which are alternative versions of a gene, and sexual reproduction shuffles the alleles that already exist into new combinations in each generation. Both processes are continuous, and the variation they produce is the basis of the differences that selection acts upon.

A population is described by its allele frequencies, the proportion of each allele among the copies of a gene in the population. If a gene has two alleles, A and a, and half the copies are A, the frequency of A is 0.5. Evolution is the change in these frequencies over generations, and it is measured by sampling the population at different times and comparing the proportions.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the genetic variation in a population can be measured by sequencing, by identifying the alleles at specific loci and by tracing how they are transmitted. These measurements allow biologists to detect when a change in allele frequency has occurred.
