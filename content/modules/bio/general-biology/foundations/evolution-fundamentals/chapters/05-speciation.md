Speciation is the process by which one population divides into two or more groups that can no longer exchange genes. The defining step is reproductive isolation. Once two groups stop interbreeding, the genetic differences that arise between them are no longer diluted, and they can diverge further. Darwin (1859) titled his book on the origin of species, yet he did not have a full account of how new species arise, and the mechanism was developed by later workers.

Allopatric speciation occurs when populations are geographically separated. A mountain range, a river or an ocean can divide a population, and each part then accumulates different mutations, experiences different selection and undergoes drift. If the separation lasts long enough, the populations may become reproductively isolated even if they come back into contact. Many island species are thought to have arisen this way.

Sympatric speciation occurs without geographical separation. It can happen when a population splits according to a trait that affects mating or the choice of habitat, such as the timing of breeding or the food source. It is more difficult to demonstrate than allopatric speciation, because the evidence must rule out the possibility that the populations were once separated.

Dobzhansky (1973) argued that speciation is central to understanding the diversity of life, and that the study of isolating mechanisms is a key part of evolutionary biology. Isolating mechanisms include differences in mating behaviour, in the timing of reproduction, in the structure of reproductive organs and in the viability of hybrids.

Biologists study speciation by measuring genetic divergence, by testing for hybrid incompatibility and by observing the behaviour of populations in the field and in the laboratory. The continuum between populations and species is also important, because many groups show incomplete isolation that can be studied to understand how the process unfolds.
