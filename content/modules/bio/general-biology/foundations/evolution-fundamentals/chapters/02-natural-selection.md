Natural selection is the process by which individuals with heritable traits that improve survival or reproduction leave more offspring than others. Darwin (1859) described it as the consequence of three conditions: individuals in a population vary, the variation is inherited, and more offspring are born than can survive. Given these conditions, the trait that helps its bearer survive or reproduce will tend to become more common in later generations.

Selection does not act on the population as a whole. It acts on individuals, and its effect on the population is a change in allele frequencies. Suppose a population of insects includes a variant that resists a particular predator. If the predator removes the other individuals more often, the resistant variant will make up a larger share of the next generation. The change is small in a single generation and can be large over many.

Selection can take several forms. Directional selection favours one extreme of a trait and shifts the population toward it. Stabilising selection favours intermediate values and reduces variation. Disruptive selection favours both extremes and can split a population. Each form leaves a characteristic pattern in the distribution of a trait, and biologists can recognise the pattern in field data.

Dobzhansky (1973) argued that the features of organisms make sense only when understood as products of selection over long periods. Adaptation in this sense is the outcome of selection acting on heritable variation, and it is what gives organisms their match to their environments.

Selection is measured by comparing the survival and reproductive success of individuals with different traits, by tracking allele frequencies across generations, and by experiments in which the environment is controlled and the outcome of competition is recorded. Such measurements allow biologists to estimate how strongly a trait is favoured.
