Not every change in allele frequency is caused by selection. In any generation, the offspring carry a random sample of the alleles of their parents, and by chance some alleles are passed on more often than others. This random change is called genetic drift. It occurs in every population, but its effects are strongest when the population is small.

Consider a population of ten individuals carrying two alleles of a gene in equal numbers. In the next generation, the alleles are sampled at random from the gametes, and by chance one allele may be lost entirely while the other becomes fixed. In a population of ten thousand, the same sampling produces much smaller changes, because the random fluctuations average out over many individuals.

Drift has two important consequences. It can remove alleles from a population even when they are neutral or slightly favourable, and it can fix alleles that are neutral or slightly harmful. Over long periods, drift can cause populations to diverge, even if they live in identical environments and are subject to the same selective pressures.

Darwin (1859) did not know about drift, and the mechanism was developed in the twentieth century as the study of populations became quantitative. The recognition of drift showed that evolution is not always the outcome of selection, and it changed the way biologists think about the origin of differences between populations.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how small populations, such as those of endangered species or of organisms that colonise new islands, are particularly exposed to drift. Biologists studying genetic diversity in such populations use drift models to estimate how fast variation is lost.

Drift is measured by comparing allele frequencies across generations, and by simulations that show how much variation is expected from random sampling alone. When an observed change exceeds what drift predicts, the investigator has evidence for selection or another process.
