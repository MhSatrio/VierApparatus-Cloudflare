Light passing through a small aperture spreads out, a phenomenon called diffraction. When a microscope images a point source, the lens does not produce a point but a small blurred disc surrounded by faint rings. Two nearby points produce overlapping discs, and below a certain separation they cannot be distinguished. The separation at which this happens is the diffraction limit.

Ernst Abbe showed in the nineteenth century that the limit is set by the wavelength of the light and by the numerical aperture of the objective. The resolution, measured as the smallest resolvable distance, is approximately half the wavelength divided by the numerical aperture. For green light of about 500 nanometres and an objective with a numerical aperture near 1.4, this gives a resolution of roughly 180 to 200 nanometres. Shorter wavelengths and higher apertures improve resolution, but there is a practical limit to both.

The limit has consequences for biology. Mitochondria, bacteria and nuclei are larger than the resolution limit and can be seen clearly. Individual ribosomes, microtubules and most protein complexes are below it and appear as blurred spots. Biologists studying these structures must use methods that avoid the limit, such as electron microscopy or the super-resolution techniques discussed in a later chapter.

Hell and Wichmann (1994) demonstrated one way around the limit. Their stimulated emission depletion method uses a second, doughnut-shaped beam to switch off fluorescence at the edge of the excitation spot, so that only a region smaller than the diffraction limit remains fluorescent. The result is an image whose effective resolution is set by the depletion beam rather than by the wavelength alone.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the consequences of the diffraction limit for cell biology, noting that structures smaller than a few hundred nanometres require either different instruments or indirect inference from many images. The limit defines what a light microscope can and cannot tell a researcher about the cell.
