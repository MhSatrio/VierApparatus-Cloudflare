Electrons have wavelengths far shorter than visible light. An electron accelerated to a typical voltage has a wavelength of a few picometres, which is many thousands of times smaller than the wavelength of light. Electron microscopes use electromagnetic lenses to focus beams of electrons, and their resolution is limited by lens quality rather than by wavelength. Modern instruments can resolve features below one nanometre.

In transmission electron microscopy, a thin specimen is placed in the beam, and electrons that pass through it are focused onto a detector. Dense structures scatter more electrons and appear dark. The method reveals the internal ultrastructure of cells, including membranes, ribosomes and the organisation of organelles. It requires that the specimen be cut into sections a few tens of nanometres thick and that it survive the high vacuum of the instrument.

Scanning electron microscopy works differently. A fine beam is scanned across the surface of a specimen, and the emitted secondary electrons are collected to build an image of the surface. The method shows three-dimensional shape and surface texture, and it is widely used to study cell surfaces, tissues and microorganisms.

Specimen preparation is the critical step in electron microscopy. Samples must be fixed to preserve their structure, dehydrated, embedded in resin and sectioned. Each step can alter the structure, and the fixation chemistry can introduce artefacts. Cryo-electron microscopy avoids many of these problems by freezing the specimen rapidly, so that water forms glass instead of crystals, and then imaging it at low temperature.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how electron microscopy transformed cell biology by revealing the membrane systems and filaments that light microscopy could not resolve. Many discoveries about the organisation of organelles were made with this method.

Electron microscopy complements the optical methods discussed in the previous chapters. It provides much finer detail but usually cannot image living cells, so the two approaches are often used together.
