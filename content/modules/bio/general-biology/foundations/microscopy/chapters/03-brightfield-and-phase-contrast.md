Brightfield microscopy is the simplest form of light microscopy. White light passes through the specimen, and the image is formed by differences in how much light is absorbed. Specimens are usually stained with dyes that bind particular structures and absorb light strongly, which creates the contrast needed to see them. The Gram stain, which separates bacteria by the structure of their walls, is a brightfield method.

Living cells are difficult to image in brightfield, because they absorb little light. Their structures differ mainly in refractive index, which is the ratio of the speed of light in the structure to its speed in a vacuum. A change in refractive index alters the phase of light passing through the structure, but not its intensity, and the eye cannot see phase differences directly.

Phase contrast microscopy solves this problem. A ring-shaped aperture in the illumination path and a matching phase plate in the objective shift the phase of the background light relative to the light that passes through the specimen. The two beams interfere in the image plane, so differences in phase become differences in brightness. The technique makes living cells visible without stains, which is valuable for watching cells move, divide and change shape.

Hooke (1665) observed cells in dead tissue, which was the only option available to him. Phase contrast, developed in the 1930s, allowed investigators to study cells alive, and it remains a routine method for culture work.

Alberts et al. (2022) note that the choice between brightfield, stained and phase-contrast imaging depends on whether the cells must remain alive and whether the features of interest absorb light or only shift its phase. Each method answers a different question.
