In a conventional fluorescence microscope, the whole specimen is illuminated at once, so light from above and below the plane of focus reaches the detector and blurs the image. This is acceptable for thin specimens but poor for thick ones such as tissues or embryos. The confocal microscope solves the problem by placing a pinhole in front of the detector, conjugate to the focal point in the specimen.

Light from the focal plane passes through the pinhole, while light from other planes is blocked. The result is an optical section, a thin slice of the specimen with much less blur than a conventional image. Collecting many sections at different depths produces a stack of images, which software can combine into a three-dimensional reconstruction showing the shape and position of structures inside the specimen.

The technique uses a scanning laser beam to build each image point by point, which allows the confocal microscope to produce high-quality images of fluorescent labels. The trade-off is speed and light dose. Scanning takes time, and the laser can bleach the fluorophores and damage living cells if the illumination is too intense.

Confocal microscopy is widely used to study the three-dimensional organisation of tissues, the shape of cells within embryos, and the distribution of organelles in cultured cells. Its resolution is slightly better than that of a conventional fluorescence microscope, but it remains bounded by the diffraction limit, which is why the super-resolution methods of the next chapter were developed.

Hell and Wichmann (1994) described a method that goes beyond the limit in the focal plane, and their work shows how confocal optics and new illumination strategies can be combined. Alberts et al. (2022) note that confocal microscopy has become one of the standard tools of cell biology, because it combines optical sectioning with the specificity of fluorescent labels.
