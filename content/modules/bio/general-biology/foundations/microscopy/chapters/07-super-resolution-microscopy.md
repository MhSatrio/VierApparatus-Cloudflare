Super-resolution microscopy refers to a family of methods that produce images with detail below the diffraction limit of about 200 nanometres. They do this by exploiting properties of fluorescent molecules that the classical theory did not consider, such as their ability to be switched on and off, or their random blinking. Two main strategies have proved most useful in cell biology.

The first is stimulated emission depletion, developed by Hell and Wichmann (1994). The specimen is excited by a focused laser spot, and a second laser beam with a doughnut-shaped intensity profile is overlaid on it. The doughnut switches off fluorescence in the region around the centre of the spot by stimulated emission, so that fluorescence can come only from a central region much smaller than the original spot. Scanning this smaller spot produces an image with resolution that depends on the size of the depletion, which can be well below the diffraction limit.

The second strategy is single-molecule localisation, illustrated by the work of Betzig and colleagues (2006). In this approach, only a sparse subset of fluorescent molecules is switched on at any one time. Each isolated molecule produces a blurred spot whose centre can be located very precisely, often to within a few nanometres, by fitting the spot to a model. Repeating the process over thousands of frames builds up an image from the locations of many molecules.

These methods have revealed the organisation of proteins within synapses, the arrangement of the cytoskeleton and the nanoscale structure of membranes. They are slower and more demanding than conventional microscopy, and they require careful control of the fluorophores and the illumination.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how super-resolution imaging has changed what cell biologists can say about molecular organisation. The methods are powerful, but a claim about nanoscale structure should be supported by the statistics of the localisation and by controls that show the label does not distort the structure.
