Every microscope image is the result of preparation as well as of optics. Living cells are usually imaged in a medium that keeps them alive, but many techniques require the specimen to be fixed, which means chemically preserving its structure. Chemical fixatives such as formaldehyde cross-link proteins, and alcohols and other agents precipitate them. Each fixative preserves some structures well and distorts others, so the choice depends on what the investigator wants to see.

Staining adds contrast. Dyes bind specific classes of molecule, such as nucleic acids or lipids, and they reveal structures that would otherwise be invisible. Fluorescent labels provide the same specificity with greater sensitivity. Staining is also a source of artefact. A dye may bind to something other than its intended target, may precipitate in the cell, or may change the structure it is supposed to reveal.

Mounting, sectioning and dehydration can also alter a specimen. Dehydration removes water and can collapse soft structures. Sectioning can tear membranes or compress tissues. Air drying can create bubbles and deposits. An experienced investigator recognises these effects and compares several preparations to tell them apart from real biology.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the flexibility of methods and analyses can produce results that are not robust. The same caution applies to imaging: an image that is striking but depends on a single preparation method is weak evidence for a biological claim. Replication with independent methods, and controls that show the artefact does not occur in the living cell, strengthen the claim.

Alberts et al. (2022) stress that the validity of a microscopic observation depends on understanding the preparation that produced it. Good practice is to record the fixation and staining protocol, to include negative controls such as unstained specimens and specimens lacking the labelled target, and to confirm important observations with a second method.
