A fluorescence microscope illuminates the specimen with light of one wavelength and collects light of a longer wavelength that the specimen emits. The molecules that do this are called fluorophores. A fluorophore absorbs a photon, moves to an excited electronic state, and releases energy by emitting a photon of lower energy, which has a longer wavelength. Filters separate the excitation light from the weaker emission, so that only the fluorescence reaches the detector.

Fluorophores can be attached to antibodies that bind specific proteins, which reveals the location of those proteins in fixed cells. They can also be introduced genetically. Green fluorescent protein, isolated from a jellyfish, can be fused to another protein so that the cell makes a fluorescent version of that protein. The method allows the location and movement of a protein to be followed in living cells, which is impossible with conventional antibody staining.

Fluorescence has limits. Fluorophores eventually lose their ability to emit, a process called photobleaching, and intense illumination can damage living cells. Investigators balance the brightness of the signal against the damage the light causes, and they often choose fluorophores that are stable under the conditions of the experiment.

Betzig and colleagues (2006) showed that fluorescent proteins could be imaged at a resolution beyond the diffraction limit by localising single molecules one at a time. Their work illustrates how fluorescence can be combined with clever optics and statistics to see structures far smaller than the classical limit.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe fluorescence as one of the most powerful tools in cell biology, because it links the location of a molecule to its identity. Careful controls are needed to show that the label does not change the behaviour of the protein it marks.
