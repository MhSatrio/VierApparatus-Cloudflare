A compound light microscope uses two lens systems. The objective lens, placed close to the specimen, forms a magnified image inside the microscope. The eyepiece, or ocular, magnifies that intermediate image further for the eye or a camera. Light from a source beneath the specimen passes through it, is collected by the objective, and is directed by the eyepiece to the observer. Hooke (1665) used lenses of this kind to examine thin sections of cork and describe the compartments he called cells.

Magnification is the ratio of the size of the image to the size of the object. The total magnification of a compound microscope is the product of the objective and eyepiece magnifications. Magnification alone does not reveal detail, and increasing it beyond a certain point produces a larger but blurry image. The distinction between magnification and resolution is the central idea of this module.

Resolution is the smallest distance between two points that can be seen as separate. For a light microscope, it is limited by the wavelength of light and the numerical aperture of the objective, which is a measure of how much of the light emitted by the specimen the lens can collect. The practical limit for visible light is about 200 nanometres, which is why bacteria are visible as small objects but the internal structure of a ribosome is not.

Good imaging also depends on contrast. Most living cells are almost transparent, and their structures differ only slightly in refractive index. Unstained cells are hard to see without special techniques, and this limitation led to the development of phase contrast, which is the subject of the next chapter.

Alberts et al. (2022) explain that the choice of objective, illumination and detector determines what a researcher can see. The first decision in any microscopy experiment is therefore what detail is needed and what light the sample can tolerate.
