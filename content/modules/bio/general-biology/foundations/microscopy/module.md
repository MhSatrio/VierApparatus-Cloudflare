# Microscopy

Almost everything known about cells was learned by looking at them. Microscopy is the set of methods that make small structures visible, and every module in the curriculum depends on it, directly or indirectly. This module explains how microscopes form images, what limits the detail they can show, and how the main families of instruments are used in biology.

The chapters follow the logic of the instrument. They begin with the optics of the light microscope and the diffraction limit that sets its resolution. They then describe brightfield and phase contrast, which reveal unstained specimens, and fluorescence, which reveals specific molecules. Electron microscopy goes far beyond light in resolution, and confocal microscopy removes out-of-focus light from thick samples. Super-resolution methods, developed from the 1990s onward, overcome the diffraction limit under specific conditions. The final chapter covers the preparation of specimens, which can create artefacts that look like biology but are not.

Readers should note that a microscope image is a construction. It depends on the physics of the instrument, the chemistry of the labelling and the choices made during preparation. Learning to read an image critically is as important as learning to take one.
