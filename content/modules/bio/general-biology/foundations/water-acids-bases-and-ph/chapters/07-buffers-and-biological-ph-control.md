A buffer is a solution that resists changes in pH when acid or base is added. Most buffers consist of a weak acid and its conjugate base, present in comparable amounts. When a strong acid is added, the base in the buffer takes up the extra protons. When a strong base is added, the acid releases protons to neutralise the hydroxide. The pH changes only slightly, because the buffer absorbs most of the added material.

The buffering capacity of a solution is greatest when the pH is close to the pKa of the weak acid. The relation between pH, pKa and the ratio of conjugate base to acid is given by the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation. A buffer that is well matched to the pH it needs to maintain has a high capacity, while one that is poorly matched has little effect.

Cells depend on buffers. Phosphate buffers operate near pH 7, which is close to the interior pH of many cells, and the phosphate groups of proteins and nucleotides act as buffers within the cell. Proteins themselves are buffers, because their side chains can accept and release protons over a range of pH values.

The bicarbonate buffer system is the most important buffer in the extracellular fluid of animals. Carbon dioxide dissolves in blood and forms carbonic acid, which dissociates into bicarbonate and hydrogen ions. The enzyme carbonic anhydrase accelerates this equilibrium, and the lungs and kidneys regulate the concentrations of carbon dioxide and bicarbonate. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the body keeps blood pH within a narrow range, because deviations can damage proteins and disrupt enzyme activity.

Biologists measure buffer capacity by titration and by determining how much acid or base a solution can absorb before its pH changes by a set amount. These measurements show whether a biological system can withstand the stresses that it normally encounters.
