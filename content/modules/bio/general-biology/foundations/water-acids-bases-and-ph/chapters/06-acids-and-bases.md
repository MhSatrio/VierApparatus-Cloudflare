In the proton-transfer definition, an acid is a substance that donates a proton and a base is one that accepts a proton. Hydrochloric acid donates its proton to water, producing hydronium ions and chloride, and water acts as the base. Ammonia accepts a proton from water and produces ammonium and hydroxide, so water acts as the acid in that reaction. Water is therefore amphiprotic: it can act as either an acid or a base, depending on its partner.

Acid strength describes how completely a substance donates its proton. Strong acids such as hydrochloric acid are almost fully dissociated in water. Weak acids, which include most biological acids, dissociate only partly and establish an equilibrium between the protonated and deprotonated forms. The extent of dissociation is given by the acid dissociation constant, Ka, or more conveniently by its negative logarithm, pKa.

The pKa of a weak acid is the pH at which half of its molecules are protonated and half are deprotonated. This single number is one of the most useful in biochemistry, because it tells the biologist which form of a molecule will predominate at a given pH. Amino acid side chains, the phosphate groups of nucleotides and the carboxyl groups of fatty acids all have characteristic pKa values, and their ionisation state changes with the pH of their surroundings.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the ionisation of amino acid side chains affects the activity of enzymes, since a catalytic residue must be in the correct protonation state to function. A change in pH can therefore switch an enzyme on or off without any change in its amount.

Acid and base strengths are measured by titration, in which a solution of known concentration is added to a sample while the pH is recorded. The shape of the titration curve reveals the pKa values of the groups in the molecule, and it is a standard method for characterising biological molecules.
