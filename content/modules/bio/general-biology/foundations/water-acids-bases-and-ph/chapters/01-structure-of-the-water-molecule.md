A water molecule consists of one oxygen atom bonded to two hydrogen atoms. The oxygen atom has six valence electrons, and it shares two of them with the hydrogens. The remaining four form two lone pairs that occupy space on the oxygen. These lone pairs push the bonded hydrogens closer together, so the molecule has a bent shape with an angle of about 104.5 degrees between the two O-H bonds, rather than the straight line of a linear molecule.

The bent shape and the difference in electronegativity between oxygen and hydrogen make water strongly polar. Oxygen attracts the shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen does, so the oxygen end of the molecule carries a partial negative charge and the two hydrogen ends carry partial positive charges. The molecule therefore behaves like a small dipole, with one end attracted to positive charges and the other to negative charges.

Polarity is the reason water dissolves salts and sugars. Water molecules surround a positive ion with their negative oxygen ends and a negative ion with their positive hydrogen ends, which weakens the attraction between the ions and draws them into solution. Sugars with many hydroxyl groups form hydrogen bonds with water and dissolve readily, while nonpolar substances such as oil do not.

Ball (2008) emphasised that the structure of water is not static. The molecule's polarity allows it to take part in the chemistry of the cell as a reactant and as a medium, rather than only as a passive background. Hydrolysis, in which a bond is broken by the addition of water, is one of the most common reactions in biology, and it depends directly on water's polar structure.

Water's structure is studied by X-ray and neutron diffraction, by infrared and Raman spectroscopy, and by computer simulation. Each technique reveals a different aspect of the molecule's geometry and of the hydrogen bonds it forms with neighbours.
