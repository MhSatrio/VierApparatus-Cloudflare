Most of the water in a cell is held in the cytosol, where it acts as the solvent for proteins, metabolites and ions. Water also moves across the plasma membrane and the membranes of organelles, and the direction and rate of that movement determine the volume and internal pressure of the cell. Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a semipermeable membrane, from a region of lower solute concentration toward one of higher concentration.

The direction of water movement depends on the osmotic gradient. A solution with more dissolved particles than the cell's interior is hypertonic to the cell, and water leaves the cell, which shrinks. A solution with fewer particles is hypotonic, and water enters, which can swell and burst a cell that lacks a rigid wall. A solution with the same concentration is isotonic, and the cell maintains its volume. Red blood cells in saline solutions of different strengths show these three responses clearly.

Cells have several mechanisms for managing water. Aquaporins are channels in the membrane that allow water to pass quickly, and the number of channels in a membrane affects how rapidly the cell can respond to an osmotic change. Plant cells surrounded by a rigid wall resist swelling, and their internal pressure, called turgor, keeps the plant upright. Many single-celled organisms pump ions out of the cell to counteract the influx of water.

Ball (2008) argued that water within cells is not uniform. Water near macromolecules, in crowded compartments and at membranes can have properties that differ from bulk water, and these differences can influence the rates of reactions and the stability of structures. The view of water as a passive medium is therefore incomplete.

Water movement is measured by weighing cells or tissues, by observing changes in cell volume under a microscope and by measuring the pressure needed to prevent osmosis. These measurements reveal how cells respond to their environment and how their water balance is regulated.
