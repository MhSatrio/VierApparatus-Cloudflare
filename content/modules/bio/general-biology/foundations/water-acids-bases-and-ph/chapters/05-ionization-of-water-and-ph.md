Pure water is not entirely made of water molecules. A small fraction of them transfer a proton from one molecule to another, producing a hydronium ion, usually written as H+ for simplicity, and a hydroxide ion, OH-. The reaction is an equilibrium: 2 H2O is in balance with H+ and OH-. At 25 degrees Celsius, the product of the hydrogen ion and hydroxide ion concentrations is 1.0 times 10 to the power minus 14 molar squared, which is called the ion product of water.

In pure water the two concentrations are equal, each about 1.0 times 10 to the power minus 7 molar. Solutions in which the hydrogen ion concentration is higher than this are acidic, and solutions in which it is lower are basic. The relation between the two concentrations means that adding an acid increases H+ and reduces OH-, while adding a base does the reverse.

The pH scale converts the very small hydrogen ion concentration into a more convenient number. It is defined as the negative logarithm, base ten, of the hydrogen ion concentration. Pure water at 25 degrees Celsius has a hydrogen ion concentration of 1.0 times 10 to the power minus 7 molar, and its pH is therefore 7. Each unit change in pH corresponds to a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration, so a pH of 6 is ten times more acidic than a pH of 7.

Ball (2008) noted that the hydrogen ion is a central actor in cell chemistry, because it influences the charge of amino acid side chains, the binding of substrates to enzymes and the transport of ions across membranes. A small change in pH can therefore alter many processes at once.

pH is measured with glass electrodes, which generate a voltage that depends on the hydrogen ion concentration, and with indicator dyes, which change colour over a defined range. Careful calibration with standard buffers is essential, since an electrode that drifts by a few tenths of a unit can change the interpretation of an experiment.
