Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius at sea level, while molecules of similar size and mass, such as hydrogen sulfide, boil far below room temperature. The difference comes from hydrogen bonding. Each water molecule can form several hydrogen bonds with its neighbours, and a great deal of energy is needed to break them before molecules can escape into the vapour. Liquid water is therefore a dynamic network of bonds that continually break and reform, and this network controls most of its physical properties.

The same network gives water a high heat capacity. Raising the temperature of water requires energy to increase molecular motion, and part of that energy goes into stretching and breaking hydrogen bonds. As a result, water absorbs a large amount of heat for a small rise in temperature. This buffering of temperature is important for organisms, since a cell full of water changes temperature much more slowly than a cell made of most other materials.

Water is also cohesive. Water molecules stick to one another, which allows water to rise through the narrow vessels of plants against gravity, and it produces surface tension at the air-water boundary. Ball (2008) pointed out that these properties are not confined to the bulk liquid. The organisation of water near surfaces, such as membranes and proteins, differs from the bulk, and this interfacial water influences how biomolecules behave.

Water's density shows an unusual pattern. Liquid water is densest at about four degrees Celsius, and ice is less dense than liquid water. In ice, each molecule is held in an open lattice by hydrogen bonds, which leaves more space between molecules than in the liquid. Because ice floats, lakes freeze from the top down, and the water beneath remains liquid, which allows aquatic life to survive the winter.

These properties are measured by calorimetry, which determines heat capacity, by tensiometry, which measures surface tension, and by density measurements across temperature. Each measurement is a window onto the hydrogen-bond network that holds the liquid together.
