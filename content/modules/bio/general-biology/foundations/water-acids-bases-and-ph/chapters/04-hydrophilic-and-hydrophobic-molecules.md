Hydrophilic molecules are those that interact favourably with water. They carry charged groups, such as the carboxylate of an amino acid, or polar groups, such as the hydroxyl groups of a sugar, which form hydrogen bonds with water. Hydrophobic molecules are those that do not. Long hydrocarbon chains, the nonpolar parts of fatty acids and the side chains of leucine and phenylalanine are hydrophobic. Most biological molecules are partly hydrophilic and partly hydrophobic, and the balance determines how they behave.

The hydrophobic effect is the tendency of nonpolar groups to cluster together in water. It is not caused by an attraction between the nonpolar groups themselves, which are weak. It arises because water molecules around a nonpolar surface must rearrange into a more ordered cage that they cannot form easily. Clustering the nonpolar groups reduces the total area of ordered water, which is entropically favourable. Ball (2008) discussed how the structure of water near such surfaces contributes to this effect.

The hydrophobic effect is the main driving force for the formation of membranes. Phospholipids have a hydrophilic head and hydrophobic tails, and in water they arrange into a bilayer, with the tails pointing inward and the heads facing the surrounding water. The bilayer is a stable sheet, not a rigid one, and it can curve to form closed compartments. This self-assembly is one of the features that makes compartmentalisation possible in living cells, as discussed in the Biological Organization module.

Proteins fold in a similar way. Hydrophobic side chains tend to be buried in the interior of a folded protein, away from water, while hydrophilic side chains are exposed on the surface. The pattern of hydrophobic and hydrophilic residues in a sequence is one of the clues that help predict how a protein will fold.

Hydrophobicity is measured by partition coefficients, which compare the concentration of a molecule in water and in an organic solvent, and by chromatography in which the retention of a molecule reflects its affinity for a nonpolar stationary phase. Such measurements allow biologists to rank molecules by their hydrophobicity and to predict their behaviour in cells.
