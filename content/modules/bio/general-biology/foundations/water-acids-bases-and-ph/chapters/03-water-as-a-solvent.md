A solvent dissolves a solute to form a solution. Water is the most important solvent in biology, because it dissolves a wider range of substances than any other common liquid. Ionic compounds dissolve by hydration, in which water molecules surround each ion. Polar molecules dissolve because their charged or polar groups form hydrogen bonds with water, and nonpolar molecules remain largely insoluble.

Hydration is a shell of water molecules arranged around an ion. Sodium, which is small and carries a single positive charge, attracts water molecules tightly and is surrounded by a well-defined shell. Larger ions attract less tightly. The shell changes the effective size of the ion as it moves through solution, and this is one reason that ions of different size move through membrane channels at different rates.

Ball (2008) argued that the water surrounding biomolecules is not a featureless backdrop. Water molecules bound to proteins and nucleic acids can change their conformation and their interactions, and in some cases water is needed for a protein to fold or a ligand to bind. The solvent is therefore part of the molecular machinery of the cell.

Solutions also show colligative effects. The freezing point of water falls, and its boiling point rises, when a solute is dissolved in it, and the change depends on the number of dissolved particles rather than their identity. Osmosis, the movement of water across a membrane toward a solution with more solute, is a consequence of the same principle. Cells must control their solute concentration to avoid swelling or shrinking when placed in different environments.

Measurements of solubility and of hydration use conductivity, which depends on ion mobility, and spectroscopy, which reveals changes in the vibrations of water molecules near an ion. Together they show how water surrounds and interacts with solutes, and how that interaction determines what can be dissolved and at what concentration.
