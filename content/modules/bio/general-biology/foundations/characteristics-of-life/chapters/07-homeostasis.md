Homeostasis is the maintenance of stable internal conditions. Temperature, pH, the concentration of ions and the volume of fluid inside an organism tend to remain within limits even when the environment changes. The stability is not a sign of inactivity. It is produced by continuous adjustment, and it breaks down when the adjustment fails.

Feedback is the mechanism that makes homeostasis possible. In a negative feedback loop, a change in a variable triggers a response that opposes the change. When blood glucose rises after a meal, the pancreas secretes insulin, which promotes uptake of glucose into cells and storage as glycogen. As glucose falls, the signal weakens and secretion stops. The system does not hold glucose at a single value, but it keeps the value within a range that the body can tolerate.

Cells use the same logic. Many enzymes are inhibited by the products of the pathways they catalyse, so that a metabolic route slows when its output accumulates. Ion pumps in the plasma membrane restore the concentration gradients that the cell depends on, and they consume energy to do so. The energy cost is part of the homeostatic strategy: stability is paid for.

Homeostasis is costly, and the cost sets limits. Lane and Martin (2010) connected energy budgets to the complexity a cell can maintain, and the same budget applies to regulation. A system that must maintain many variables needs many sensors, many effectors and a steady supply of energy to run them.

Biologists study homeostasis by perturbing a system and measuring how quickly it returns to its set range. The response time, the amplitude of deviation and the mechanism of return together characterise how well a system regulates itself, and the same measurements are used in medicine to judge whether a patient's control systems are working.
