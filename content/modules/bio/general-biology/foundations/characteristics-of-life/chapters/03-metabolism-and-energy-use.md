Metabolism is the total of the chemical reactions that a living system carries out. It is commonly divided into two parts. Catabolism breaks large molecules into smaller ones and releases energy in the process, as when glucose is oxidised during respiration. Anabolism uses energy to build larger molecules, as when amino acids are joined into proteins. The two halves are linked, and most of the energy released by one is spent on the other.

The link is carried mainly by the nucleotide adenosine triphosphate, usually written ATP. Hydrolysis of its terminal phosphate bond releases a usable amount of free energy, and that energy is coupled to reactions that would not occur spontaneously. Alberts et al. (2022) present ATP as the common currency of energy in cells, a role that reflects its position at the centre of catabolic and anabolic pathways.

Energy enters the living world in different forms. Photosynthetic organisms capture light and store it in chemical bonds. Chemotrophs obtain energy by oxidising inorganic or organic compounds. Heterotrophs eat other organisms and extract their stored energy. These strategies are all metabolic, and the differences among them shape the ecology of every habitat on Earth.

Energy supply imposes limits on what a cell can be. Lane and Martin (2010) argued that the energy available per gene differs greatly between prokaryotes and eukaryotes, and that this difference constrains the number and size of genes a cell can maintain. Their argument is contested, but it shows why metabolism is not merely a biochemical detail. Energetic economics may determine the architecture of life.

Metabolism is also measured in practice. Respirometry records oxygen consumption, calorimetry records heat output, and isotopic labelling traces the fate of atoms through a pathway. Each measure has limits, but together they allow biologists to say how much energy a system is using and what it is using it for.
