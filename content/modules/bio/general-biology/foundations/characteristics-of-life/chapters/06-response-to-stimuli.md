A living system responds to changes in its surroundings. A bacterium swims up a gradient of a nutrient, a leaf turns toward light, and an animal withdraws its hand from a hot surface. In each case a stimulus is detected, the information is passed through the system, and a response follows. Koshland (2002) included this capacity, which he called improvisation, among the organising principles of life, because it allows a system to adjust to conditions that its genome did not specify in advance.

Detection is carried out by receptors. In bacteria, transmembrane proteins bind specific molecules outside the cell and change conformation, which alters their activity inside the cell. In animals, photoreceptors in the retina absorb light and change shape, starting an electrochemical signal. The sensitivity and specificity of a receptor determine which stimuli the system can respond to.

Signal transduction converts the detection event into a form that the rest of the cell can use. Phosphorylation cascades, changes in second messenger concentrations and alterations in gene expression are common mechanisms. Alberts et al. (2022) describe these pathways as networks rather than simple chains, because many signals converge on the same targets and a single receptor can activate several outputs.

The response may be fast or slow. A neuron can change its firing rate within milliseconds, while a plant may take days to redirect its growth. The timescale depends on whether the response requires new proteins to be synthesised or only the modification of existing ones.

Measuring responses is a standard method in experimental biology. Investigators apply a defined stimulus, record the response, and vary one factor at a time. Dose-response curves show how the strength of the response depends on the intensity of the stimulus, and the shape of that curve often reveals how many steps lie between detection and action.
