Biologists rarely agree on a single sentence that defines life, and many of them doubt that one can be written. The problem is that every simple property that has been proposed is shared by something non-living, and every living system lacks some property that was once thought essential. Growth is shared by crystals and flames. Reproduction is shared by a crystal seeded in a supersaturated solution. Metabolism is shared by a flame that consumes fuel. A definition built on a single property therefore either admits non-living objects or excludes living ones.

For this reason, working biologists describe life by a cluster of properties and ask how many of them a system displays and how well it displays each one. Koshland (2002) organised the properties around a few general principles, and most textbooks follow a similar strategy. The cluster approach does not give a yes or no answer for every case, but it produces answers that can be checked against evidence.

The approach also clarifies what a dispute is about. When researchers argue over whether a virus is alive, they usually disagree about how much weight to give to replication that depends on a host, since a virus has its own genome and structure but no independent metabolism. The argument is informative because it shows which properties the disputants regard as central. A definition that works well for cells may need to be adjusted for entities that exist only inside cells.

Studying the cluster has practical value. A microbiologist who needs to decide whether a sample contains living bacteria can test for metabolic activity, division and the uptake of a labelled substrate. A conservation biologist assessing a population can count reproduction and survival. In each case the properties are measured rather than assumed, and the result is only as good as the choice of measurements.

The lesson of this chapter is that life is best understood as a pattern of properties that co-occur, and that a clear definition must say which properties are required and which are merely typical.
