Living systems are ordered. A bacterial cell contains thousands of distinct molecular species arranged in membranes, compartments and polymers, and the arrangement is far from random. Proteins fold into specific shapes, membranes separate the inside of a cell from its surroundings, and the genetic material is packaged in a defined way. Compared with the chemicals that surround it, the cell is a highly organised state.

Order alone is not unusual, since crystals are also ordered. What distinguishes a living system is that it maintains its order by continuous exchange with its environment. A crystal stays ordered as long as nothing disturbs it. A cell must take in nutrients, release waste and spend energy to keep its arrangement intact. When the flow of energy stops, the order decays, which is one of the most practical signs of death.

The thermodynamic framing is useful but must be used carefully. The second law of thermodynamics states that the total entropy of an isolated system does not decrease. A living cell lowers its own entropy by exporting disorder to the environment, usually as heat and waste molecules. The total entropy still rises, so there is no violation of physical law. Koshland (2002) emphasised the compartmentalisation of chemistry as one of the organising principles that make this possible.

Biologists measure organization in several ways. Electron microscopy reveals the ultrastructure of membranes and organelles. Fluorescent labelling shows whether a protein is concentrated in one compartment or spread evenly through the cell. Measures of gradients, such as differences in ion concentration across a membrane, quantify how far a cell departs from equilibrium with its surroundings.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell as a system whose order is generated and sustained by the same molecular machinery that carries out its functions. This means that order cannot be separated from activity: a cell that stops working also stops being ordered in the same way.
