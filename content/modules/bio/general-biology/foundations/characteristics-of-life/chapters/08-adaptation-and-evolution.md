The last of the widely cited properties of life is adaptation over generations. Individual organisms respond to their environment during their lives, and that response is a property of the individual. Evolutionary adaptation is different. It is a change in the heritable traits of a population, produced when some variants leave more surviving offspring than others. The two are related, but they operate on different timescales and through different mechanisms.

Dobzhansky (1973) argued that the traits of organisms make sense only in the light of their history, and adaptation is the clearest instance. A feature such as the thick fur of an Arctic mammal is explained by the survival of ancestors that carried variants producing more insulation. The feature is not chosen by the individual during its life. It is the outcome of many generations of differential survival.

Three conditions are needed for evolution by natural selection to occur: variation among individuals, heritability of that variation, and differences in the number of offspring that survive to reproduce. If one of them is absent, the population does not change in the way selection predicts. The conditions are simple to state and often difficult to test in natural populations.

Adaptation is therefore not perfect or permanent. An environment that changes faster than a population can respond may leave the population poorly suited to its conditions. Antibiotic resistance in bacteria is an example of rapid adaptation, since a population exposed to a drug can evolve resistance within a small number of generations.

Koshland (2002) included adaptability among the organising principles of living systems, and the two levels of adaptation, individual and evolutionary, together account for much of the persistence of life. Biologists measure adaptation by comparing traits across populations, by tracking allele frequencies over time, and by testing whether a trait improves survival or reproduction in the environment where it occurs.
