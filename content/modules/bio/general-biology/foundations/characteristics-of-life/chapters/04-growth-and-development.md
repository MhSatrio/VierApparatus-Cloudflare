Growth and development are often used as if they meant the same thing, but they describe different changes. Growth is an increase in size, mass or number of cells. Development is the process by which an organism acquires its adult structure and function, including the formation of tissues, organs and behaviours in a specific order. A tadpole that grows larger has grown. A tadpole that develops legs and lungs has developed.

Both processes depend on information. Koshland (2002) listed a genetic program among the features of living systems, and development is the clearest case of that program in action. The information in the genome does not dictate every detail directly. It specifies which proteins are made, when and where, and those proteins build structures in a sequence that depends on signals from neighbouring cells.

Growth requires matter and energy, but it is controlled rather than unlimited. A cell divides when signals tell it to divide and stops when those signals end. In multicellular organisms, the rate and extent of growth are coordinated among tissues, so that a limb grows in proportion to the rest of the body. When that control fails, the result can be uncontrolled proliferation, as in many cancers.

The environment also shapes growth. Plants respond to light, water and nutrients by changing their rate of growth and the direction in which they grow. Animals respond to food supply, temperature and social cues. Two genetically identical organisms raised in different conditions can differ in size and shape, which shows that the genome sets a range of possible outcomes rather than a single fixed result.

Biologists study growth by measuring mass, length and cell number over time, and they study development by tracing the fate of individual cells with labels or by imaging embryos. Alberts et al. (2022) describe development as a sequence of regulated decisions, and the same logic applies whether the organism is a bacterium, which grows and divides, or a mammal, which builds a body plan over many years.
