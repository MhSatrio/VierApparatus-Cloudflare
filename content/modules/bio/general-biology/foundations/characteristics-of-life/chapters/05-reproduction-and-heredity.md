Reproduction is the production of new individuals or new cells from existing ones. Bacteria and many single-celled eukaryotes reproduce asexually, usually by dividing in two. Many plants and animals reproduce sexually, combining genetic material from two parents to form offspring that differ from both. Both strategies pass on the organisation of the parent, but they differ in how much of the genetic information is copied unchanged.

Heredity is the transmission of information from parent to offspring through the chemical structure of genetic material. Watson and Crick (1953) proposed that DNA consists of two complementary strands, so that each strand can serve as a template for the other. This structure explains how a cell can duplicate its genome before dividing, and why the two daughter cells receive the same sequence of bases.

Copying is not perfect. DNA polymerases make errors at a low rate, and many errors are corrected by proofreading and repair systems before they become permanent. Some errors persist. A mutation that changes a single base may alter a protein, may have no effect, or may be harmful. Over generations, mutations provide the raw material on which natural selection acts, so the fidelity of copying is a balance between preserving information and generating variation.

Sexual reproduction adds further variation. Meiosis shuffles the combinations of chromosomes and the segments that exchange between them, so that each gamete carries a different mixture of parental genes. Fertilisation then combines two such mixtures. The result is that the offspring of one pair of parents can differ widely from one another and from either parent.

Biologists study heredity by crossing organisms with known traits and counting the offspring, by sequencing genomes to compare parents and children, and by introducing specific mutations to see their effects. The principle is the same across these methods: the information that passes between generations is a physical molecule, and it can be read, compared and changed.
