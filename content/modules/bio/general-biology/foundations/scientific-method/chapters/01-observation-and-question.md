Most biological research begins with an observation. A microbiologist notices that a colony of bacteria grows poorly on a plate that normally supports it. A field worker counts fewer frogs in a pond than in previous years. A clinician sees that patients who share a genetic variant respond differently to a drug. Each observation is a fact about the world, but it is not yet a question, and a question is what allows a study to be designed.

The first step is to separate what was seen from what was inferred. "The colony is smaller on this plate" is an observation. "The plate lacks a nutrient the bacteria need" is an interpretation. Keeping the two apart matters, because an interpretation can be wrong while the observation remains correct. Good notes record the measurement, the conditions, the number of samples and any unusual features, and they leave interpretation for later.

Once the observation is clear, it can be turned into a question. A useful question names the factor that might explain the observation and the outcome that would be affected by it. "Why does the colony grow poorly?" is too broad to test. "Does removing the iron source from the plate reduce colony size?" names a factor, an outcome and a comparison. The narrower question is less exciting, but it can be answered.

Platt (1964) argued that the progress of a field depends on whether its investigators ask questions that can be decided by a single experiment. He compared fields in which many alternative explanations were pursued with fields in which a single explanation was tested over and over. The contrast is a lesson about question design: a question that admits several answers should be broken into parts that can each be tested.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe many discoveries that began with an unexpected observation and became productive only when the observation was translated into a specific question. Observation supplies the problem. The question defines the experiment, and the experiment determines what can be learned.
