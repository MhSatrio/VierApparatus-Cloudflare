An experiment is a deliberate intervention designed to reveal a cause. The investigator changes one factor, holds others constant, and measures what happens. The power of the design comes from the comparison it produces. If the treated group differs from the untreated group in a way the untreated group does not, the difference is evidence that the treatment caused it.

The factor that the investigator changes is called the independent variable. The measurement that responds is the dependent variable. In the iron example from the previous chapter, the independent variable is the presence or absence of added iron, and the dependent variable is colony size after a fixed time. Every other condition, such as temperature, the strain of bacteria, the composition of the medium and the time of measurement, should be the same for all plates. Any variable that changes with the treatment and affects the outcome is a confound, and confounds make the result ambiguous.

Designing an experiment means deciding, before the work begins, how each outcome will be measured and what result would support each hypothesis. Platt (1964) argued that this step is often neglected, and that investigators sometimes run an experiment and then search for a measurement that gives an interesting answer. A design fixed in advance prevents that drift.

Biological materials add complications. Living samples vary, they change during the experiment, and they respond to handling. A well-designed experiment controls for these factors by randomising the order of treatments, by using samples from more than one source, and by recording conditions that might matter. Where randomisation is impossible, as in many field studies, the investigator must describe the comparison honestly and consider what alternative explanations remain.

Alberts et al. (2022) illustrate the logic of experimental design through classic studies in cell biology, in which a single well-chosen comparison answered a question that had resisted many less controlled attempts. The lesson applies at every scale: plan the comparison first, then decide what to measure.
