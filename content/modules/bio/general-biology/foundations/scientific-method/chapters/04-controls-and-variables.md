A control is a condition that shares every feature of the experiment except the one being tested. Controls are the reference points against which an effect is judged. Without them, a change in the outcome cannot be attributed to the treatment, because the same change might have arisen from something else that happened during the experiment.

A negative control is a condition in which the treatment is expected to have no effect. Plates inoculated with the same bacteria and left without added compound show the baseline growth of the strain. If the compound is expected to inhibit growth, the negative control shows what normal growth looks like. A negative control also detects contamination: if the negative control grows when it should not, the experiment has a problem that the treatment cannot explain.

A positive control is a condition in which the expected effect is known to occur. It shows that the assay is capable of detecting the effect at all. If a method is designed to detect a change in protein levels, a positive control with a known change confirms that the detection system works. Without this check, a negative result could mean that the treatment had no effect or that the assay was insensitive, and the two cannot be distinguished.

Variables that are not controlled introduce bias. Temperature differences between incubators, different batches of medium, and changes in technician or time of day can all alter the outcome. Ioannidis (2005) argued that many published findings are unreliable partly because the number of flexible choices in design, measurement and analysis is large, and each choice can shift the result toward a desired conclusion. Fixing as many variables as possible, and recording those that cannot be fixed, reduces this risk.

Good practice is to state all controls in the protocol before the experiment begins and to report them in the publication. A reader should be able to see what was compared with what, and why each comparison was chosen.
