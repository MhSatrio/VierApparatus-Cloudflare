A result is reproducible when an independent investigator, following the same method, obtains the same answer. Reproducibility is the strongest check that a finding reflects something real about the world rather than an accident of one laboratory, one batch of reagents or one analyst. Many biological findings have failed this check when others attempted to repeat them, and the failures have led to a more careful examination of how research is done.

Publication bias is one reason the literature can mislead. Studies that find a clear, positive effect are more likely to be written up and accepted than studies that find no effect. If many laboratories test the same hypothesis and only the positive results are published, the published record overstates the evidence for the hypothesis. The problem is not dishonesty by any single investigator but a filter that operates across the field.

Ioannidis (2005) modelled this process and concluded that, under plausible conditions, a large share of published research findings can be false. The reasons include small sample sizes, the flexibility of analysis, bias in design and the pressure to report positive outcomes. The argument is a model rather than a measurement of any particular field, but it identifies the mechanisms that any field should check.

Platt (1964) anticipated some of these concerns when he advocated comparing alternative hypotheses rather than seeking confirmation of one. A field that tests alternatives and publishes the outcome of each test is less exposed to selective reporting than one that pursues a favoured explanation.

Practical measures improve reproducibility. Investigators can register their planned analyses before collecting data, report all experiments including negative ones, share raw data and protocols, and replicate key findings in independent laboratories. Each measure makes it harder for an unlucky result to disappear and harder for a lucky one to be overstated.
