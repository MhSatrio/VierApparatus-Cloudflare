Science is a public activity. A result has value only when it is communicated in a form that others can assess, check and build upon. The main channels are journal articles, conference presentations, preprints, datasets and the protocols that accompany them. Each channel serves the same purpose: to make the claim, the evidence and the methods available for scrutiny.

Peer review is the process by which editors and independent experts evaluate a manuscript before publication. Reviewers assess whether the question is well defined, whether the design can answer it, whether the statistics are appropriate, and whether the conclusions go beyond the evidence. Peer review can catch errors and improve presentation. It cannot guarantee that a result is correct, because reviewers usually see the manuscript but not the raw data, and they cannot repeat the experiment.

Ioannidis (2005) pointed out that the review process operates within the same incentives that shape the choice of what to study and report. Reviewers and editors are more likely to accept results that are novel and positive, which reinforces publication bias. Peer review is therefore one important check among several, and it works best when combined with replication and open data.

The detail of a report matters. A methods section should describe the organisms or samples, the reagents, the conditions, the controls and the analysis in enough detail that a competent investigator could repeat the work. Vague descriptions make a result difficult to test, and a result that cannot be tested cannot be trusted with confidence.

Alberts et al. (2022) stress that the conventions of scientific writing are not ornamental. They are tools for reporting observations accurately, separating data from interpretation, and giving credit to the sources on which a study depends. A reader who understands these conventions can judge the strength of a paper before reading its conclusions.
