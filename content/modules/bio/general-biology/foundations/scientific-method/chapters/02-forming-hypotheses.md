A hypothesis is a proposed explanation for an observation, stated so that it can be tested. In biology a hypothesis usually takes the form of a causal claim: a particular factor influences a particular outcome, through a particular mechanism. The mechanism does not always have to be known in advance, but the claim must say what should happen if it is true and what should happen if it is false.

A useful hypothesis makes a prediction that differs from the predictions of its rivals. Suppose a bacterial colony grows poorly on a plate. One hypothesis says that the plate lacks iron. Another says that it contains a compound that inhibits growth. Adding iron to the plate predicts recovery under the first hypothesis and no recovery under the second. The experiment is informative because the two hypotheses diverge in their predictions.

Platt (1964) recommended that investigators keep several hypotheses in view at once. Each hypothesis should be written down with its predicted outcome for each planned experiment. When the results arrive, the investigator can eliminate those hypotheses whose predictions failed and concentrate on the remainder. A single favoured hypothesis invites the investigator to look for confirmation, which is much easier to find than refutation.

The requirement that a hypothesis could be shown false is important. A statement that explains every possible outcome gives no guidance about what to measure. Biological explanations sometimes drift into this condition, for example by invoking an unobserved factor that can be adjusted to fit any result. A careful researcher asks what observation would count against the hypothesis, and designs the experiment to make that observation possible.

Hypotheses are also provisional. A hypothesis that survives a test has not been proved. It has been exposed to a risk and has survived that risk, and the next test may show that it applies only in some conditions. Good practice is to state the scope of a hypothesis, the organisms, tissues or conditions to which it is meant to apply, so that the boundaries of its success are clear from the start.
