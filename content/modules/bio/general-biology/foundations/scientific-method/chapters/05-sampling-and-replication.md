Living systems vary. Two genetically identical cultures of bacteria grown in the same flask will not behave in exactly the same way, and two mice from the same litter will differ in weight, behaviour and immune response. A single measurement therefore cannot distinguish an effect of treatment from the ordinary spread of biological variation. Replication, the repetition of the experiment with new samples, is the means of estimating that spread.

Biologists distinguish technical replicates from biological replicates. Technical replicates repeat the measurement on the same sample, for example running the same extract through a gel twice. They reveal errors in the measurement process but say nothing about variation between samples. Biological replicates use independent samples, such as separate cultures, different animals or different patients. Only biological replicates allow the investigator to generalise beyond the sample that was studied.

The number of biological replicates determines how precisely the effect can be estimated. A treatment that changes an outcome by a large amount may be visible in three replicates. A subtle effect may need dozens. Power analysis, which is discussed in the statistics modules of the curriculum, provides a formal way to choose the number in advance.

Sampling also matters. A sample should represent the population about which the investigator wishes to draw a conclusion. A study of a single laboratory strain of a bacterium says little about the species as a whole, and a study of mice of one age and sex says little about all mammals. Ioannidis (2005) noted that small studies with restricted samples are more likely to produce results that do not hold in larger or more varied populations.

Alberts et al. (2022) emphasise that the reliability of cell biology rests on repeated observations under varied conditions, not on a single striking image. A result that appears in several independent replicates, prepared on different days, is far more persuasive than one that appears once.
