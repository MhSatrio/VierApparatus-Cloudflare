# Scientific Method

Biology advances by testing claims against observations that could show them to be wrong. This module develops that idea in practical terms. It covers how a question becomes a hypothesis, how an experiment is designed so that its result can discriminate between hypotheses, how replication and statistics guard against chance, and how the scientific literature itself can introduce errors.

The first three chapters follow the working cycle of a laboratory project: observation and question, hypothesis and prediction, and experimental design. The next two address what makes evidence trustworthy: controls and variables, then sampling and replication. The last three examine the statistical and social side of research, including the problems that Ioannidis (2005) analysed, and the processes of peer review and communication through which claims are checked.

No single chapter claims that the method is simple. Biological systems are complex, experiments are expensive, and many questions cannot be answered by a single decisive test. The module therefore presents the method as a set of safeguards, each addressing a specific way that a conclusion can go wrong, and it asks the reader to identify which safeguard is missing when a claim seems too strong.

Readers should work through the chapters in order. Later chapters refer to the vocabulary of the earlier ones, and the reference list gives the primary sources for the methodological arguments.
