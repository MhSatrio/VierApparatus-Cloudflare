Every cell requires a continuous supply of energy. The energy is needed to build macromolecules, to pump ions across membranes, to move the cytoskeleton, and to keep the cell's structure from decaying. Cells obtain this energy in two main ways. Phototrophs capture light and store it in chemical bonds, while chemotrophs extract energy from the oxidation of chemical fuels such as sugars and fats.

The energy is held temporarily in molecules that can release it in controlled steps. ATP is the most important of these carriers. Its hydrolysis releases energy that is coupled to reactions that would not occur on their own, as described in the Chemistry for Biology module. Electron carriers such as NADH and NADPH carry reducing power, which is used to build molecules and to drive the production of ATP.

Energy conversion takes place in specific locations. In eukaryotes, the mitochondria carry out respiration, in which the electrons of food molecules are passed to oxygen through a chain of carriers, and the energy released pumps protons across the inner membrane. The resulting gradient drives ATP synthesis. Chloroplasts carry out photosynthesis, in which light energy drives the synthesis of sugars from carbon dioxide and water. Bacteria perform many of the same reactions in the plasma membrane.

Lane and Martin (2010) argued that the energy supply of a cell sets the limits of its complexity, because each protein and each gene requires energy to be maintained. The energetic cost of building and running a cell is therefore central to understanding why cells are the size and shape that they are.

Energy use is measured by consumption of oxygen, by production of carbon dioxide and heat, and by the concentration of ATP and related nucleotides in cell extracts. These measurements show how much energy the cell uses and how it responds to changes in supply.
