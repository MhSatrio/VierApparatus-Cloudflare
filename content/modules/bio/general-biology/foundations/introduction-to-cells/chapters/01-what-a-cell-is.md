A cell is a small, membrane-bounded unit that contains genetic information, a chemically active interior, and the machinery needed to copy itself. These three elements are the minimum that biologists generally require before they call a system a cell. A membrane separates the cell from its surroundings, the genetic material stores the instructions for making new components, and the interior carries out the reactions that keep the cell running.

Hooke (1665) first described the compartments that later became known as cells. His observations were of dead plant tissue, and the living contents of cells were not seen until microscopes improved. The modern definition rests on the observation that all known living systems are built from cells or are viruses, which depend on cells to reproduce.

The smallest cells known are bacteria of a few tenths of a micrometre in diameter, and some have been described that are smaller still. Cells of this size can carry the full set of machinery needed for life, which shows that a cell does not need to be large to be complete. Mycoplasma, a group of bacteria with very small genomes, is often cited to show how few parts a cell can use.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell as a system whose parts depend on one another. The membrane needs proteins to be built, the proteins need the genetic material to be specified, and the genetic material needs enzymes to be copied. Removing one part disrupts the rest, which is why no single component can be called the cell.

The definition has limits. Some structures, such as the red blood cells of mammals, have lost their nucleus and cannot divide, and the boundary between a cell and a virus is not always sharp. Biologists therefore treat the definition as a working rule, and they adjust it when new evidence arrives.
