Multicellular organisms are built from cells that cooperate. In animals, plants and fungi, a single fertilised cell divides repeatedly and its descendants take on different roles. Cells that stay together form tissues, and tissues form organs. The cells of one organism almost always share the same genome, so their differences come from which genes they express.

Specialisation begins during development. Signals from neighbouring cells and from the wider environment cause a cell to switch on a particular set of genes, and that pattern is then inherited by its daughters. A muscle cell and a skin cell have the same DNA, but each expresses the genes it needs for its job and silences the others. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how this differentiation is maintained by regulatory networks that reinforce the chosen state.

Cells communicate through direct contact and through secreted signals. Gap junctions allow small molecules to pass between adjacent cells, and receptors on the surface of one cell bind signals released by another. Hormones and growth factors carry messages over longer distances, and the response depends on the receptors present on the target cell. The integration of these signals is what allows a multicellular organism to behave as a whole.

The evolution of multicellularity has occurred several times, and the Woese et al. (1990) classification of life into domains shows that multicellular forms are found in several lineages. Multicellularity therefore arose from different ancestors through different routes, and the cells that cooperate in each lineage have their own histories.

Cell specialisation is studied by tracing lineages with genetic markers, by measuring gene expression in individual cells, and by observing cell fate during development in living embryos.
