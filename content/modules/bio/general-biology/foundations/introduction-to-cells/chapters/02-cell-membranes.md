The plasma membrane is the boundary of the cell. It is a lipid bilayer, a double layer of phospholipids whose hydrophobic tails face one another and whose hydrophilic heads face the water on either side. The bilayer is impermeable to most charged and polar molecules, and this property allows the cell to keep its interior different from its surroundings. Proteins are embedded in the bilayer and carry out many of the membrane's functions.

The membrane's functions include transport, signalling and adhesion. Transport proteins move specific molecules across the membrane, some passively and others by using energy. Receptor proteins detect signals from outside the cell and pass them inward. Adhesion proteins link the cell to its neighbours or to the extracellular matrix. Each of these functions depends on the arrangement of proteins within the bilayer.

Ball (2008) pointed out that water is central to how membranes work. The hydrophobic effect that organises the bilayer also governs how proteins insert into it, and water near the membrane surface affects the behaviour of the molecules that approach it. The membrane is therefore not a passive wall but an interface whose properties depend on the water it separates.

Passive transport allows small uncharged molecules, such as oxygen and carbon dioxide, to cross the bilayer by simple diffusion. Larger or charged molecules need transport proteins. Channels form pores that allow ions to pass when they open, and carriers bind their substrate and change shape to move it across. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how these mechanisms maintain the concentration differences that cells require.

Membranes are studied by electron microscopy, by fluorescent labelling of proteins and lipids, and by reconstitution, in which purified proteins are inserted into artificial bilayers to measure their activity. Each method reveals a different aspect of how the membrane works.
