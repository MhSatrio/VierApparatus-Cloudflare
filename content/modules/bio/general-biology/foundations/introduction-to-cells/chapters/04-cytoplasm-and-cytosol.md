The cytoplasm is everything inside the plasma membrane, excluding the nucleus in eukaryotes. It contains the cytosol, the fluid phase in which dissolved molecules are found, together with the organelles, filaments, and assemblies that the cell builds. The cytosol makes up most of the volume of many cells, and it is where many of the core reactions of metabolism take place, including glycolysis and the synthesis of many amino acids and nucleotides.

The cytosol is much more concentrated than a dilute solution. Macromolecules fill a large fraction of the available space, often estimated at a substantial proportion of the volume, and the result is a crowded environment. Ball (2008) emphasised that water in such a crowded space behaves differently from bulk water, and this affects how molecules move and react. Crowding can speed some reactions by bringing reactants together and slow others by reducing the space in which molecules can move.

The cytosol is not uniform. Concentrations of ions, metabolites and proteins differ between regions of the cell, and the pH of the cytosol is typically maintained near neutral. Some compartments, such as the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum, are chemically distinct from the cytosol, and the cell uses membranes to separate them.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the cytoplasm is organised. Filaments of the cytoskeleton give it structure and transport, and the movement of organelles and vesicles through it depends on molecular motors. The cytoplasm is therefore a dynamic, organised medium rather than a bag of solution.

Cytosolic properties are measured by fluorescent indicators that report ion concentrations or pH, by microinjection of tracers, and by the diffusion of labelled proteins, which reveals how crowded the interior is.
