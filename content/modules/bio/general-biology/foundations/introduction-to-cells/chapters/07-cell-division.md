Cell division is the process by which one cell produces two. It requires that the genome be copied and that the copies be separated into two daughter cells. Because the two strands of DNA are complementary, each strand can serve as a template for a new partner, so the copying step produces two identical double helices from one (Watson and Crick, 1953).

In eukaryotes, division follows a cycle with distinct phases. In interphase the cell grows and copies its DNA during the S phase. In mitosis the duplicated chromosomes condense, align, separate and are distributed to two nuclei. Cytokinesis then divides the cytoplasm. The cycle is controlled by checkpoints that halt progress when DNA is damaged or when conditions are unfavourable, and by cyclin-dependent kinases that drive each transition.

Bacteria divide in a simpler way. Their single circular chromosome is copied from a fixed origin, and the two copies move toward opposite ends of the cell. A ring of the protein FtsZ assembles at the midpoint and constricts the cell envelope, producing two daughter cells. The process is fast in favourable conditions, and some bacteria divide every twenty minutes or less.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the cell cycle is coordinated with growth, so that a cell does not divide until it has doubled its mass. The link between size and division is a central question in cell biology, and the answer differs between bacteria and eukaryotes.

Cell division is studied by time-lapse microscopy, by flow cytometry, which measures DNA content in individual cells, and by genetic screens that identify the proteins required at each stage.
