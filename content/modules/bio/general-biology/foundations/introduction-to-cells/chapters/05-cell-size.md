Most cells are microscopic. A typical bacterial cell is a few micrometres across, and a typical eukaryotic cell is ten to a hundred micrometres in diameter. The main reason is geometry. A cell takes up nutrients and releases waste across its surface, and it uses those materials throughout its volume. As a sphere grows, its volume increases with the cube of its radius, while its surface area increases only with the square. The surface-area-to-volume ratio therefore falls as the cell gets bigger.

The consequence is that a large cell has less membrane available per unit of cytoplasm to supply its needs. Diffusion is effective over distances of a few micrometres, but it becomes too slow over larger distances. A cell that grows too large cannot deliver oxygen, nutrients and signalling molecules quickly enough to its interior, and its metabolism will be limited by transport rather than by the capacity of its enzymes.

Large cells solve the problem in several ways. Some develop extensive folded membranes, which increase the surface area without increasing the volume. Some are branched or elongated, which keeps every part close to the surface. Some organise the cytoplasm with cytoskeletal tracks and motor proteins, which move materials over distances that diffusion cannot cover. Neurons, which can extend over a metre in a large animal, rely on this kind of active transport.

Lane and Martin (2010) argued that the energy available to each gene constrains the size and complexity of a cell. Their analysis showed that eukaryotic cells have much more energy per gene than prokaryotes, which permits larger genomes and more complex organisation. Cell size and energy supply are therefore closely connected.

Cell size is measured by microscopy and by calibrated image analysis. Investigators relate size to the number of cells in an organism, to the rate of growth and to the mechanisms that determine when a cell divides.
