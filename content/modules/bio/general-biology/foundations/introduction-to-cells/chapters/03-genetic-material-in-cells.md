Every cell stores its hereditary information in nucleic acids. In almost all living organisms this is DNA, a double helix whose two strands carry complementary sequences of bases. Watson and Crick (1953) proposed this structure, and the complementarity of the strands explains how the genetic material can be copied accurately before a cell divides.

The genome is the complete set of DNA in a cell. In bacteria it is usually a single circular chromosome, and in eukaryotes it is divided among several linear chromosomes that are packaged with proteins into chromatin. The amount of DNA varies widely among species, and the number of genes does not simply track the complexity of the organism, but every cell of a given organism usually contains the same genome.

Cells of the same organism are not identical, because they use different parts of the genome. The pattern of gene expression differs between a liver cell and a nerve cell, and the difference is established during development and maintained by regulatory proteins and chemical modifications of the DNA and its packaging. The genome is the same, but the set of active genes is not.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how DNA is replicated before cell division, so that each daughter cell receives a complete copy. Replication begins at defined origins and proceeds in both directions, with enzymes that unwind the helix and copy each strand. Errors are corrected by proofreading and repair systems, which keep the rate of permanent changes low.

Genetic material is studied by extraction, sequencing and analysis of the bases, and by microscopy of chromosomes in dividing cells. These methods show what is stored in the cell and how it changes.
