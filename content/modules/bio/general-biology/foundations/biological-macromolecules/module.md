# Biological Macromolecules

Living cells are built from four families of large molecules. Polysaccharides store and provide energy and form structural materials such as cell walls. Lipids form the membranes that separate cells from their surroundings. Proteins carry out most of the chemical work of the cell, and nucleic acids store and transmit genetic information. Each family is made by joining smaller units, and each unit's chemistry constrains the shapes and functions that the finished molecule can have.

The module treats the families in turn. It begins with carbohydrates and lipids, then moves to amino acids and the levels of protein structure. It describes how proteins fold, how nucleic acids carry information, how polymers are formed and broken by condensation and hydrolysis, and how the three-dimensional structures of macromolecules were first determined. The reference list includes the original papers for several of the key discoveries.

The central idea is that structure predicts function, at least approximately. A protein's fold creates binding sites and catalytic surfaces, a membrane's lipid composition sets its permeability, and a nucleic acid's sequence specifies a message. These relationships are the working foundation of molecular biology, and they are developed further in the Biochemistry and Molecular Biology modules of the curriculum.

Readers should expect some chemical detail. Each chapter explains the chemistry needed to follow the biology, and each finishes with an example that shows why the structure matters.
