The structure of a protein is described at four levels. The primary structure is the sequence of amino acids joined by peptide bonds, which are covalent bonds between the carboxyl group of one residue and the amino group of the next. The sequence is read from the amino terminus to the carboxyl terminus, and it contains the information that determines all higher levels of structure.

The secondary structure consists of local folding of the backbone into regular shapes. The alpha helix is a coil stabilised by hydrogen bonds between the backbone of each residue and the residue four positions further along. The beta sheet consists of strands lying side by side, with hydrogen bonds between them. Pauling and Corey (1951) proposed the pleated sheet from the geometry of the peptide bond, and the idea that the backbone adopts regular, hydrogen-bonded conformations remains central to protein structure.

The tertiary structure is the overall three-dimensional arrangement of one polypeptide chain. It is stabilised by hydrophobic interactions among buried side chains, by hydrogen bonds, by ionic bonds between oppositely charged residues and by disulfide bonds between cysteines. Kendrew and colleagues (1960) determined the tertiary structure of myoglobin, showing that a single chain of about 150 residues folds into a compact shape with a pocket for its haem group.

The quaternary structure describes the arrangement of two or more polypeptide chains into a complex. Hemoglobin, for example, consists of four chains, and their interactions change the affinity of the protein for oxygen. Quaternary assembly allows proteins to act cooperatively and to form structures, such as the capsids of viruses, that no single chain could form alone.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how these levels are organised in practice. The primary structure determines the fold, the fold determines the active site and the active site determines function. Changes at any level can alter activity, which is why mutations in a protein's sequence are often studied by examining their effect on its structure.

Protein structures are determined by X-ray crystallography, nuclear magnetic resonance and electron microscopy, and they are stored in public databases, which allow researchers to compare structures and infer function.
