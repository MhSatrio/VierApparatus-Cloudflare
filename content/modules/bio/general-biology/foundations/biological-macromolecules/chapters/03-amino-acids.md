An amino acid has a central carbon atom, called the alpha carbon, bonded to four groups: an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen atom and a side chain. The side chain is what distinguishes one amino acid from another. Twenty amino acids are encoded by the genetic code and incorporated into proteins in cells, and each has a characteristic side chain whose chemistry determines how the protein behaves.

The side chains are commonly grouped by their properties. Some are nonpolar and hydrophobic, such as the side chains of alanine, valine, leucine and phenylalanine. Others are polar but uncharged, such as serine, threonine and asparagine. Some carry a positive charge at physiological pH, including lysine and arginine, and others a negative charge, including aspartate and glutamate. Cysteine carries a thiol group that can form disulfide bonds, and proline has a ring that restricts the angles of the polypeptide backbone.

The alpha carbon is a stereocentre in all amino acids except glycine, and in proteins almost all amino acids have the L configuration. This uniformity is a striking feature of biology, since the chemistry allows both forms. Pauling and Corey (1951) built their model of the pleated sheet from the geometry of the peptide backbone, and their analysis depended on the fixed planar geometry of the amide bond and the limited range of angles that the backbone can adopt.

The properties of the side chains determine how a protein folds. Hydrophobic residues cluster in the interior of a folded protein, polar and charged residues favour the surface, and proline and glycine occur at positions where the chain must turn. A protein's sequence is therefore a set of instructions for its shape, and the side chains are the words in which those instructions are written.

Amino acids are identified by chromatography, which separates them by their chemical properties, and by mass spectrometry, which determines their mass and fragments. Their concentrations in cells are measured by these same methods, which reveal how the pools of amino acids change in response to nutrition and stress.
