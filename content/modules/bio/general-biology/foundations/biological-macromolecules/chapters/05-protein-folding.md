A newly synthesised polypeptide must fold into a specific three-dimensional shape to function. Anfinsen (1973) reviewed the work that established the principle that the native structure of a protein is determined by its amino acid sequence. His experiments showed that a denatured protein can refold spontaneously when the denaturing conditions are removed, and that it adopts the same structure as before. The sequence therefore contains enough information to specify the fold, under the conditions in which the protein is normally found.

The principle is sometimes called the thermodynamic hypothesis. The native state is the conformation with the lowest free energy under physiological conditions, and the folding process moves the chain toward that state. Hydrophobic interactions drive most of the process, since burying hydrophobic residues reduces the number of ordered water molecules, as discussed in the chapter on hydrophobic molecules. Hydrogen bonds, electrostatic interactions and disulfide bonds refine the structure.

Folding is not a random search through all possible conformations. Levinthal calculated that a chain trying every conformation at random would take longer than the age of the universe to fold, yet real proteins fold in milliseconds to seconds. Folding therefore proceeds through partly folded intermediates, which guide the chain toward the native state. The intermediates have been studied by spectroscopy and by rapid mixing experiments.

Some cellular proteins need help. Molecular chaperones bind to unfolded or partly folded chains, prevent them from aggregating and release them in a form that can fold. Failures in folding are associated with disease. Misfolded proteins can form aggregates, and aggregates of certain proteins are found in the brains of people with Alzheimer disease and Parkinson disease.

Folding is studied by measuring the change in a spectroscopic signal as a denaturant is added or removed, by hydrogen exchange, which reveals which parts of the chain are protected, and by computational simulation. The field continues to develop, since predicting the fold from the sequence remains an active research problem.
