Nucleic acids are polymers of nucleotides. Each nucleotide consists of a sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogenous base. In DNA the sugar is deoxyribose and the bases are adenine, guanine, cytosine and thymine. In RNA the sugar is ribose and thymine is replaced by uracil. Nucleotides are linked by phosphodiester bonds between the phosphate of one nucleotide and the sugar of the next, giving each strand a direction, conventionally written from the five-prime end to the three-prime end.

Watson and Crick (1953) proposed that DNA is a double helix in which two strands run in opposite directions and are held together by hydrogen bonds between their bases. Adenine pairs with thymine through two hydrogen bonds, and guanine pairs with cytosine through three. The pairing rule means that the sequence of one strand determines the sequence of the other, and that each strand can serve as a template for copying the whole molecule.

The information in DNA is carried in the order of its bases, not in its chemical composition. Four bases can form many sequences, and the sequence of a gene specifies the order of amino acids in a protein through the genetic code. RNA carries this information from DNA to the machinery that builds proteins, and some RNAs also have structural and catalytic roles.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the double helix allows replication, transcription and repair, since each process requires the strands to separate and then recombine with complementary partners. The stability of the helix depends on the hydrogen bonds between bases and on the stacking of the bases in the interior of the molecule.

Nucleic acid sequences are determined by sequencing methods, which read the order of bases, and their structures are studied by X-ray diffraction, nuclear magnetic resonance and cryo-electron microscopy. The double helix was first inferred from X-ray diffraction data, and modern methods extend the approach to the full range of nucleic acid structures found in cells.
