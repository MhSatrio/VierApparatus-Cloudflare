A polymer is a large molecule made of many repeating units called monomers. Most biological polymers are formed by condensation reactions, in which two monomers join with the loss of a water molecule. A hydroxyl group from one monomer and a hydrogen from another combine to form water, and the two monomers become linked by a new covalent bond. The reaction is repeated to lengthen the chain, so a protein or a polysaccharide grows one residue at a time.

Condensation is endergonic, so it cannot occur spontaneously in the aqueous environment of the cell. The energy comes from coupling to the hydrolysis of ATP, as described in the chapter on chemical reactions and energy. In a ribosome, for example, each peptide bond is formed by a reaction that is powered by the energy stored in the aminoacyl-tRNA. The coupling is a general solution to the problem of building polymers in water.

Hydrolysis is the reverse reaction, in which water is added across a bond to break it. Digestion is largely hydrolysis: enzymes such as amylases break starch into sugars, proteases break proteins into peptides and amino acids, and nucleases break nucleic acids into nucleotides. The reaction is exergonic and proceeds spontaneously in principle, but enzymes are needed to make it proceed at a useful rate.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how cells balance synthesis and breakdown. A polymer that is continuously made and degraded can respond quickly to changes in demand, and the rate of turnover is itself a regulated property. Some macromolecules, such as the proteins of the cytoplasm, have half-lives of hours, while others persist for the life of the cell.

The chemistry of polymerisation is studied by tracking the incorporation of labelled monomers into polymers, and by mass spectrometry, which detects the mass changes produced by each reaction. These methods reveal the rate at which polymers are built and broken down in living cells.
