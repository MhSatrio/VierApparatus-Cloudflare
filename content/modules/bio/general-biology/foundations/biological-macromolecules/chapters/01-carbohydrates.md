Carbohydrates are molecules built from carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, with a formula close to a multiple of CH2O. The simplest are monosaccharides, such as glucose, which has six carbon atoms and a ring structure formed by one of its hydroxyl groups reacting with an aldehyde or ketone group. Glucose is the central fuel of most cells. Two monosaccharides joined by a glycosidic bond form a disaccharide, such as sucrose, which is glucose linked to fructose.

Polysaccharides are long chains of monosaccharides. Their properties depend on the sugar units, the bonds between them and the degree of branching. Starch, the storage polysaccharide of plants, is a mixture of amylose, an unbranched chain, and amylopectin, which is branched. Glycogen, the storage form in animals, is more highly branched than starch, which allows enzymes to release glucose from many ends at once. Cellulose, the structural polysaccharide of plant cell walls, is an unbranched chain of glucose units joined in a different orientation, and this difference makes it both strong and insoluble.

The difference between starch and cellulose is a difference of geometry, not of composition. Both are chains of glucose, but the glycosidic bonds in cellulose lock the chains into straight fibres that pack together through hydrogen bonds. Humans cannot digest cellulose because they lack the enzymes that cleave its bonds, while many bacteria and fungi can. Lewis (1916) gave the basic account of how atoms are joined in molecules, and the detailed structures of polysaccharides depend on this principle.

Carbohydrates also serve as recognition molecules. Sugar chains attached to proteins and lipids on the cell surface determine how cells recognise one another and how pathogens bind to them. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how these glycans are synthesised in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus, and how their structure is modified during their journey to the surface.

Carbohydrates are analysed by chromatography, mass spectrometry and nuclear magnetic resonance, which reveal the identity of each sugar unit, the sequence of the chain and the position of its linkages.
