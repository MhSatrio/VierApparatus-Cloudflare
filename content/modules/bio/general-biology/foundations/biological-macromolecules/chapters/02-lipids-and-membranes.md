Lipids are a diverse family of molecules defined by their solubility rather than by a single structure. They are largely hydrophobic, so they dissolve in organic solvents and not in water. Fatty acids are long hydrocarbon chains with a carboxyl group at one end. Triglycerides consist of three fatty acids attached to a glycerol backbone and serve as the main energy store in animals. Steroids, such as cholesterol, have a fused ring structure, and they regulate the fluidity of membranes and act as signalling molecules.

Phospholipids are the lipids that matter most for membranes. A phospholipid has a glycerol backbone, two fatty acid tails and a phosphate group carrying a polar head. The molecule is amphipathic, meaning that it has one hydrophilic region and one hydrophobic region. In water, the hydrophobic tails are pushed together by the hydrophobic effect that Ball (2008) described, and the hydrophilic heads face the surrounding water.

The result is a bilayer, a double sheet of phospholipids with their tails facing inward and their heads facing outward on both surfaces. The bilayer is stable, flexible and self-sealing, so it can curve to form closed compartments. Its interior is a hydrophobic core that blocks the passage of most ions and polar molecules, which is why membranes can maintain different concentrations on either side.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the bilayer as a fluid structure in which lipids and proteins move laterally. Fluidity depends on the length and saturation of the fatty acid tails, which affects how tightly the lipids pack together. Cholesterol moderates fluidity, stiffening membranes at high temperature and preventing them from freezing too tightly at low temperature.

Membranes are studied by electron microscopy, by fluorescence techniques that track the movement of labelled lipids, and by artificial bilayers, which allow the permeability and mechanical properties of membranes to be measured under controlled conditions.
