The structure of a protein is determined from experimental data, and the first complete protein structure was reported by Kendrew and colleagues (1960). They determined the three-dimensional arrangement of the atoms in myoglobin using X-ray diffraction of crystals. Their achievement required years of work, and it showed that a protein's structure could be resolved at atomic detail, which opened the field of structural biology.

X-ray crystallography works by growing a crystal of the protein, exposing it to an X-ray beam and recording the pattern of diffracted spots. The positions and intensities of the spots are related to the electron density in the crystal through a mathematical transform. The phase information that the transform requires is the difficult part, and early workers solved it by introducing heavy atoms into the crystal, which scatter X-rays strongly and provide a reference. Kendrew's work used this approach.

Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy determines structure in solution. It measures the distances between pairs of hydrogen atoms that are close in space, and these distance restraints are combined into a model of the protein. NMR is well suited to smaller proteins and to flexible regions that do not crystallise, and it can reveal the dynamics of a structure in addition to its average shape.

Electron microscopy has become an important method for large complexes. Cryo-electron microscopy freezes samples in a thin layer of ice and images many copies of the complex. Computer processing averages the images to reconstruct the three-dimensional structure, and the method now achieves near-atomic resolution for many assemblies that cannot be crystallised.

The structures determined by these methods are used to understand function. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the structure follows from the sequence, and modern structure determination provides the data against which folding theories and prediction methods are tested. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how structures are used to design drugs and to interpret the effects of mutations.

Each method has limits. Crystallography requires a crystal, which may be impossible to obtain, NMR is limited by molecular size, and cryo-electron microscopy requires many copies of the particle. Biologists choose the method according to the protein and the question.
