The data of bioinformatics fall into a small number of types, and each type has its own structure and its own sources of error. The most basic are nucleotide sequences, the strings of A, C, G and T that make up DNA, and protein sequences, the strings of amino acids. Sequences are generated by sequencing machines, and the output contains errors that depend on the technology, so the reliability of a base call must be judged before the sequence is used. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the chemistry of the bases and the amino acids that these strings encode.

Genome annotations are the labels attached to a sequence. They state where the genes, exons and regulatory regions lie, and they are produced by a combination of computational prediction and experimental evidence. Annotations are revised as new evidence arrives, so an annotation is a best current estimate, not a fixed fact.

Expression data measure how much of each transcript or protein is present, usually by counting sequencing reads or by measuring signal intensity. Counts depend on the library preparation and the sequencing depth, and comparisons between samples require normalisation.

Protein structures are described by the coordinates of their atoms, and they are archived in the Protein Data Bank. Berman (2000) described the archive that collects these structures, and each structure carries the resolution and quality indicators that determine how far its details can be trusted. Interaction networks record which molecules bind or act on one another, and they are often incomplete and biased toward the interactions that have been studied most.

Images, from microscopes and from imaging methods, form another type. They require their own preprocessing, and their quantitative analysis must account for the optics and the noise of the instrument.

Sandve et al. (2013) recommended recording the source, version and processing of each data type, since the same type of data can change meaning with the way it was prepared.
