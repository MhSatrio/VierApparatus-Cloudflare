Most microorganisms cannot be grown in the laboratory, so their genomes are unknown from culture. Metagenomics sidesteps this by sequencing all the DNA of a sample, from soil, water, a gut or a surface, and then working out which organisms are present and what they can do. Quince et al. (2017) describe the path from sampling to analysis for this kind of study, and the topic follows the same path: the design of the sequencing, the profiling of who is present, the reconstruction of genomes from the mixture and the description of what the community does.

The chapters follow the stages of a study. The first sets out the principles of shotgun sequencing applied to a mixed sample. The taxonomic profiling chapter shows how marker genes identify the organisms present, following the approach of Segata et al. (2012). Later chapters cover the assembly of genomes from the mixture, the functional profile of the community and the biases that make metagenomic results hard to compare between studies.

A metagenome is a sum of many genomes, most of which are incomplete in any single sample. The analyst must keep in mind that every result is an estimate of a mixture, and that a claim about a single organism inside the mixture carries the uncertainty of separating it from the rest.
