A figure in ggplot2 is built from layers, each of which states a data source, a mapping of variables to visual properties and a geometric shape. The mapping assigns a column to the horizontal position, another to the colour and a third to the shape of the points, and the layers are added one at a time. This grammar makes it possible to build complex figures from simple parts and to change one part without rebuilding the rest. Wickham et al. (2019) describe the tidyverse, of which ggplot2 is a central member.

The choice of plot type depends on the question. A bar of mean expression hides the spread of the data, and two groups with the same mean may differ greatly in their distributions. A box plot, a violin plot or a plot of the individual points shows the spread and the number of observations, and it is the honest choice when the sample sizes are small. The figure should show the data, not only a summary of it.

Uncertainty must be shown. An error bar, a confidence interval or a band indicates how precisely an estimate is known, and a figure without it invites the reader to treat a small difference as large. The scale of the axes matters as well: a truncated axis can make a small difference look dramatic, and a logarithmic scale can make a large one look small, so the choice should be stated.

Ioannidis (2005) described how selective reporting of results distorts the scientific record, and a figure can distort it in the same way when only the striking comparisons are shown. Platt (1964) recommended that figures show the comparison that would discriminate between alternative explanations, so that the reader can see what the data support.

A figure should be generated by the script that produces the analysis, so that it can be regenerated when the data or the code change.
