Proteomics is applied wherever the state of the cell is best described by its proteins. In basic research it measures how a cell's protein content changes in response to a stimulus, and it identifies the complexes and modifications that carry a signal. In medicine it is used to search for biomarkers in blood and tissue and to characterise disease states that transcripts do not reveal. Aebersold and Mann (2016) describe the breadth of these uses as the reason mass spectrometry became central to the study of the proteome.

Each application asks a different question, and the evidence it needs differs. A response to a stimulus asks which proteins change and how much, and the statistics of the previous chapter answer that. A biomarker asks whether a measurement separates two clinical groups, and that claim requires validation on samples that were not used to find the marker, in the same way that a diagnostic signature requires an independent test. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result found in one set of data and chosen for its strength can fail on new data, and biomarker discovery is especially exposed to this failure.

Protein complexes and modifications connect proteomics to the mechanisms of the cell. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling pathways in which modified proteins act, and the identification of a modification is the first step in showing that it regulates a function. The function itself requires experiments beyond the mass spectrum.

The quantities of a proteomic measurement are relative and depend on the method. Cox and Mann (2008) describe the quantification pipeline on which many of these measurements are made, and a clinical claim that rests on an absolute level of a protein needs a method calibrated for absolute quantities. A relative change between two conditions does not establish a level.

The applications share a rule: a proteomic measurement should be used for the question it can answer, and the claim should be tested against the evidence that question requires.
