Transcriptomics is applied wherever the state of a cell or tissue matters. In basic research it reveals the response of cells to a signal, the programme of genes that drives development and the differences between cell types. In medicine it is used to classify tumours and to search for markers of disease, and in ecology and agriculture it describes the responses of organisms to their environment. Wang et al. (2009) described the breadth of these uses as one of the reasons the method spread so quickly.

Each application asks a different question. A response to a stimulus asks which genes change and how quickly. A developmental programme asks which genes are active at which stage, and a classification asks whether the profile separates the groups of interest. The evidence each needs differs, and the same profile may answer one question well and another poorly.

Classification by expression profile carries a particular risk. A profile that separates two groups in one study may fail to separate them in another, because the separation may reflect the batch, the population or the processing rather than the biology. Mortazavi et al. (2008) showed how expression can be mapped across a mammalian transcriptome, and the mapping gives the basis for profiles, but a profile used for diagnosis must be validated on independent samples. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that is found in one set of data and selected for its strength can fail on new data, and a diagnostic claim is open to exactly this failure.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how gene expression is regulated in the cell, and the regulation that acts after transcription limits how far a profile can be read as a measure of function. A transcriptome profile is a strong source of hypotheses about regulation, and a weaker source of conclusions about what the cell does.

The applications therefore share a rule: a transcriptome profile should be used for the question it can answer, and the claim should be tested against the evidence the question requires.
