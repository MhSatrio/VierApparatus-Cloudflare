A transcript is an intermediate between a gene and its activity. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the path from transcription through translation to a functioning protein, and each stage is regulated. A transcript that is abundant may be translated poorly, and a protein may be stable for a long time after its transcript has disappeared. A change in transcript level therefore predicts a change in protein level only where the later stages are unchanged, and this is often not the case.

The gap between transcript and protein is measured by proteomics. A protein's abundance can be measured from the same sample, and the agreement between the two levels shows how far the transcript is a guide to the protein. Where the two disagree, the regulation acts after transcription, and a report that claims a change in function from the transcript alone is making an assumption that the data do not support. Wang et al. (2009) described the transcriptome as a measure of expression, and expression in this sense is a level of RNA.

The connection to function needs further evidence. A change in a transcript that encodes an enzyme may or may not change the activity of the enzyme, and the activity must be measured directly for the claim to be made. Platt (1964) recommended that a claim be tested against its alternatives, and a function inferred from a transcript should be compared with the alternative that the protein level or the activity did not change.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that is selected from many measurements can overstate its meaning, and a transcriptome study that reports many changes will include some that are not biologically meaningful. The genes of interest should be chosen by a rule fixed in advance, and the functional claim should be restricted to the genes that pass both the statistical threshold and the test at the protein or activity level.

A transcriptome study therefore reports transcript changes as transcript changes. The claim about function is a separate claim, with its own evidence.
