The transcriptome is the set of RNA molecules that a cell is making at a given time. It records which genes are active, how strongly, and in which forms, since a single gene can produce several transcripts by alternative splicing. Wang et al. (2009) describe how RNA sequencing became the method for measuring the transcriptome, replacing the hybridisation arrays of earlier decades, and Mortazavi et al. (2008) showed how the reads of such a sequencing run can be used to map and quantify the transcripts of a mammalian genome.

The topic covers the stages of a transcriptome study. It begins with the principle of RNA sequencing and the design of experiments, including the replicates and the conditions that a study must compare. The later chapters address the quantification of transcript abundance, the detection of alternative splicing, and the interpretation of transcript levels as evidence of biology. The analysis of differential expression, which compares the levels between conditions, is the subject of the RNA-seq analysis topic that follows.

A transcript level is a measurement of RNA, not of the function of the cell. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the steps between a transcript and a protein and the regulation that acts at each, so the relation between transcript level and protein activity is indirect, and the topic keeps that distinction in view.
