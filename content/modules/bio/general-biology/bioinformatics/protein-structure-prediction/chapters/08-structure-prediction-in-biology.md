Predicted structures have widened what biology can see. Jumper et al. (2021) describe a method whose predictions cover much of the proteome of a model organism, and the coverage extends structural knowledge to proteins that were never determined experimentally. The applications follow from that breadth: a structural view of a whole proteome, the study of families whose members were previously unknown in structure and the generation of hypotheses about many proteins at once.

The structural view of a proteome is a catalogue. It assigns a predicted shape to each protein and groups the proteins by fold, which can reveal families that sequence search missed. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the families of proteins expanded through duplication and divergence, and a structural catalogue shows where the divergence has preserved the fold while changing the sequence. The catalogue is a set of hypotheses about relationships, and its claims carry the confidence of each model.

Predicted structures also suggest function. A fold may resemble that of a characterised enzyme, and the resemblance points to a hypothesis about the protein's activity. The hypothesis must be tested, since a fold is compatible with several functions, and the claim should be stated as a candidate rather than a finding. Platt (1964) recommended that such hypotheses be framed so that a test can exclude the alternatives.

The scale of predicted structure creates a risk. When thousands of structures are generated and the most striking are reported, the reported set is selected, and the selection inflates the apparent value of the predictions. Ioannidis (2005) described how selection of this kind makes findings less reliable than they appear, and a study of many predictions should report all the predictions examined, not only those that look promising.

The applications share a rule. A predicted structure should be used for the question its confidence can answer, and the selection of structures for claims should be made by a rule fixed in advance.
