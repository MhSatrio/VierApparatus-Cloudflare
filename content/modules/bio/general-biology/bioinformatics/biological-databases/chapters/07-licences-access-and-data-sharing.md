Access to a biological database is not the same as the right to reuse its data. A database may be freely readable while its records carry conditions on how they may be copied, combined or redistributed, and the conditions are stated in the licence that the resource publishes. An analysis that redistributes a database's contents in a new product must respect the licence, and the researcher should read it before building on the data.

The sequence archives, which Benson et al. (2012) describe, make their records available without restriction, so that their contents can be used widely, and the same openness is the basis of the FAIR principles described by Wilkinson et al. (2016). Those principles ask that data be accessible under clear conditions and that their reuse be possible, so that a result can be checked and built upon by others.

Human genomic data require additional care. Such data can identify an individual, and their release is governed by consent agreements, by law and by the policies of the repositories that hold them. Access to controlled human data is usually granted through an application that states the purpose of the study and the obligations of the user, and an analysis that uses such data must comply with the terms under which they were obtained.

Deposition is the obligation that comes with the benefit of public data. A researcher who publishes a result that depends on new sequences or structures should deposit the data in a public archive with an accession number, so that others can check the result. Peng (2011) argued that the availability of the data and code is the condition under which a computational finding can be verified.

The practical steps are to record the licence and access conditions of each data source, to respect them in the analysis and in any redistribution and to deposit the data behind a published result where the terms allow.
