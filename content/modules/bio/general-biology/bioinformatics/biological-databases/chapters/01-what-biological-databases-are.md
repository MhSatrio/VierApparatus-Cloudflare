A biological database is an organised collection of biological data that can be searched and retrieved by computer. Databases differ in what they hold and in how their contents are produced. A primary archive holds the data as they were submitted, in the form given by the researcher who generated them. A secondary resource takes data from one or more primary archives, checks them, integrates them with other information and annotates them, and it adds value by making the information easier to use.

GenBank is a primary archive for nucleotide sequences. Benson et al. (2012) describe how the records are submitted by researchers and exchanged between the international partners that maintain the archive, and the records carry the information that the submitter provided. The archive does not in general verify the biological claims of a submission, so the quality of a record depends on the care of the person who submitted it.

Secondary resources add curation and integration. A resource may combine the sequence of a protein with its function, its domains and its links to other databases, and the resulting record is more useful than the raw submission. The curation is a human process in some resources and an automatic one in others, and the difference matters for the reliability of the annotation.

Databases are the shared memory of biology. A result obtained in one laboratory can be compared with results from others only if the data are deposited in a common archive with agreed identifiers, and the value of a database grows with the number of records it contains and the consistency of their annotation. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular biology that these records document, and the archives make that documentation available to everyone.

Sandve et al. (2013) recommended that the sources of the data in an analysis be recorded, and a database name, record identifier and version are the minimum that such a record needs.
