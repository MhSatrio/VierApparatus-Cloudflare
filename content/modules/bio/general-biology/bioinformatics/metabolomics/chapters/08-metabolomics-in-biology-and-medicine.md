Metabolomics is applied where the chemistry of an organism or a sample is the object of study. In nutrition and medicine it describes how the metabolite profile of the body responds to diet, exercise and disease. In microbiology it characterises the chemistry of communities and their products, and in biochemistry it suggests the function of enzymes whose substrates were not known. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the metabolic pathways that these applications probe, and the profile is a window onto them.

Each application asks a different question. A response to diet asks which metabolites change and by how much, and the statistics of the earlier chapters answer that. A biomarker asks whether a profile separates clinical groups, and such a claim requires validation on independent samples. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that is found in one dataset and chosen for its strength can fail when tested again, and metabolite biomarkers are exposed to this failure.

Enzyme function is a case where metabolomics generates hypotheses. An enzyme whose gene is disrupted may cause a metabolite to accumulate, and the accumulated metabolite suggests the substrate of the enzyme. Platt (1964) recommended a test that excludes the alternatives, and a suggested substrate should be confirmed by measurement with the purified enzyme rather than inferred from the profile alone.

The limits are the same across applications. A metabolite profile records levels, not fluxes, and the identifications carry confidence levels that must be kept in view. A claim about a clinical or biological outcome should be restricted to the identifications that are secure and to the questions that the level data can answer.

The applications therefore share a rule: a metabolomic measurement should be used for the question it can answer, and the claim should be tested against the evidence that question requires.
