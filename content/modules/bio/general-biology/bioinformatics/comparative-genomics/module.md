Comparative genomics asks what differs between genomes and what they share. The comparison identifies the sequence that is conserved between species, the rearrangements that have moved sequence to new positions, the gene families that have expanded or contracted, and the genes that are unique to one lineage. These differences are the raw material for asking how organisms diverged, and they are the basis of many functional hypotheses about a gene's role.

The chapters begin with the alignment of whole genomes, which Delcher et al. (1999) introduced as a fast way to find the long matches between two sequences, and the synteny that the matches reveal. They then cover gene families, the detection of selection in protein-coding genes and the controls that prevent an apparent difference from being an artefact of how the genomes were assembled or annotated.

A comparison is only as reliable as the genomes it compares. Two genomes assembled and annotated with different methods can differ for reasons that have nothing to do with biology, and a careful analysis checks for this before drawing a conclusion. The emphasis of the topic is therefore on the controls as much as on the comparisons.
