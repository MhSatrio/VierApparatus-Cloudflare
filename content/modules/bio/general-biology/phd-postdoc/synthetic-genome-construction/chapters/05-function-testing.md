A synthetic genome is tested by asking whether the host it produces functions as well as the native one. The tests must cover the essential functions of the genome, and growth in a range of conditions is a demanding first test, since a host that cannot grow as well as its reference has a defect that matters. The comparison must be made under matched conditions, with the same medium, temperature and handling, so that differences can be attributed to the genome.

Each difference must be measured with an estimate and an interval. A small reduction in growth rate may be real but close to the variation between cultures, and the interval shows whether it can be distinguished from that variation. Cumming (2014) argues that the difference between the synthetic and native hosts should be reported with its interval, rather than as a yes or no judgement of equivalence.

Attribution of a difference to a specific module is achieved by replacing modules one at a time and measuring the consequence of each replacement. A module whose replacement restores the native phenotype is implicated in the difference. Ioannidis (2005) described how the attribution of an effect to one cause when several are present can mislead, and the design must test for interactions among modules.

Transcriptomic and proteomic profiling identify changes in gene expression that accompany a difference. These profiles are large and contain many changes that are unrelated to the phenotype, and their interpretation requires the same correction for multiple comparisons as any other screen. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the full set of phenotypes, including those that match the native host, should be reported, so that the reader can see the whole picture and not only the defects.
