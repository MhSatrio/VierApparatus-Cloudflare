Introducing a synthetic chromosome into a host is among the most demanding steps of building a synthetic genome, because the construct is large and fragile and the host must accept it. The transfer method must deliver the construct intact, and the host must retain it through division. Cong et al. (2013) described the genome engineering that makes changes to a host possible, and the transfer of an entire chromosome extends the same principles to a much larger scale.

A selection ensures that the synthetic chromosome is retained in the host, since a construct that is lost during division would leave the host with its original genome. The selection should be stable over many generations, and its loss should be monitored. The retention must be shown, not assumed, because a host that has lost the construct may still grow and appear healthy.

The native genome must then be replaced, in one step or in stages. Replacement in stages allows the function of each step to be tested, but it takes longer and creates more intermediate states. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the sequence of steps and their outcomes should be reported, so that the path to the final host can be followed.

Verification confirms that the host carries the intended synthetic chromosome and not a version that has been rearranged, truncated or contaminated. Sequencing of the host genome shows the presence of each module and the absence of the native sequences that were to be replaced. Peng (2011) argued that the reproducibility of a result depends on the checks that confirm its materials, and this verification is the basis of any claim about the host.

The number of attempts, the successes and the failures should be recorded with their reasons. The rate of success is an estimate that depends on the number of attempts, and it should be reported with its interval. Failures are informative about the constraints of the design and should not be omitted from the report.
