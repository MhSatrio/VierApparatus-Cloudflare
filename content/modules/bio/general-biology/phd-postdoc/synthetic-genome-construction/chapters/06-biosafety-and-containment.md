An organism with a synthetic genome is a new kind of object, and its hazard must be assessed before work with it begins. The assessment considers the danger the organism could pose to people, animals and the environment, the likelihood of its escape or spread and the consequences if it did. The containment measures chosen must be proportionate to this risk, and institutional biosafety committees review the plan before the work proceeds.

Genetic safeguards, such as auxotrophies that make the organism dependent on a supplied nutrient, or kill switches that trigger death under certain conditions, are designed to limit survival outside the laboratory. Their reliability must be tested, since a safeguard that fails at a measurable rate may not be adequate for the risk. Ioannidis (2005) described how a claim of reliability drawn from a small number of tests can greatly overstate the performance of a system, and the number of tests must be sufficient to estimate the failure rate.

Cumming (2014) argues that the failure rate of a safeguard should be reported with its interval, since a safeguard tested in a few million cells may still fail in a rare cell that the tests did not reach. The failure rate that is acceptable depends on the hazard, and the justification for the safeguard must state the rate it achieves and the rate the risk requires.

Horizontal gene transfer, in which genetic material passes to other organisms, is a further risk. The synthetic sequences may be transferred to other species, and the consequences of such transfer must be assessed. Peng (2011) argued that the methods and the materials of such work must be available to those who review it, and the containment measures should be documented so that they can be checked.

Dual-use concerns arise when methods developed for beneficial purposes could be misused. The investigators should consider whether the methods could be used to cause harm and whether the publication should omit details that would give such uplift. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the openness of science is a value, and it must be balanced against these concerns, with the decision made by people with relevant expertise and reported openly.
