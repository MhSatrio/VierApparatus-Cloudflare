A report of a synthetic genome must allow a reader to reconstruct the construct from its description and to evaluate its claims. The complete sequence and the design file should be deposited with identifiers, since the sequence defines the object and the design file records why each choice was made. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that a result that cannot be reconstructed cannot be tested, and the deposition of these files is the minimum for a synthetic genome.

The design rules and each departure from the native genome should be described in the report. A reader who wishes to understand the construct needs to know which sequences were removed, which were recoded and why, and the consequences that were tested. Peng (2011) argued that the steps by which a result was obtained are part of the result, and the design rules are the steps by which the construct was obtained.

The assembly must be reported with the screening and verification at each level, including the number of clones examined and the failures encountered. The residual error rate should be stated with its interval, and the regions that could not be verified must be identified. Cumming (2014) argues that uncertainty should be reported wherever it exists, and a claim that the genome is error-free must be supported by a verification that could have detected errors.

The functional comparison with the native reference should be reported with estimates and intervals, and the biosafety assessment and the safeguards in place should be described. The report should close with an honest statement of what the construct demonstrates and what it does not. Ioannidis (2005) described how claims that outrun their evidence mislead the field, and a synthetic genome is a strong result only when its limits are stated as clearly as its achievements.
