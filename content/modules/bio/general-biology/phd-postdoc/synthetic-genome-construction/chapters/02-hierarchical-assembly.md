Long DNA is too large to synthesise in one piece, so it is assembled from shorter fragments that are joined in a defined hierarchy. Oligonucleotides are synthesised and combined into short double-stranded fragments, which are then joined into modules, and the modules into the full construct. Each level of the hierarchy is a separate assembly step, and an error at any step propagates to every construct that depends on it.

Orthogonal junctions and selectable markers ensure that each step produces the intended product. A junction that can join only to its designed partner prevents the wrong fragments from combining, and a marker that is present only in the correct product allows the correct clones to be selected. Ioannidis (2005) described how a method that appears to work may produce mixed products that go unnoticed, and the orthogonality of the junctions guards against this.

Intermediate clones are screened by sequencing and by a diagnostic digest that produces a predicted pattern of fragments. The screen should confirm that the clone has the intended identity before the next step begins, and clones that fail should be discarded rather than carried forward. Cumming (2014) argues that the number of clones screened should be reported, since the probability of finding a correct clone depends on it, and the uncertainty of that probability is relevant to planning.

Each intermediate must be tracked with its identity and its verification status, so that the origin of every part of the final construct can be traced. A failed step is repaired by re-assembly from verified parts rather than by reusing a partially correct intermediate, and this discipline protects the construct from hidden errors. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the record of each step should be kept and reported, so that the construction can be repeated and checked.
