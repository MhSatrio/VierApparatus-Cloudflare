Every whole-cell model has gaps, and the honest account of a model is the account of what it cannot yet say. Some gaps are processes that are known to occur but for which no parameter has been measured, and the model must either omit them or assume a value. Others are genes whose products are unannotated, so that their roles are absent from the model. Karr et al. (2012) described the processes that their model could not represent, and an explicit list of such gaps is part of any responsible report.

Regulatory interactions are sometimes inferred from expression patterns but not confirmed, and a model built on an inferred regulation inherits its uncertainty. The conditions that the model does not represent, such as stress, nutrient shifts or the presence of other organisms, further limit its scope. Ioannidis (2005) described how a model's predictions can look complete while resting on unexamined assumptions, and the list of inferred interactions guards against this.

Some predictions are impossible without the missing knowledge. A model that lacks a process cannot predict the phenotype that depends on it, and the gap determines the boundary of the model's competence. Cumming (2014) argues that the uncertainty of the predictions should be stated as widened by each gap, since a prediction that depends on an assumption is less certain than one based on measurement.

Gap analysis ranks the missing knowledge by its consequence for the predictions of interest, and this ranking directs experimental work toward the most consequential gaps. A gap that affects no prediction of interest can wait, while one that determines a central phenotype is a priority. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the gaps should be reported openly, so that the community can see what the model does not yet know and contribute to filling it.
