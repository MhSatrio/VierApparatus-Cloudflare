An independent research question must satisfy two conditions. It must be distinct enough from the mentor's programme to be the researcher's own, and it must be connected enough to the researcher's expertise to be feasible. Finding a question that meets both conditions requires a map of the field that shows where the evidence is thin, where results conflict and where the researcher's methods can contribute. Ioannidis (2005) described how many research programmes pursue questions chosen for fashion rather than for the gap they fill, and the map guards against this.

The distinction between a question that extends the mentor's work and one that is genuinely independent is a matter of its foundation. An extension builds directly on the mentor's results, whereas an independent question rests on the researcher's own reading of the evidence and their own methods. Both can be valuable, but the independent question is the one that establishes the researcher's identity. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the independent researcher should be able to justify the question on its evidence rather than on the authority of the mentor.

The significance of the question should be assessed against the existing evidence, not merely by its novelty. A question that is novel but unimportant contributes little, while one that resolves a conflict or fills an important gap is significant even if it is not new. Cumming (2014) argues that the significance is best stated through the effect the answer would change and the precision with which it can be estimated.

The question should be stated as an aim with an expected effect and the precision the study will achieve, since a review panel must be able to judge whether the programme can answer it. Ioannidis (2005) described how a question that cannot be answered decisively at the planned scale wastes resources, and the statement of expected precision protects against that outcome.

Feasibility is tested with preliminary work that the researcher can conduct before the main programme begins. A pilot study shows whether the methods work and whether the effect is of the expected size, and it strengthens the case for the question. Peng (2011) argued that the preliminary work should be recorded so that it can be reproduced, and the question is then stated in terms that a funder and a panel can assess.
