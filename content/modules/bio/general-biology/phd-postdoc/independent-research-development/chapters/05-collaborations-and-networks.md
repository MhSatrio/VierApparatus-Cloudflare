Collaboration extends an independent programme by bringing methods, samples and expertise that the investigator does not have, and networks provide the independent replication on which the credibility of findings depends. Collaborators should be chosen for the complementary methods they offer, and the investigator should be wary of collaborations that offer only a service. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that collaboration strengthens the reproducibility of science, since findings tested by independent groups are more secure.

Formal agreements define the responsibilities of each partner, the sharing of data and the terms of authorship. Such agreements should be made before the work begins, and they should protect the independence of the investigator's question and the right to publish. Ioannidis (2005) described how the uncertain allocation of credit and data can produce disputes and distort the record, and the agreement prevents this.

Collaboration involves a balance between dependence and independence. An investigator who depends on a partner for a key method must ensure that the method is well validated and that the partner's results can be checked. An investigator who is independent must still respect the partner's expertise. Cumming (2014) argues that the uncertainty of a method supplied by a partner should be assessed as carefully as one the investigator produces.

The independent investigator should direct the collaborative question, so that the work advances the investigator's programme and not only the partner's. A collaboration that serves only as a service provision yields little for the investigator's own track record. Peng (2011) argued that the record of a collaboration should show who designed and analysed the work, so that the contributions are clear.

Networks and consortia pool data and resources, and they allow findings to be replicated across independent groups. Participation in a consortium requires the sharing of data under agreed terms, and the investigator should contribute as well as benefit. Managing the conflicts of collaboration and the expectations of partners is a continuing responsibility, and it should be handled openly and early.
