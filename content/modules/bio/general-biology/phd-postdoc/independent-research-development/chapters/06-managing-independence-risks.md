Independence brings freedom, and it brings risks that the researcher did not face under a mentor. Isolation is the loss of the daily guidance and the network that a mentor provided, and it can leave the new investigator without anyone to check their judgement. Ioannidis (2005) described how the absence of scrutiny allows errors and biases to accumulate, and the independent investigator must build the scrutiny that the mentor previously supplied.

The pressure to publish quickly is among the most dangerous of these risks, because it can encourage flexible analysis, selective reporting and the overstatement of results. A researcher who must produce papers to secure the next grant may be tempted to report the favourable analysis and to omit the rest. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that pre-registration, complete reporting and the discipline of the analysis plan are the practical defences against this pressure, and they should be adopted from the start.

The temptation to pursue fashionable questions for their funding appeal is a related risk, since fashionable questions attract attention but may offer less value than a patient programme on an important gap. Cumming (2014) argues that a programme should be judged by the precision and value of its estimates, and not by the attention it receives.

The burden of administration can displace the research time that the programme needs, and overcommitment to a single programme leaves the investigator vulnerable if it fails. The investigator should protect research time and maintain a portfolio of questions that spreads the risk. Peng (2011) argued that the reproducibility of a group's work depends on the time its leader gives to the methods, and the protection of that time is a scientific priority.

The mental health of a leader who carries responsibility for a team is a serious concern, and it is often neglected. Mentoring and peer networks provide support, perspective and the external judgement that independence otherwise lacks. Building such networks is among the most important tasks of the new investigator, and it protects both the researcher and the quality of the work.
