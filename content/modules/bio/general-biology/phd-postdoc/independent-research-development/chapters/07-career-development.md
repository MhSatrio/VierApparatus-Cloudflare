A research career is judged by appointment and promotion committees, and their criteria shape the behaviour of researchers who wish to advance. Where the criteria count only the number of publications, the incentive is to produce many papers, and the rigour, transparency and data sharing that make research reliable receive little credit. Ioannidis (2005) described how such incentives contribute to the unreliability of a field, and the early-career investigator must understand the criteria that will judge them.

The balance between the number of publications, the quality of methods, the contribution of data and code and the leadership of a programme should be made explicit in the investigator's record. A researcher who has shared well-documented data, published reproducible code and led a programme with rigorous methods has built a case that a committee should recognise, even if the publication count is modest. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the evaluation of research should reward these contributions, and the investigator should make them visible.

Team contributions must be recognised, and the investigator's own role within them must be stated clearly. A committee needs to see what the investigator did, not only what the group produced, and the record of independent contributions documents the investigator's leadership. Cumming (2014) argues that the record should show the precision of the investigator's estimates and the replication of their findings, since these reveal the quality of the work more directly than a count.

Replication, open data and teaching should be part of the evaluation, since they contribute to the reliability and the transmission of knowledge. An investigator who has replicated a finding or shared a dataset that others have reused has made a contribution that a publication count does not capture. Peng (2011) argued that the reuse of data and code is a measure of their value, and this should count in evaluation.

The pace of career progression is linked to funding cycles, and the investigator should plan the sequence of grants and outputs so that progress is steady. Advocacy for evaluation practices that reward rigour and transparency is part of the responsibility of established investigators, and early-career researchers can contribute to this change by insisting on the same standards in their own work.
