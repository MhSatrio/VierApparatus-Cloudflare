An independent programme is evaluated by funders, institutions and the field, and its report must show each of the things they need to judge. The question, the aims and the expected precision must be stated, and the investigator's leadership must be clear from the record of who designed, analysed and reported the work. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that a programme that cannot be evaluated cannot be trusted, and the report is the means of evaluation.

The methods and the pre-registered analysis plans should be described, and the outputs, including publications, must have their data and code deposited with identifiers. A reader should be able to check the programme's claims from its deposited materials. Peng (2011) argued that the means of verification must be available, and the deposition of data and code provides them.

Preliminary and negative results should be reported alongside the successes. A programme that reports only its successes overstates its performance, and its failures are often the most informative part of its record. Cumming (2014) argues that each estimate should be reported with its interval, including those that did not reach the expected effect, so that the reader can judge the programme's precision. Ioannidis (2005) described how the omission of negative results distorts a field, and the honest report of them protects the record.

The collaborations and their agreements, and the governance and the safety and ethics arrangements of the group, should be described, so that the conditions under which the work was done are visible. The statement of the programme's limits and of the conditions under which its findings apply completes the report. A programme's findings are claims about specific questions, populations and conditions, and the report should state their scope clearly so that they are not taken beyond the evidence that supports them.
