Most genes in a genome have an assigned function only by inference, and many have none. Characterising the function of an uncharacterised gene begins with prediction from its sequence, its structure and its position in the genome, but every prediction is a hypothesis. Ioannidis (2005) described how functional annotations inferred from homology can propagate errors through databases, and a prediction must be tested in the organism before it is accepted.

Loss-of-function alleles, produced by deletion, mutation or knockdown, are the usual first test. The phenotype of the mutant is compared with that of a control under the same conditions, and the comparison is made across several environments and developmental stages, since a gene may matter only under particular conditions. Jinek et al. (2012) described the programmable nuclease that now allows such alleles to be made precisely, and the design of the controls matters as much as the allele itself.

The rescue experiment is decisive. Restoring the wild-type gene to the mutant should reverse the phenotype, which shows that the phenotype is caused by the loss of that gene rather than by a second mutation in the background. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the controls that establish causation should be reported, and a phenotype without a rescue remains a correlation with the mutation.

The gene's pathway is identified through its interacting partners and through epistatic analysis, in which the phenotype of a double mutant reveals which genes act in the same route. Cumming (2014) argues that each quantitative phenotype should be reported with its interval, since a small difference in a noisy measurement is weak evidence of a role. Each claim about function should be stated with the evidence supporting it and the uncertainty that remains.
