A loss-of-function allele removes the gene or its product, and the choice of method depends on the organism and the question. Programmable nucleases allow targeted deletions and precise edits, and Jinek et al. (2012) described the RNA-guided enzyme that made such editing widely available. Cong et al. (2013) showed that several targets could be edited at once in mammalian cells, which extends the approach to gene families. Each method should be chosen so that its side effects are known and controlled.

The allele must be verified at the DNA, transcript and protein levels. A deletion at the DNA level may still leave a transcript that is partly functional, and a stop codon may allow the production of a truncated protein. Ioannidis (2005) described how the failure to verify a reagent can lead to conclusions that do not hold, and verification is the first safeguard in any genetic experiment.

The genetic background must be matched so that the phenotype can be attributed to the allele. Background mutations introduced during editing, or inherited from the parent strain, can produce differences that look like the effect of the target. Heterozygous and wild-type controls raised in parallel under identical conditions are necessary, and the comparison is made against them rather than against historical data.

Phenotyping should use standardised measures across environments, developmental stages and tissues, and the measurement should be blinded where the scoring is subjective. Cumming (2014) argues that each phenotype should be reported as an estimate of the difference from the control with its interval, since a visible effect may be small compared with the natural variation. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the blinding and the controls should be described in the report, so that the reader can judge the strength of the phenotype.
