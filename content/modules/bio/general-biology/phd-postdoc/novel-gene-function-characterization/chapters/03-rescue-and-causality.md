A rescue experiment is the strongest test of causation in genetics. If restoring the gene reverses the phenotype of the mutant, the loss of that gene is shown to be responsible for the phenotype, and the reversal is what distinguishes a genetic cause from a correlated defect. The rescue must be designed with care, because the reintroduced gene can itself alter the phenotype, and a rescue that is not properly controlled proves nothing.

The wild-type gene should be restored under its native regulatory control, so that it is expressed in the same tissues, at the same level and at the same time as the endogenous gene. A transgene driven by a strong, unnatural promoter may rescue the phenotype for reasons that do not reflect the natural function. Cong et al. (2013) described the editing methods that make such constructs precise, and the design of the construct must still be justified.

A domain-deleted or catalytically dead variant identifies which feature of the protein is required. If the variant fails to rescue, the deleted feature is necessary; if it rescues, the feature is dispensable for the phenotype. The control that the rescue construct does not alter the phenotype in the wild-type background is essential, since an overexpressed protein may have effects of its own.

A second, independent allele excludes the possibility that a second mutation in the background causes the phenotype. Two alleles generated by different methods, which both produce the phenotype and which both are rescued, provide strong evidence. Ioannidis (2005) described how a single result that depends on one reagent is fragile, and independent confirmation guards against this.

The rescue should be reported with the same quantitative rigour as the original phenotype. Cumming (2014) argues that the degree of rescue should be estimated with its interval, since a partial rescue is informative and must not be described as complete. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that every control should be described, so that the strength of the causal claim can be judged.
