A report of gene function must make clear what has been shown and what has been inferred. A predicted function, a phenotype, a rescued phenotype and a mechanistically established function are four different levels of evidence, and they should not be presented as equivalent. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the strength of each claim should be visible to the reader, and a title or abstract that promises a mechanism when only a phenotype was observed misleads.

Each functional claim should be stated with the alleles, the controls and the conditions on which it rests. A phenotype observed in one allele under one condition supports a narrower claim than the same phenotype confirmed in two alleles across several conditions. Ioannidis (2005) described how findings drawn from a narrow base are often over-generalised, and the report should state the base explicitly.

Phenotypes should be presented as estimates with intervals, showing the size of the effect and the precision with which it was measured. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval is the means of conveying how strongly the data support the claim, and a phenotype reported without it cannot be assessed. The raw phenotypic data should be deposited so that the analysis can be repeated and the estimates checked.

Sequences, alleles, strains and data should be deposited with identifiers, so that an independent group can obtain the materials and repeat the key experiments. Peng (2011) argued that the reproducibility of a result depends on the availability of the means to check it, and the gene should be named in accordance with the nomenclature of the organism's research community so that it can be found and compared. The discussion should state alternative explanations and the limits of the conditions tested, and the functional assignment should be revised as further evidence arrives.
