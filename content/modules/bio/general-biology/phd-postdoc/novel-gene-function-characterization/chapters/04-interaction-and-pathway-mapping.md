A gene's function is understood better when its place in a pathway is known, and two methods are used to find that place. Interaction screens identify the proteins associated with the gene product, and genetic analysis reveals which genes act with it. Each method produces candidates that must be tested, since neither establishes that a partner acts on the same process. Ioannidis (2005) described how large screens produce many false positives, and the candidates must be filtered before they are interpreted.

Affinity purification coupled with mass spectrometry captures proteins that associate with the gene product in the cell. The list of co-purified proteins contains both direct partners and members of the same complex, and it contains contaminants that bind non-specifically. Controls, such as purifications from cells lacking the tagged protein, distinguish genuine associations from background. Cong et al. (2013) described the genome engineering that makes such tagged lines possible, and the tag must be shown not to alter the protein's function.

Direct physical interaction is distinguished from co-purification through a larger complex by reconstitution with purified components, or by the mapping of contact sites. Yeast two-hybrid and proximity labelling methods detect interactions in different contexts, and each has its own false positive rate. Cumming (2014) argues that the strength of an interaction should be reported as an estimate with its uncertainty, rather than as a simple yes or no.

Epistatic analysis asks whether two genes act in the same pathway or in parallel. If a double mutant shows the phenotype of the more severe single mutant, the genes are likely in the same pathway; if the double mutant is more severe than either single, they are likely in parallel. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the pathway map should be reported with the evidence for each link, so that readers can see which connections are established and which are provisional.
