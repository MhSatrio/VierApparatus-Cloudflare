A gene that is characterised in one species can be tested in another by complementation, in which the orthologue from the second species is introduced into a mutant of the first. If the orthologue restores the phenotype, the function is conserved in a meaningful sense. If it does not, the function has diverged, or the orthologue is not expressed or regulated correctly in the new host, and the reason must be investigated before a conclusion is drawn.

A failed complementation is ambiguous. It may reflect a true difference in protein activity, or a difference in the regulation of the gene, or simply a difference in the host environment. Ioannidis (2005) described how a negative result can be taken as proof of difference when it reflects a technical failure, and the controls must show that the orthologue is expressed and that the host supports the process.

Divergence in regulation is often more important than divergence in protein activity. The protein may be functionally identical while the timing or place of its expression differs, and this produces a different phenotype without any change in the protein itself. Cong et al. (2013) described how precise editing of regulatory sequences can test this possibility directly, and the regulatory and coding differences should be separated experimentally.

The evolutionary rate of a gene indicates which parts of its sequence are under functional constraint. Regions that change little across species are likely to be essential, and regions that change rapidly may be dispensable or may have adapted to new roles. Cumming (2014) argues that the comparison of rates should be reported with its uncertainty, since the estimate of constraint depends on the number of species compared. The phenotypes produced by orthologues should be compared quantitatively, to identify both the shared roles and the species-specific ones, and Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the comparison should be pre-specified where possible.
