A gene may have no visible phenotype under the conditions most often used in the laboratory and still be essential under others. Its function is then conditional, and a study that tests only standard conditions will wrongly conclude that the gene is dispensable. The characterisation of a gene must therefore include the variation of the conditions that the organism is likely to encounter, such as temperature, nutrient supply, stress and developmental stage.

Inducible and tissue-specific systems allow the gene to be removed at a chosen time or in a chosen place. Removing a gene in an adult tissue avoids the lethality that would prevent the study of its later role, and it isolates the tissue in which the gene acts. Cong et al. (2013) described the genome engineering that makes conditional alleles possible, and the induction must be shown to be efficient and to affect only the intended target.

Genotype-by-environment interaction describes the situation in which the effect of an allele depends on the environment. Its presence means that the function observed in one condition does not generalise to others, and the analysis must estimate the interaction term with its interval. Cumming (2014) argues that the size of an interaction is often more informative than the main effect, and it should be reported with its uncertainty.

The conditions under which the function was and was not observed must be reported. A gene characterised only under one condition can be described as conditionally essential, but the description must state the conditions and not imply that the function is universal. Ioannidis (2005) described how findings drawn from narrow conditions are often over-generalised, and Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the boundaries of a finding should be stated so that others can test its generality.
