A genome editing experiment is reported so that a reader can judge the edit and repeat it. The report begins with the guide, the repair template or editor, the delivery route and the dose, since each affects the outcome. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that a method is only as useful as its description, and an editing method described without its guide sequence or its dose cannot be repeated.

The intended edit must be stated as a sequence, and the verified product distribution reported with it. The report should give the frequency of the intended conversion, of bystander edits and of indels, and the number of independent clones on which each figure rests. Cumming (2014) argues that each efficiency should be given with its interval, since the number of clones analysed determines how precisely it is known, and a single figure without its uncertainty overstates the knowledge.

The off-target and safety assessment must be reported with its method, its sensitivity and the regions it could not examine. A negative finding from a method of limited sensitivity is reported as such, and the limits are stated. Ioannidis (2005) described how a claim of safety drawn from an incomplete assessment can mislead those who rely on it, and the limits protect the reader from overconfidence.

The matched controls for the consequences of editing, such as mock-treated or parental cells subjected to the same culture, must be reported. The deposition of guide sequences, constructs and sequencing data with identifiers allows an independent group to verify the edit and to repeat the analysis. Peng (2011) argued that the reproducibility of a result depends on the availability of the means to check it, and any change to the protocol made during the work must be recorded and reported with its reason.
