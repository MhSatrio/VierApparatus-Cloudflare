The editing reagents must reach the nucleus of the target cells, and the route of delivery determines how long the nuclease remains active. A plasmid that persists for days keeps the nuclease present for longer, which increases the chance of off-target cutting. A ribonucleoprotein complex, which is a preassembled nuclease and guide, acts quickly and is degraded, which limits the window of activity. Mali et al. (2013) used plasmid delivery in human cells, and the choice of route should be justified by the balance between efficiency and risk.

The dose must be optimised to achieve efficient editing without excessive toxicity. Too little reagent produces few edits, and too much causes cell death or stress that alters the phenotype under study. A dose titration with measured efficiency and viability at each level identifies a workable range, and the chosen dose should be reported with the measured efficiency and its interval. Cumming (2014) argues that efficiency should be reported as an estimate with its uncertainty, since it varies with the cell type and the batch.

Selection markers enrich for cells that received the reagents, which raises the frequency of edits in the population. The marker must be removed or shown not to affect the phenotype under study, since a drug resistance gene or a fluorescent protein can change the behaviour of the cell. Ioannidis (2005) described how a population selected for one property may differ from the population as a whole, and the verification should therefore be made on individual clones.

Single-cell cloning isolates a population derived from one cell, which is genetically uniform and allows the analysis of each clone separately. Clones should be verified by sequencing at the target and by a functional readout, and the number of independent clones analysed should be reported. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the number of independent replicates is a basic part of the methods, and a result from one clone is weak evidence of a general effect of the edit.
