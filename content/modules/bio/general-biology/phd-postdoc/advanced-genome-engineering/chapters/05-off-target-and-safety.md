Predicted off-target sites are a limited guide to the risks of an edit, because the prediction misses sites that differ from the guide in unexpected ways, and it cannot detect structural changes such as large deletions or translocations. Unbiased methods detect breaks across the genome without relying on prediction, and they provide a more complete picture of where the nuclease acted. Jinek et al. (2012) described the nuclease mechanism that determines which sites can be cut, and the detection methods test these possibilities directly.

Sequencing-based methods identify structural variants that predicted-site analysis would miss, including translocations between cut sites and large deletions. Each method has a sensitivity, and a negative result from a method of limited sensitivity does not show that no change occurred. The safety assessment must state the sensitivity of the methods used, so that a reader can judge how much the absence of a finding means. Cumming (2014) argues that the detection rate should be reported with its interval, since a method that found no changes in a few cells establishes little.

An off-target edit matters only if it lies in a gene or a regulatory region whose disruption could alter the phenotype under study. An edit in a non-coding region far from any gene may be irrelevant, while one in a tumour suppressor or in a key regulatory element may be consequential. The assessment should classify each off-target site by its genomic context and state which are plausibly important. Cong et al. (2013) noted that the number of off-target sites varies with the guide, and the classification should be applied to each guide used.

Edited clones accumulate changes during culture, and whole-genome sequencing of clones can reveal changes that arose after the editing. These changes may be unrelated to the edit, and they can confound the phenotype if they are not identified. Ioannidis (2005) described how an unrecognised difference between samples can produce a spurious result, and the genome of each clone should be compared with that of the parental line. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the limits of the safety assessment should be reported, including the regions the methods could not examine.
