A nuclease break is a form of DNA damage, and the cell responds to it by arresting growth, repairing the lesion or in some cases triggering death. This response has consequences for the edited population. Cells that tolerate the break and repair it well may be selected, and cells that lose a chromosome segment or carry an extensive deletion may be lost from the population. Cong et al. (2013) noted that multiple breaks increase this stress, and the consequence must be assessed for every experiment.

The selection against damaged cells can bias the population toward edits that are less disruptive, and this bias can influence the interpretation of a phenotype. A phenotype observed in a surviving clone may reflect the survivors rather than the typical edited cell. Ioannidis (2005) described how selection within an experiment can produce results that do not represent the population the investigator intended to study, and the bias must be considered in the design.

The damage response also changes the expression of many genes, including those involved in stress, cell cycle and immune signalling. These changes can be mistaken for the consequence of the intended edit, and they confound a transcriptional readout. The comparison should therefore include controls that experienced the same damage without the intended change, so that the specific effect of the edit can be separated from the general response.

Controls must be treated identically in culture, including mock transfection and the same selection, so that the stress of the process is matched. Growth rate and viability should be measured and reported with their intervals. Cumming (2014) argues that the difference between edited and control cells should be estimated with its interval, since a small reduction in growth may be real but difficult to distinguish from variation between cultures. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the matched controls should be described in the methods so that the reader can judge the attribution of the phenotype.
