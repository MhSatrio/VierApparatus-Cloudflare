Genomic data are inherently identifying, since a genome can be linked to a person through relatives, through public genealogical databases or through rare variants. Even summary statistics can carry a risk of re-identification in some settings, and the release of such statistics must be considered carefully. The governance of a large cohort must therefore protect individual-level data in a way that the open sharing of findings cannot. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that openness is a value, but it must be weighed against the privacy of the people who make the research possible.

Controlled access allows approved researchers to analyse individual-level data within a secure environment, under a data use agreement that states the purposes and the conditions of use. Summary statistics may be shared more openly, but the risk they carry must be assessed for each release. Wilkinson et al. (2016) described the principles of findable and reusable data, and these principles are pursued within the limits set by the protection of participants.

The consent under which participants agreed to their data being used is the basis of the governance. Its scope defines the research that may be done and the audiences to which findings may be disclosed. A use that goes beyond the scope of the consent requires fresh consent or an authorisation, and the governance must enforce this. Ioannidis (2005) described how the misuse of data beyond their terms can damage the trust on which future research depends.

The audit and reporting of access show who used the data and for what purpose, and they provide the accountability that the arrangement requires. The return of individual results to participants, where it is appropriate, carries a duty of care, since the results may have clinical significance. Cumming (2014) argues that the uncertainty of such results should be communicated with them, so that participants understand what they mean.

The access arrangements and the restrictions that apply to the findings should be reported, so that readers know how the data may be used and how the results may be verified. A cohort whose data are restricted must still allow approved researchers to check its findings.
