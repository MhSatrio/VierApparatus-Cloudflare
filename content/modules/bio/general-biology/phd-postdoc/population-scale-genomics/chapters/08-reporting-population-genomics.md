A population-scale genomic study is reported by its cohort, its analysis and its findings, and each part must be visible to a reader who wishes to evaluate it. The cohort must be described with its sampling frame, its response and its representation by ancestry. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the population on which a finding rests determines what it means, and the cohort description is the first thing a reader needs.

The analysis plan and the significance threshold must be reported, with the derivation of the threshold from the number of independent tests. The controls for stratification and relatedness must be described, and the genomic inflation factor reported to show how well the controls worked. Ioannidis (2005) described how the multiplicity of tests and uncorrected confounding produce false findings, and the threshold and the controls are the safeguards.

The effect sizes must be reported with their intervals, together with the results of replication in independent cohorts. Cumming (2014) argues that the agreement between intervals is the appropriate criterion for replication, and a finding without replication should be presented as provisional. The polygenic score accuracy must be reported in each ancestry group, with the design of the validation, since accuracy varies across groups.

The fine-mapping results should be reported with their credible sets and the assumptions made, and the governance and access arrangements for individual-level data should be stated. Peng (2011) argued that the means to check a result must be available, and the deposition of summary statistics and code with identifiers makes the analysis reproducible within the limits of privacy.

The report should close with the populations to which the findings apply and those to which they may not. A genomic finding is a claim about specific populations, and its scope must be stated so that it is not extended beyond the groups in which it was tested.
