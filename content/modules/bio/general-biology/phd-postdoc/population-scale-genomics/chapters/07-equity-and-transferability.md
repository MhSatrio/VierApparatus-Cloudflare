Most genomic discovery studies have been conducted in populations of European ancestry, and the findings from them transfer poorly to other groups. Variants may have different frequencies, different linkage structures and different effects in different populations, so a finding made in one group may be inaccurate or absent in another. The imbalance means that the benefits of genomic medicine accrue disproportionately to the groups already well served, and the imbalance itself must be reported as a limitation. Ioannidis (2005) described how samples that do not represent the population of interest produce findings that do not generalise.

The reduced power and increased uncertainty of analyses in under-represented groups are a consequence of their smaller samples, and they must be stated. An association that is not significant in a small group may still be present, and the absence of significance is not evidence of absence. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval in each group should be reported, since the widest intervals are in the groups with the least data.

Strategies for diversifying cohorts include deliberate recruitment across ancestry groups and the analysis of diverse samples with methods that account for ancestry. The strategies have costs and require the involvement of the communities concerned, and their effect on the transferability of the findings should be evaluated. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the composition of the sample is part of the claim, and the diversity of the sample should be reported with every finding.

A difference in an association between groups does not establish a biological difference between them, because the groups also differ in environment, in measurement, in the structure of the data and in many other respects. Claims that such differences reflect the biology of groups are scientifically unsound and have been used to justify harmful distinctions. The report should state this explicitly, and the findings should be presented by group rather than as a single average that hides the differences. Peng (2011) argued that the analysis by group must be documented so that it can be checked.

Researchers have a responsibility to communicate the limits of transferability to clinicians, to the public and to the communities whose data were used. A finding presented without its scope invites misapplication, and the statement of scope is part of responsible genomic research.
