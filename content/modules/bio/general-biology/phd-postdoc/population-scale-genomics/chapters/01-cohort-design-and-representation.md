The design of a population cohort determines what can be concluded from it, and the sampling frame sets the boundary of its generalisation. A cohort recruited from a particular region, a particular health system or a particular set of volunteers represents that population, and the findings apply most directly to it. Ioannidis (2005) described how the composition of a sample can produce results that do not hold in the general population, and the sampling frame must be reported for every cohort.

Self-selection is a common source of bias, since people who volunteer for research often differ in health, education and behaviour from those who do not. An association estimated in a self-selected sample may be distorted if the selection depends on both the exposure and the outcome, a bias known as collider bias. Cumming (2014) argues that the response rate and the characteristics of responders and non-responders should be reported with their intervals, so that the size of the potential bias can be judged.

Phenotypes collected through questionnaires or linked records vary in accuracy, and the misclassification of phenotypes weakens associations in ways that are often underestimated. The validation of each phenotype against a more accurate measure should be reported where it exists. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the measurement of each variable should be documented, since the quality of the phenotype determines the quality of the association.

Consent and recontact arrangements determine whether a cohort can be used for future studies, and they must respect the terms under which participants agreed. Representation of ancestry groups is a particular concern, because genetic findings from one group often transfer poorly to others, and under-representation limits the benefit of the research to those who are already well served. The composition of the cohort by ancestry must be reported, and the consequences of the imbalance for the transferability of the findings must be stated. Peng (2011) argued that the description of the data determines how they can be reused, and the cohort description is part of that description.

Retention over time and attrition can bias longitudinal analyses, since participants who leave a cohort may differ from those who stay. The attrition should be reported, and its effect on the analysis examined. The report should give the sampling frame, the response rate and the composition of the cohort, so that a reader can judge the generalisation of its findings.
