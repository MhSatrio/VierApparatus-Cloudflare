Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy observes the nuclei of atoms in a protein dissolved in solution, and it reports on structure and dynamics without the need for crystals. Each resonance must first be assigned to a specific atom, which is a substantial task in a large protein. Once assigned, the resonances give restraints that describe the structure, and the restraints are the data from which the structure is calculated.

The nuclear Overhauser effect gives distances between atoms that are close in space, typically within about five ångströms, and these distance restraints are the main structural data. Chemical shifts report on the local environment and the secondary structure, and relaxation measurements report on the timescales of motion. Ioannidis (2005) described how a model constructed from incomplete data can appear more certain than it is, and the number and quality of the restraints must be stated.

A structure is calculated as an ensemble of conformations that satisfy the restraints. The spread of the ensemble is a measure of how precisely the restraints determine the structure, but it is not a measure of accuracy. A tight ensemble may be wrong if the restraints are biased or if the calculation has converged on a single incorrect solution. Cumming (2014) argues that precision and accuracy must not be confused, and the uncertainty of the structure should be reported with the intervals appropriate to each.

The dynamics revealed by relaxation are often as important as the structure, because flexible regions may be functional. These measurements report on motions over a range of timescales, and their interpretation requires a model of the motion. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the restraints, the ensemble and the validation should be deposited, so that the structure can be checked and the dynamics can be reanalysed.
