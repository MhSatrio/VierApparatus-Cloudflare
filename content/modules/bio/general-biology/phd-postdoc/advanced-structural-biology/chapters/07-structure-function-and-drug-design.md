A structure of a protein bound to a ligand shows where the ligand sits and which residues contact it, and this information explains the affinity and selectivity of the ligand. The explanation is a hypothesis that predicts the effect of changing a contact residue, and it becomes useful when the prediction is tested. Ioannidis (2005) described how an explanation that fits the known data can be unfalsifiable, and the value of a structure lies in the predictions it makes.

Structure-based design uses the shape of the binding pocket to propose molecules that might bind more strongly or selectively. Candidate molecules are docked into the pocket and scored, and the highest-scoring candidates are taken forward. Jumper et al. (2021) showed that predicted structures can be used where experimental ones are unavailable, and the same caution applies to the models used for docking.

Docking scores are approximate, and their correlation with measured affinity is often weak. A high score does not guarantee strong binding, and the ranking of candidates by score may not match the ranking by measurement. Cumming (2014) argues that the agreement between prediction and measurement should be reported with its interval, since a claim of predictive power requires that the uncertainty be known.

Fragment screening and crystallographic soaking identify binding sites by detecting small molecules bound to the protein. These experiments find sites that the designer may not have anticipated, and they provide structural evidence for each binding mode. Predictions from the structures must be tested by mutagenesis and by binding measurement, and Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the structures and the measurements should be reported together, so that the link between structure and function can be judged.
