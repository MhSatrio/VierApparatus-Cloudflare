A systems medicine study combines data, models and clinical claims, and its report must make each link checkable. The patient cohorts must be described with their composition and their size, and the data platforms, the harmonisation and the missingness must be stated. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the population on which a claim rests determines what the claim means, and the description of the cohort is the first thing a reader must see.

The stratifications should be reported with their consensus, their stability and their association with outcome, and the driver claims with the level of causal evidence for each. A claim that a process drives the disease must be accompanied by the kind of evidence that supports it, whether association, quasi-experiment or intervention. Ioannidis (2005) described how the confusion of these levels leads to failed therapeutic targets, and the classification makes the confusion visible.

The network models should be reported with their calibration, their sensitivity to the structure and the parameters, and the predictions with their uncertainty. The clinical validation must name the external cohort, the calibration of predicted risks and the incremental value over established factors, each with its interval. Cumming (2014) argues that every agreement and every risk should be given with the uncertainty that its sample supports.

The governance and consent conditions must be stated, along with the restrictions they impose on the analysis and on the release of data. Where the data are controlled, the access procedure should be described so that qualified researchers can verify the findings. Peng (2011) argued that the means of verification must exist for a result to be trusted, and the governance must permit it.

The report should close by stating the populations and the clinical decisions to which the findings apply, and the populations and decisions to which they do not. A systems medicine finding is a claim about a specific group and a specific choice, and a statement of its scope protects patients from its misapplication.
