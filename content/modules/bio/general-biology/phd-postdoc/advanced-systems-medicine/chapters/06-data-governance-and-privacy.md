Patient data are among the most sensitive data in research, and their use in systems medicine must respect the consent under which they were given. The consent defines the purposes for which the data may be analysed and the audiences to which findings may be disclosed. A systems medicine study that uses data for a purpose beyond the consent breaches the trust of the patients who contributed, and the governance must enforce the limits. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that openness is valuable, but it does not override the consent of those whose data are used.

De-identification removes direct identifiers, but genomic data are inherently identifying, since a genome can be linked to a person through relatives or through public databases. The claim that genomic data are anonymous is therefore false, and the governance must treat them as identifiable. Wilkinson et al. (2016) described the principles of findable and reusable data, and these principles must be balanced against the identifiability of patients.

Controlled access allows approved researchers to use the data under a data use agreement, without releasing the data to the public. The approval criteria, the conditions of use and the audit of access should be stated, so that the data are used only as agreed. Secure computing environments, in which the analysis is performed without the data leaving a protected system, further reduce the risk. Ioannidis (2005) described how restricted access can prevent the verification of findings, and the controlled procedure must allow qualified researchers to check the results.

Equity is a governance concern as well as an ethical one. If the data over-represent certain populations, the systems medicine findings will apply best to them, and the benefits may not reach the under-represented. The report should state the composition of the data and the populations to which the findings may not apply. Cumming (2014) argues that the variation across subgroups should be reported with its interval, so that the limits of generalisation are visible.

The governance arrangements, the restrictions that shaped the findings and the access conditions should be reported. Peng (2011) argued that the reproducibility of a result depends on the means to check it, and the governance must be designed to permit verification by approved parties while protecting the patients.
