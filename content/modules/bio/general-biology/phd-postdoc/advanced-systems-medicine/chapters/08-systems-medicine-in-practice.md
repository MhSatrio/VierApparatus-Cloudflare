A molecular test that predicts outcome has not yet shown that it improves care. A clinical utility study tests whether using the test to guide decisions leads to better outcomes than the standard approach, and this is a demanding requirement that many promising tests have not met. Ioannidis (2005) described how the accumulation of prognostic findings has often outpaced evidence that they change outcomes, and the utility study is the step that closes this gap.

Clinicians must understand the test and its uncertainty in order to use it well. Training should cover what the test measures, how well it predicts, what its interval is and what it does not tell the clinician. Cumming (2014) argues that a test result should be presented with its interval, since a single risk value conveys a precision that the test may not have. Communicating the uncertainty to patients is equally important, and it should be done in plain terms that allow them to take part in the decision.

After adoption, the test must be monitored for drift in its performance, since laboratories, populations and practices change over time. A test that performs well in the study population may perform differently elsewhere, and the monitoring should compare its calibration with observed outcomes in each setting. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that such monitoring is part of the evidence, and its results should be reported.

Equitable access is a requirement of responsible translation. A test that only well-resourced patients can obtain widens existing inequalities, and the cost, the availability and the distribution of the test should be considered from the outset. Peng (2011) argued that the means of verification should be available to all who need to check the test, and this principle extends to the means of access.

The communication of a test result to a patient should include its uncertainty and its limits, as the evidence on which the test rests permits. A result presented as certain when it is probabilistic can lead to decisions that the evidence does not support, and an honest account of the uncertainty respects both the patient and the science that produced the test.
