A driver process is one whose disruption contributes to the disease, and it is distinct from a process that is merely associated with the disease. Network analysis can identify processes that are central, modular or enriched in patients, and these are candidate drivers. Ioannidis (2005) described how associations found in observational data are frequently not causal, and the candidate drivers must be tested before they are treated as causes.

A process may appear central in a network because it has been studied extensively, so that many interactions are recorded for it, rather than because it drives the disease. This study bias inflates the apparent centrality of well-known genes and proteins. The control is to compare centrality with a measure that accounts for the number of studies, and the bias must be reported. Cumming (2014) argues that the strength of an association should be stated with its interval, and the bias can make a weak association look strong.

Perturbation experiments establish causation by changing the process and observing the effect on the disease. In cells and animals this is often feasible, and in patients it may be possible through trials or natural experiments. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the distinction between association and intervention should be made explicit in any claim about a driver, since the two support very different levels of confidence.

Downstream consequences of the disease are often correlated with the driver and may be mistaken for it. A process that changes as a result of the disease is a marker, not a cause, and the separation requires the temporal order of events or an intervention that targets the upstream process. Mendelian randomisation uses genetic variants that influence an exposure to test the direction of causation, and natural experiments exploit situations in which a change occurs without the patient's choice. These quasi-experimental designs give evidence of direction, and their assumptions must be stated.

The inferred drivers should be reported with the evidence for each level of causation, from association through quasi-experiment to intervention. Peng (2011) argued that the analysis must be reproducible, and the classification of the evidence is part of the claim. Ioannidis (2005) described how the confusion of these levels has led to many failed therapeutic targets, and an honest classification protects against this error.
