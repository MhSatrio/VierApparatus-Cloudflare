Systems medicine applies the methods of systems biology to disease, treating a disease as a perturbation of a molecular network rather than as the malfunction of a single gene. Multi-omic measurements from patients are integrated into network models, and the structure of the network suggests which processes drive the disease and which are its consequences. The approach promises to explain the heterogeneity of disease and to suggest therapies, but it depends on patient data that are noisy, confounded and often limited in number. Ioannidis (2005) described how patient-derived signatures are frequently unreproducible, and the approach must be judged by its validation.

Patients can be stratified into subtypes by their molecular profiles, and the stability of each subtype must be assessed. A stratification that changes when a few patients are removed, or when a different method is used, is not a reliable description of disease biology. Cumming (2014) argues that the uncertainty of each stratum should be reported with its interval, since a small stratum has a wide uncertainty about its membership and its characteristics.

Driver processes inferred from the structure of networks are hypotheses about causation, and association in patient data does not establish that a process causes the disease. A driver becomes a therapeutic target only when intervention on it changes the disease in an experiment or a trial. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the distinction between association and intervention should be stated explicitly in any claim about a therapeutic target.

Models of the response of a disease network to perturbation can be used to predict therapeutic effects, and these predictions must be validated in independent cohorts and, where possible, prospectively. Peng (2011) argued that the data, the code and the stratification method must be available so that the findings can be reproduced, and the remaining chapters examine the integration of multi-omic data, the stratification, the drivers and the clinical validation.
