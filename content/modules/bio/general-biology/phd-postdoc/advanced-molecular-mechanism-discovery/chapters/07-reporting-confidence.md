A mechanism is reported as a set of steps, and each step carries a different degree of confidence. Some steps have been established by replicated perturbation and reconstitution; others rest on a single line of evidence and remain provisional. A report that presents all steps with equal assurance misleads the reader about which parts of the account can be relied upon. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the strength of each claim should be stated so that the reader can see the basis of the whole.

A clear presentation is a diagram in which each arrow represents a step and is accompanied by the evidence that supports it. The reader can see at a glance which arrows are supported by intervention and which by association, and the diagram can be revised as evidence changes. Ioannidis (2005) described how a plausible account can persist for years without the evidence for its individual steps being examined, and the diagram makes that examination routine.

Each step should be stated with the experiment that would falsify it. A mechanism that cannot be shown to be wrong by any experiment is not a scientific claim, and the statement of falsifying experiments commits the investigator to a test. The alternatives that the data do not exclude should also be reported, so that the reader knows the account is one of several possibilities rather than the only one consistent with the evidence.

Quantitative claims within the mechanism should be reported with their estimates and intervals. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval conveys the uncertainty of a measurement, and the reader can judge whether a quantitative step is firm or tentative. The account of the mechanism must be revised as new evidence arrives, and the revision should be recorded so that the history of the understanding is visible.
