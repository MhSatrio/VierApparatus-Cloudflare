A mechanistic hypothesis states which molecules participate in a process, in what order they act and how each step depends on the one before. A useful hypothesis is specific enough to be wrong. It must predict observations that would not occur if it were false, and a hypothesis that accommodates every possible result explains nothing. Ioannidis (2005) described how flexible hypotheses that can absorb any outcome produce findings that appear confirmed but are untested.

From a hypothesis, the investigator derives predictions. For each step the question is what would be observed if the step occurred and what would be observed if it did not. A prediction is useful when the two outcomes are distinguishable with the available methods. Cumming (2014) argues that a prediction should be stated with the size of the effect expected and the precision with which it can be measured, since a prediction that cannot be distinguished from its alternative from the data is of little use.

Competing mechanisms should be ranked by the experiments that separate them. Where two mechanisms predict the same outcome for an experiment, that experiment cannot distinguish them, and a different one is needed. The investigator should identify the experiment that would favour one mechanism over another and state in advance the outcome that would favour each. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the documentation of predictions before the experiments are performed protects the interpretation from hindsight.

The investigator must be willing to abandon a mechanism that fails a decisive test. This is often difficult, because a mechanism may have been developed over years and may be closely tied to a reputation. Ioannidis (2005) emphasised that the ability to abandon a false hypothesis is a sign of a healthy field, and the readiness to accept a negative result is as important as the ability to propose a positive one.
