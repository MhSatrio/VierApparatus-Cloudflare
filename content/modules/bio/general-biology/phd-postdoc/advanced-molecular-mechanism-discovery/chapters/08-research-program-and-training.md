A research programme built around a molecular mechanism is a sequence of experiments, and each should follow from the one before. The first experiments establish that the process occurs and that a candidate component is involved. Later experiments test the predictions that follow from the mechanism, and each result either strengthens the account or redirects the programme. Ioannidis (2005) described how a programme that continues without testing its central claim can accumulate effort without knowledge, and the sequence must be designed to test.

The researcher must be trained in the methods the programme requires: perturbation, reconstitution and quantitative analysis. Training in the statistics of estimation, in the reporting of intervals and in the principles of reproducibility is equally important, since the value of the programme depends on the reliability of its measurements. Cumming (2014) argues that a researcher who understands the interval can judge when a result is firm enough to build upon.

Collaborations bring complementary methods and the independent replication that the mechanism needs. A structural biologist, a computational modeller or a laboratory with a different organism can test the mechanism in ways the original group cannot. The collaborations should be planned so that the work is shared fairly and so that the independent replications are conducted with pre-specified methods. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that collaboration strengthens the reproducibility of science, and the programme should be designed to benefit from it.

A programme must manage the risk that its central mechanism is wrong. The investigator should identify the result that would lead to redirection and state it in advance, so that the programme can change course without loss of face or of evidence. Negative results must be published at each stage, since they inform the field and prevent others from repeating the same failed route. Peng (2011) argued that the record of a programme, including its failures, is part of its contribution.

The researcher's independence grows through the formulation of their own hypotheses and the design of their own tests. A postdoctoral researcher who begins to propose the mechanisms that the programme should test, rather than only executing the plans of others, develops the judgement on which an independent career depends.
