Kinetic measurements describe how fast a molecular step proceeds, and thermodynamic measurements describe the energy of its states. Together they distinguish between mechanisms that predict different rates and affinities. A mechanism that predicts a particular dependence of rate on substrate concentration is tested by measuring that dependence, and the fit of the measured curve to the predicted one is the basis of the comparison.

The fitting of a kinetic model yields parameters, such as a maximum rate and a Michaelis constant, each of which should be reported with its interval. An estimate without its interval conceals how well the data determine it, and two mechanisms may differ in their best-fit parameters while their intervals overlap so much that the data cannot distinguish them. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval is the essential summary of a fitted parameter, and it should be shown alongside the estimate.

The predictions of alternative mechanisms must be compared with the measured data. The comparison should use a formal criterion, such as the difference in fit between two models that accounts for their number of parameters, so that a more complex mechanism is not favoured merely because it fits more closely. Peng (2011) argued that the model and the fitting procedure must be stated so that the comparison can be repeated by others.

Binding affinities and thermodynamic parameters describe the energy of interactions, and they are sensitive to the conditions of measurement, such as temperature, pH and ionic strength. A parameter measured under one set of conditions may not hold under another, and the conditions should be reported with each measurement. Ioannidis (2005) described how the sensitivity of a result to unrecognised conditions can produce findings that do not replicate, and the reporting of conditions is the safeguard.
