Reconstitution tests sufficiency by rebuilding a process from purified components. If a process occurs when a defined set of components is combined under defined conditions, those components are sufficient for it within that system. The strength of the result depends on the purity and the defined character of the components, since a contaminating factor could produce the process and be wrongly credited.

Each component must be purified to a defined standard and its identity and activity verified. Purity is measured, and the activity of each preparation is tested so that a batch that has lost function does not invalidate the experiment. Peng (2011) argued that the materials and their characterisation are part of the methods, and a reconstitution that cannot be reproduced because its components are poorly defined provides little evidence.

Omission controls show that each component is required in the reconstituted system. Each component is removed in turn, and the process should fail when it is absent. A process that continues without a component the mechanism supposedly requires either indicates that the component is not needed or that another factor substitutes for it. Cumming (2014) argues that the magnitude of the loss should be reported with its interval, since a partial loss is informative about the contribution of each component.

Sufficiency in a defined system does not establish sufficiency in the cell, where many other factors are present. A component that is sufficient in a test tube may be regulated in the cell by factors that are absent from the reconstitution. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result obtained under simplified conditions may not survive the transfer to a more complex system, and the bridge between the two must be established by further experiments. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the conditions of the reconstitution should be reported fully, so that readers can judge how far the result extends.
