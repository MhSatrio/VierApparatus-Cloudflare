A perturbation experiment alters a component and observes the consequence for the process. Removing a component by genetic knockout, knockdown or chemical inhibition tests whether it is needed, and adding or overexpressing it tests whether it is sufficient. The strength of the inference depends on the design of the perturbation and of its controls, and an effect that appears after a perturbation is not automatically a consequence of the component that was targeted.

Controls must distinguish a specific effect from non-specific ones. A chemical inhibitor may kill cells or act on other targets, and a genetic knockdown may alter gene expression in ways unrelated to its intended target. The control should show that the effect depends on the component, for example by using a second, independent method of perturbation that gives the same result. Ioannidis (2005) described how off-target effects have produced many findings that did not hold, and the independent method guards against this.

The rescue experiment restores the component to the perturbed system and tests whether the effect is reversed. A rescue that reverses the effect is strong evidence that the component was responsible, since it shows a dependence that can be turned on and off. Peng (2011) argued that the steps of such experiments must be recorded so that they can be repeated, and the rescue must be described as precisely as the perturbation itself.

A graded response, in which the size of the effect changes with the dose of the perturbation or the time after it, strengthens a causal interpretation. Each effect should be quantified with its interval, since the strength of a mechanistic claim depends on how precisely the consequence is measured. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval conveys the uncertainty of the measurement, and a perturbation whose effect is imprecise supports a weaker claim.

A perturbation can alter other parts of the system, so the consequence observed may not be a direct effect of the targeted component. The investigator must consider the indirect routes by which the effect could arise and design experiments that distinguish them. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the limits of the interpretation should be stated, and a perturbation that is consistent with several routes should be reported as such.
