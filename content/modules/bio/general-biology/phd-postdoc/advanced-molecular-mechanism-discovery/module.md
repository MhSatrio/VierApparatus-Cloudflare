A molecular mechanism describes how a process happens: which molecules interact, in what order, and with what consequence. Discovering a mechanism at doctoral and postdoctoral level means proposing a specific account and testing it against alternatives. Ioannidis (2005) described how the pursuit of a single attractive account can lead to findings that do not hold, and the discipline of the field lies in testing each step rather than accepting a plausible story.

Perturbation is the central experimental tool. A component is removed, added or altered, and the consequence for the process is measured. If the mechanism requires the component, its removal should abolish the process, and if the component is sufficient, its addition should restore or produce it. Cumming (2014) argues that the size of each effect should be reported with its interval, since the strength of a mechanistic claim depends on how precisely the consequence is measured.

Reconstitution tests sufficiency by rebuilding the process from purified components. A process that occurs when the defined components are combined in a test tube is strong evidence that those components are sufficient, though it does not show that the same components act in the same way inside a cell. Peng (2011) argued that the computational and analytical steps of such work should be reproducible, and the quantitative data should be deposited with the protocols.

Kinetic and thermodynamic measurements distinguish between competing mechanisms that predict different rates or affinities. A mechanism is strengthened when its quantitative predictions match measurements, and weakened when they do not. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the mechanism should be reported with the degree of confidence each step deserves, and the distinction between a well-tested step and a conjecture should be made explicit. Replication by independent laboratories is the final test, and the mechanism is established only when others confirm it.
