A translational programme is reported by its stages, and each stage must be visible to a reader who wishes to judge whether the finding has earned the next step. The discovery and the mechanism should be stated, together with the go and no-go criteria registered before each stage. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that a programme whose criteria are hidden cannot be evaluated, and the criteria are the basis of every decision the programme made.

The relevance evidence for the models used, the early clinical results with their doses, safety and uncertainty, and the biomarker validation with its context of use should each be reported. A reader should be able to see what each stage established and how far its conclusions reach. Ioannidis (2005) described how a translational claim that omits the evidence for its intermediate steps is impossible to assess, and the stage-by-stage account makes the chain visible.

Any stage that did not proceed must be reported with its failure analysis, since the failure is part of what the programme taught. Cumming (2014) argues that the magnitude of each failed effect should be reported with its interval, so that the reader can judge whether the failure was decisive or uncertain.

The collaboration structure and its safeguards, and the regulatory and ethical pathway followed, should be described. Peng (2011) argued that the data and protocols should be deposited with identifiers, so that the stages can be checked and the programme can be reproduced by others.

The report should close with an honest statement of the populations and conditions to which the translated finding applies, and of the evidence that remains to be obtained. A translational finding is a claim about a particular condition, and the report must state its scope and the questions it leaves open, so that its users do not take it beyond the evidence.
