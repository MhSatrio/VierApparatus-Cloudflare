An animal model is useful for translation only if it resembles the human condition in the ways that matter. Face validity concerns resemblance of the disease, construct validity concerns resemblance of the mechanism, and predictive validity concerns whether the model responds to treatments in the way people do. Predictive validity is the most demanding and the most informative, and it is tested by applying treatments with known human outcomes to the model and comparing the responses. Ioannidis (2005) described how models that reproduce a disease appearance may still fail to predict treatment response, and the predictive test is what establishes relevance.

The concordance between the model's response and the human response is quantified, and the agreement should be reported with its interval. A model that agrees with humans for some treatments and disagrees for others reveals where its relevance ends. Cumming (2014) argues that the uncertainty of the concordance depends on the number of treatments tested, and a small number of comparisons gives a wide interval.

Species and strain differences can alter the response to a treatment, so that a result in one strain may not appear in another or in people. The strain and species used must be reported, and the difference from the human condition stated. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the models used should be described fully, since the result depends on the model as much as on the treatment.

Cell culture and organoids offer human-derived systems that avoid some species differences, but they lack the systemic context of the body, including the immune system, the circulation and the metabolism of the whole organism. Their results must be interpreted with this limitation in mind, and the evidence needed before they inform a human decision is stronger than that required for a basic mechanism. Peng (2011) argued that the procedures for generating and culturing these systems must be documented so that the results can be reproduced.

The use of multiple models with complementary strengths gives a more complete picture of relevance than any one model. The report should present the relevance evidence and the discordances between model and human response, since the discordances often teach the most about the limits of the model and the mechanism.
