A go criterion turns a scientific question into a decision rule, and it states what the next stage requires. The criterion is usually an effect size that must be excluded as too small, or an effect that must be shown with a stated confidence. Ioannidis (2005) described how a programme without a clear threshold can proceed on the strength of any favourable result, however small, and the threshold prevents this.

The threshold should be chosen by what effect would justify the cost and the risk of the next stage, and it should be expressed with an interval. A requirement that the lower bound of the interval exceed a minimal clinically important effect is more demanding and more informative than a requirement that the point estimate be positive. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval conveys the uncertainty that the decision must take into account, and the criterion should be framed in its terms.

Stopping criteria state the circumstances in which the programme ends, such as a safety signal that exceeds a limit or an effect that falls below the threshold with adequate precision. A stopping criterion that is defined in advance prevents the continuation of a failing programme by reinterpretation, and it protects the participants and the resources involved.

The criteria should be pre-registered with a date, so that they cannot be adjusted after the results are known. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that pre-registration is the basis of the credibility of a decision rule, since a criterion that moves to fit the data is no criterion at all. Any change to the criteria must be documented with its reason and reported, and the original criteria must remain visible.

The decision record states the outcome against each criterion, whether the programme proceeded, stopped or was modified, and the evidence on which the decision was based. Peng (2011) argued that the record of decisions is part of the reproducibility of the programme, and it allows others to judge whether the decisions followed from the evidence.
