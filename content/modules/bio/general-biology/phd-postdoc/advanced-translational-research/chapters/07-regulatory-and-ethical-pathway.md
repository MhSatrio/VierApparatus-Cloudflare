A translational product must pass through a regulatory and ethical pathway before it reaches patients or the public, and the pathway exists to protect those who will bear its risks. The preclinical package supports the first human use, and the clinical programme establishes safety and efficacy in stages, each of which must provide the evidence needed for the next. Ioannidis (2005) described how a programme that advances without adequate evidence at each stage exposes people to risk that the evidence cannot justify, and the staged evidence requirement is the protection against this.

The registration and complete reporting of trials are ethical requirements, since participants accept risk on the understanding that the results will contribute to knowledge. A trial whose results are withheld has failed those who took part, and a regulator that approves a product on incomplete evidence has been misled. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the registration of trials before they begin and the reporting of their results after they end are basic conditions of a trustworthy evidence base.

Ethical review of protocols and informed consent are required for each trial, and they must be sufficient for the participants to understand the risks. Consent that is technically complete but incomprehensible does not meet the standard, and the review committee must consider the comprehensibility of the consent as well as its completeness. Cumming (2014) argues that the risks should be described with their uncertainty, since a participant who understands the interval of a risk is better placed to decide.

Pharmacovigilance monitors safety after approval, because rare or delayed harms may appear only in wide use. The reporting of adverse events, their assessment and the action taken are part of the ongoing responsibility of the developer and the regulator. Post-approval evidence confirms that the benefit observed in trials holds in routine care, and where it does not, the regulatory decision should be revisited. Peng (2011) argued that the data behind these decisions must be available for scrutiny, and the regulatory decision and its uncertainty should be communicated clearly to clinicians and the public.

The pathway is a sequence of decisions, each of which should be documented and justified by the evidence available at the time. The record of the pathway allows later readers to see why a product was approved or withdrawn, and it is part of the accountability that protects the public.
