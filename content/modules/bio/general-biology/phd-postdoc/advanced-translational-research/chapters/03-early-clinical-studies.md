First-in-human studies give a new intervention to people for the first time, and their purpose is to establish a safe dose and to observe the earliest signals of effect. The starting dose is derived from the toxicology in animals, with safety margins that account for the difference between species, and the escalation proceeds in cohorts while the participants are monitored for toxicity. The design must protect participants at every step, since the risk is greatest when the dose is least known.

Dose escalation designs increase the dose in cohorts according to a rule, and the rule determines how many participants are exposed to each level. Common rules stop escalation when a dose-limiting toxicity is observed in a stated proportion of a cohort, and the rule must be pre-specified. Ioannidis (2005) described how the rules for proceeding can be bent when the pressure to advance a programme is strong, and the pre-specified rule is the safeguard.

Pharmacokinetic sampling characterises the exposure of the body to the drug, and pharmacodynamic markers show whether the mechanism is engaged in people. A mechanism that is engaged at a dose that is safe is evidence of a rational dose, and the relation between exposure and marker should be estimated with its interval. Cumming (2014) argues that the variability between participants should be reported alongside the typical exposure, since it shapes the dose that works for each person.

The sample sizes of early studies are small, so every estimate has a wide interval, and early efficacy signals must be interpreted with this uncertainty in view. A promising signal in a few participants may vanish in a larger study, and the signal cannot establish benefit. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the early results, including those that did not show a signal, must be reported in full.

Safety monitoring requires stopping rules and independent oversight of the accumulating data, and the events must be recorded and assessed for their relation to the intervention. The report should give the doses, the safety events, the early efficacy signals and the limitations of the size, each with its uncertainty. Peng (2011) argued that the data and the procedures must be documented so that the early results can be checked.
