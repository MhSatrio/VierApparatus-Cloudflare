Most translational programmes fail, and the failures are a rich source of knowledge if they are analysed. A failure can be a failure of the hypothesis, in which the mechanism was wrong, or a failure of the test, in which the mechanism may have been right but the model, the dose, the endpoint or the patient selection prevented it from showing. Ioannidis (2005) described how the distinction is often blurred, and an analysis that treats every failure as a refutation of the mechanism loses much of what the failure could teach.

Failures are classified by stage and by cause. Common causes include irrelevant models, inadequate doses, wrong endpoints, incorrect mechanisms, poor selection of patients and insufficient statistical power. Each cause implies a different lesson, and the classification directs the revision of the programme and of the field's practice. Cumming (2014) argues that the magnitude of each failed effect should be reported with its interval, since the size of the failure determines what can be learned from it.

A negative result may be a true negative or a false negative, and the distinction depends on the power of the study. A study with too few participants may fail to detect a real effect, and its negative result says little about the effect. The interval of the estimate shows whether the effect could be large enough to matter, and if the interval includes effects of clinical importance, the negative result is inconclusive. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the interval must be reported with every negative result, so that the reader can tell an informative negative from an uninformative one.

The reporting of failures and of their causes allows the field to learn from them, and it is discouraged by a culture that rewards only success. Failures that are not reported are repeated by others, and the cost of this repetition is borne by patients and by the funders. Peng (2011) argued that the complete record of a research programme, including its failures, is necessary for the field to progress.

Failure analysis should revise the mechanism and the programme, and the revised hypothesis should be tested in turn. The revision must be honest, and it should not be used to rescue a failed mechanism by changing the hypothesis after the result is known. Ioannidis (2005) described the danger of post hoc rescue, and the revision must be pre-specified as a new hypothesis, not disguised as an explanation of the old one.
