Translational research carries a discovery across the gap between the laboratory and the clinic or the field, and most discoveries fail somewhere along the way. The failures are costly, and they are often the result of decisions made without clear criteria for proceeding. A translational programme should therefore define, before each stage begins, the evidence required to move forward and the evidence that would stop the programme. Ioannidis (2005) described how the pressure to advance a promising finding can override the scepticism that the evidence requires, and explicit criteria protect against that pressure.

Animal models and human-derived systems are used to test a mechanism before it is applied to people. Their results are trusted only to the extent that the model reflects the human condition, and the relevance of the model must be established by evidence rather than assumed. Cumming (2014) argues that the agreement between the model and the human response should be quantified with its interval, since a model that agrees in one endpoint may disagree in another.

First-in-human and early clinical studies establish the dose, the safety and the early signals of benefit. Their small size gives wide intervals, and their conclusions must be provisional. Safety monitoring must be built in from the start, and the stopping rules for harm must be stated. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the results of these early studies should be reported in full, including those that stopped the programme, since they are the evidence on which later stages depend.

Biomarkers link the mechanism to a measurable endpoint, and their validation is a translational step in its own right. A biomarker that tracks the mechanism in the laboratory may not track it in patients, and its performance must be shown in the population of interest. Peng (2011) argued that the assays and the procedures must be documented so that the biomarker can be measured by others.

When translation fails, the failure analysis asks why, and it is one of the most useful activities in the field. The analysis should examine whether the model was relevant, whether the dose was adequate, whether the endpoint was appropriate and whether the mechanism was correct. Ioannidis (2005) emphasised that the lessons of failure are lost when failures are not reported, and the failure analysis should be published.

Collaboration among academic, clinical and industrial partners brings resources and expertise, and it brings conflicts of interest that must be declared and managed. The report of each translational stage should give its evidence, its criteria, its decision and its uncertainty, so that the path from discovery to application can be followed.
