Artificial intelligence can accelerate molecular research, but its use carries responsibilities that the speed of the methods can obscure. A model is only as representative as the data on which it was trained, and many organisms, populations and conditions are poorly represented in the biological record. A model that performs well on well-studied organisms may fail on others, and a claim of general applicability must be tested across the groups it is intended to serve. Ioannidis (2005) described how the composition of the literature shapes what is believed, and models trained on that literature inherit its gaps.

Models that predict or design biological molecules carry dual-use risks, since the same capability that designs a therapeutic molecule could in principle design a harmful one. The investigators should assess these risks, consider the information that a publication would release and involve those with expertise in biosafety and security in the decision about what to share. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that openness is a value, and it must be weighed against these risks with explicit reasoning rather than dismissed.

Automated design and experimental decisions require human oversight. A model that proposes experiments should not carry them out without review, and the criteria by which its proposals are accepted or rejected should be stated. Cumming (2014) argues that the uncertainty of each proposal should be shown to the people who decide, so that they do not treat a point estimate as a certainty.

Transparency about the role of AI is necessary in the generation of results and in the preparation of publications. The methods, the models, the data and the degree of automation should be described, so that readers can judge the contribution of each. Peng (2011) argued that the means to check a computational result must be available, and this principle applies to the use of AI as to any other method.

Claims made from models must be validated before they inform clinical or environmental decisions, and the responsibility of researchers extends to the consequences of their claims. Credit must be attributed fairly between the investigators, the developers of the models and the producers of the data on which they rest. The responsibility also includes reporting failures and correcting claims that the evidence does not support, since a model whose errors are hidden can do more harm than one whose limits are known.
