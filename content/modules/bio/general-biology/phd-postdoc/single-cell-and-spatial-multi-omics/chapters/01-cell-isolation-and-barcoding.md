Single-cell analysis begins with the separation of cells from the tissue, and this step can change the cells it is meant to measure. Enzymatic dissociation and mechanical stress activate stress responses that alter gene expression, and the state measured may be partly the state of the dissociation. Ioannidis (2005) described how a measurement can reflect the procedure as much as the phenomenon, and the dissociation protocol must be controlled and reported.

Nuclei are an alternative to whole cells, and they are useful for tissues that are difficult to dissociate, such as neurons or frozen samples. Nuclear measurements capture a smaller set of transcripts than whole-cell measurements, since cytoplasmic RNA is absent, and the difference must be taken into account when comparing the two. The choice of material should be justified by the question and by the limits it imposes.

Each cell or nucleus is captured with a unique barcode, so that all of its molecules can be attributed to it. Droplets or wells are the usual capture systems, and the efficiency of capture determines how many cells are recovered from the number loaded. Cumming (2014) argues that the capture efficiency should be reported with its interval, since it varies between runs and affects the precision of every later estimate.

Two cells captured in the same droplet produce a doublet, which appears as a cell with a mixed profile. The doublet rate rises with the number of cells loaded, and doublets must be estimated and removed, since they can create cell types that do not exist. The estimate of the doublet rate should be reported with its uncertainty, and the method used to detect doublets should be stated. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that such quality metrics should be reported so that others can judge the data.
