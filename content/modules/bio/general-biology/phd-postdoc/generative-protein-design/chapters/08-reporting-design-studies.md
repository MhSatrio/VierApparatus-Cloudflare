A protein design study is evaluated by the completeness of its report, since the success of a method is meaningless without the record of what it was applied to and what it produced. The targets and the backbone sampling must be described, along with the generation settings and the diversity of the designs. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the design of the study determines what can be concluded from it, and the report must make that design visible.

The computational filters should be reported with their thresholds, their calibration and the fraction of designs that passed each. A filter whose threshold was chosen after the designs were examined invites doubt, and the thresholds should be stated as fixed in advance. Cumming (2014) argues that each fraction should be reported with its interval, since the number of designs determines how precisely it is known.

The complete cascade of experimental outcomes must be reported, with every design counted in the denominator of each stage. The success rates should be given as proportions with binomial intervals, and the functional measurements with their uncertainty. Ioannidis (2005) described how the reporting of only the successful outcomes produces a literature that overstates what methods can achieve, and the complete cascade protects against this.

The sequences, the structures and the raw data should be deposited with identifiers, so that other groups can check the designs and compare their own methods against them. Peng (2011) argued that the availability of the means of verification is what makes a computational claim credible, and the deposition is that means.

The biosecurity assessment and the safeguards should be stated, and the report should close with an honest account of the designs that did not work and of the limits of the method. A method is best understood through its failures as much as its successes, and a report that states both gives the community the information it needs to improve the method.
