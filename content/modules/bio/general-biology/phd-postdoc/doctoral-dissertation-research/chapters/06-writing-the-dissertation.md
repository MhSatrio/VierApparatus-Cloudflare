A dissertation is the record that the examiners and later readers will judge, and it must be coherent as well as complete. The structure should move from the question and its context through each study to an integrated discussion, so that the reader can follow the argument from its starting point to its conclusions. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the clarity of a report is part of its honesty, since a reader who cannot follow the argument cannot judge its claims.

Each study should be reported with its design, its analysis, its results and the interval of each estimate, in a form that allows its evaluation. The reader should be able to see what was done and what was found without needing the candidate's account of it. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval should appear wherever an estimate is reported, since the examiners must judge the precision as well as the direction of each finding.

Negative and inconclusive results must be included, with their interpretation. A result that does not reach significance is not an absence of effect, and the dissertation must state what the interval shows and what it does not. Ioannidis (2005) described how omitted negative results distort the record, and a dissertation that includes them is more reliable and more useful to the field.

Where work was joint, the candidate's contribution must be stated clearly, so that the examiners can see which parts of the work the candidate performed. The discussion should state the uncertainty of each conclusion and the limits of the work, and it should not claim more than the evidence supports. Peng (2011) argued that the reproducibility of the work depends on the record of who did what, and the statement of contribution provides it.

The references should trace each claim to its source, and the document should be revised against the reporting standards for its designs. The final revision is the candidate's last responsibility, and a dissertation that is carefully checked against its evidence is the strongest preparation for the defence.
