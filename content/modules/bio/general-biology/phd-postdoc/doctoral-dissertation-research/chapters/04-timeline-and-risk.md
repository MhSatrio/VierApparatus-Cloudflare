A doctorate has a fixed end, and the timeline must be planned so that the work can be completed within it. The work should be decomposed into studies, each with an estimated duration that includes the time needed to learn the methods and to analyse the data, not only to collect them. Ioannidis (2005) described how the optimism of a plan often underestimates the time that experiments take, and the estimates should be realistic.

The critical path is the sequence of dependent studies that determines the earliest submission date, and it is the most important part of the plan. A study that depends on the result of another cannot begin until that result is known, so delays on the critical path delay the whole. The candidate should identify the critical path and protect it.

Experiments fail, and the timeline must include contingency for failure and for delay. A contingency may be a second method for the same measurement, a smaller pilot that tests feasibility early, or an alternative study that can proceed in parallel. Cumming (2014) argues that the precision of each study should be considered when planning the contingency, since a study that is too small to be informative wastes its allotted time.

Milestones allow progress to be reviewed with the supervisor, and each should be verifiable, such as the completion of a validated method or the collection of a stated number of observations. The reviews provide an opportunity to change the plan before the time is lost. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the record of progress against the plan is part of the transparency of the work, and it should be kept.

The candidate and the supervisor should agree in advance on the circumstances in which the direction should be changed, since a direction that is unproductive may be persisted with for too long. Peng (2011) argued that the plan and its revisions should be recorded so that the reasons for each change are visible to the committee.
