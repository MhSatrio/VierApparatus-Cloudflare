A dissertation depends on methods that the candidate can apply reliably, and the candidate must acquire both the techniques and the judgement to use them. The choice of methods should be guided by whether they can answer the question with the required precision, not by familiarity alone. A method that is precise in principle may be unreliable in the hands of a beginner, and the candidate should not assume competence that has not been demonstrated.

Before a method is used to answer the dissertation question, the candidate should validate it in their own hands, using positive and negative controls. A positive control shows that the method can detect a known effect, and a negative control shows that it does not produce spurious signals. Ioannidis (2005) described how methods that have not been validated in the laboratory where they are used produce unreliable data, and the candidate's validation is the protection against this.

Statistical training allows the candidate to design, analyse and interpret their own studies. The candidate should be able to justify a sample size, to fit the model that matches the design, to report the interval of each estimate and to recognise pseudoreplication. Cumming (2014) argues that the interpretation of intervals is the most important statistical skill for an independent researcher, and it should be learned thoroughly.

Computational skills make the analysis reproducible, and the candidate should be able to script the analysis, record the software environment and keep the code under version control. Peng (2011) argued that a computational result is reproducible only when these practices are followed, and the dissertation's examiners should be able to rerun the analysis from the deposited code and data.

Consultation with specialists is appropriate for methods the candidate does not master, and the candidate should say so in the dissertation. The methods used, the training behind them and the validation performed should be stated honestly, since the examiners must be able to judge whether the candidate's competence supports the claims made. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the transparency of methods extends to the expertise of the investigator, and it is part of the integrity of the work.
