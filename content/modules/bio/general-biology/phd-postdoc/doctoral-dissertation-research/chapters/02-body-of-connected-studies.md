A dissertation that consists of connected studies is more convincing than one that consists of unconnected experiments, because each study can test a prediction derived from the one before. The sequence should be planned so that the first study establishes the system and the basic effect, the second tests its mechanism or generality, and the third examines the boundaries of the finding. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that a sequence of pre-specified tests is the strongest form of evidence a single investigator can produce.

The analysis of each study must be pre-specified before its data are collected. The primary outcome, the model and the exclusion rules should be recorded in advance, and any departure from them should be reported with its reason. Ioannidis (2005) described how the flexibility of analysis can make a body of work appear conclusive when it is not, and pre-specification limits that flexibility.

A key finding should be replicated within the dissertation, so that its robustness is established before it is built upon. Replication within the work is not a repetition of effort but a test of the reliability of the result, and a replication that fails must be investigated. Cumming (2014) argues that the agreement between the original and the replication should be judged by their intervals, and a difference between them is informative about the boundaries of the effect.

The sample size of each study should be planned for a stated precision, so that the estimates will be informative whatever their value. An underpowered study yields an estimate too imprecise to guide the next study, and it wastes the candidate's time and the participants' contributions. Peng (2011) argued that the plan for each study should be recorded so that its results can be interpreted against it.

Unexpected or null results must be handled honestly. A study whose result contradicts the expectation is not a failure to be concealed, and it may point to the most interesting finding of the dissertation. The candidate should integrate the studies into a single argument, state the uncertainty of the combined conclusion, and report every study, including those that do not fit the final narrative. A dissertation that omits its inconvenient results misrepresents the work, and the examiners are entitled to see all of it.
