A doctoral dissertation is a body of original research presented as evidence of the candidate's capacity to do independent science. Its question must be bounded enough to be answered within the period of a doctorate and original enough to contribute to the field. A question that is too broad will not be answered, and one that is too narrow will not matter. Ioannidis (2005) described how the pressure to produce a convincing thesis can encourage overstated conclusions, and the dissertation should be judged by the rigour of its evidence rather than by the size of its claims.

A dissertation is usually a set of connected studies rather than a single experiment, and each study should be planned with its analysis specified in advance. The studies should build on one another, so that the later ones test predictions made from the earlier ones. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that pre-specification protects the candidate from the flexibility that makes a body of work appear more conclusive than it is, and it should be adopted from the start.

The candidate must develop the methods and the expertise that the research requires, and the training is part of the purpose of the doctorate. Cumming (2014) argues that a candidate who learns to report estimates with their intervals will be able to judge their own evidence, and this skill is among the most valuable the doctorate can give.

The timeline must allow for the failure of experiments, which is common in research. A project that depends on a single experiment that is not repeated is fragile, and the candidate should plan contingencies. The supervisor and the committee should review progress, and the candidate should be able to explain the rationale for each decision. Peng (2011) argued that the records of the work should be kept so that the dissertation can be reproduced by its examiners.

The dissertation must be written as a coherent record of the work, including its negative results, and the candidate must defend each claim against its evidence. The defence is the final test of whether the candidate understands the work they have done, and it is the occasion on which the uncertainty of each claim must be stated plainly. The chapters that follow examine the question, the body of work, the methods and expertise, the timeline, the ethics and governance, the writing and the defence.
