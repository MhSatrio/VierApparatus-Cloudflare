Precision medicine aims to match each patient to the prevention, diagnosis or treatment most likely to help them, using their molecular and clinical characteristics. The promise depends on identifying biomarkers that predict who will benefit, and on demonstrating that the benefit differs between biomarker groups. Ioannidis (2005) described how the search for subgroups that respond differently has produced many findings that did not replicate, and the rigour of the evaluation determines whether a personalised strategy is justified.

A prognostic biomarker predicts the outcome regardless of treatment, while a predictive biomarker predicts the response to a particular treatment. The distinction matters, because a marker may be prognostic without being predictive, and a therapy assigned on the basis of prognosis alone may offer no advantage over standard care. The predictive role is established by a statistical interaction between the treatment and the biomarker, and Cumming (2014) argues that the size of this interaction should be reported with its interval.

Trial designs for precision medicine include biomarker-stratified designs, in which patients are randomised within biomarker groups, and basket designs, in which a therapy is tested across diseases that share a molecular feature. Each design answers a different question, and each must be analysed with the interaction and the heterogeneity of effect in view. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the pre-specification of the biomarker groups and the analysis is essential, since post hoc subgroups are a common source of spurious findings.

Decision thresholds determine which patients are assigned a therapy, and the cost of misclassification in each direction must be considered. A patient who is wrongly denied a beneficial therapy bears a different cost from one who receives an ineffective therapy with its harms. The threshold should be chosen with these costs in mind, and its performance should be reported with its uncertainty. Peng (2011) argued that the rule and the data must be available so that the classification can be reproduced.

The clinical and economic value of a personalised strategy must be compared with standard care, since a biomarker that identifies responders may still add little if the standard approach already works for most patients. The report should give the biomarkers, the interactions, the thresholds and the outcomes, each with its uncertainty, and Ioannidis (2005) emphasised that the claimed benefit should be shown in the population in which the biomarker will be used.
