A precision medicine strategy costs money, and its value must be judged against what it costs. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio divides the additional cost of a strategy by the additional health benefit it provides compared with standard care, and it expresses the price of each unit of benefit. The ratio is an estimate that depends on the costs, the benefits and the time horizon, and each of these is uncertain. Cumming (2014) argues that the uncertainty of the ratio must be shown, since a point estimate of a ratio can conceal a very wide range.

The uncertainty is represented by the cost-effectiveness acceptability curve, which shows the probability that the strategy is cost-effective at different willingness-to-pay thresholds. The curve makes explicit that the decision depends on the threshold chosen, and it allows decision makers to see how the conclusion changes with their valuation of health. Ioannidis (2005) described how the presentation of a single favourable number can hide the uncertainty that determines whether a decision is justified, and the curve is the remedy.

The budget impact on a health system is the total spending it would require, and it differs from the cost-effectiveness because a strategy can be cost-effective per patient yet unaffordable overall. The budget impact must be estimated from the number of patients eligible for testing, and it should be reported with its assumptions about uptake.

Equity of access is a central concern, since a test that is available only in well-resourced centres or to well-insured patients widens existing inequalities. The distribution of access across socioeconomic groups and geographies should be examined, and the equity consequences should be stated. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the assumptions about access are part of the evaluation, and they should be reported openly.

Groups that the test serves poorly, for example because the biomarker was developed in a population that does not include them, may gain little or may be harmed by misclassification. The evaluation should report the performance by group, and the consequences of poor performance for each group. Peng (2011) argued that the cost and outcome data and the model must be documented so that the evaluation can be reproduced, and the report should give the costs, the benefits and their uncertainty together with the assumptions behind them.
