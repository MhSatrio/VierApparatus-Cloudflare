A precision medicine study makes a claim about a particular group of patients and a particular clinical decision, and its report must allow a reader to see whether the claim is supported. The biomarker must be defined and its measurement described, and the subgroups must be pre-specified with the number examined reported. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the definition of the population and the decision is the basis of the claim, and the report should make both explicit.

The interaction between the treatment and the biomarker must be reported with its interval, and the trial design and its decision rules must be described. The threshold used to assign therapy should be reported with its operating characteristics in an independent cohort. Cumming (2014) argues that each estimate should be shown with the uncertainty that its sample supports, and a threshold reported without its validation invites overconfidence.

The analytical validity of the companion diagnostic, including its concordance with the routine assay, must be reported. Observational evidence should be accompanied by its confounding controls and sensitivity analyses. Ioannidis (2005) described how observational associations are often confounded, and the report must show how the confounding was addressed.

The economic evaluation should be reported with its uncertainty, its assumptions and its equity consequences. Peng (2011) argued that the data and code behind each estimate must be available so that the evaluation can be repeated, and the deposition of these materials with identifiers makes the study checkable.

The report should close by stating the populations and decisions to which the findings apply and those to which they do not. A precision medicine finding is valid only for the patients and the choices it was tested on, and a clear statement of that scope protects patients from the misapplication of a biomarker beyond its evidence.
