A contribution does not end at its publication. It is tested, replicated, extended and sometimes corrected by others, and the investigator who published it has a continuing responsibility to the record. The stewardship of a contribution is the practice of responding to these tests honestly, and it is the final measure of the investigator's integrity. Ioannidis (2005) described how the defence of a published result against contrary evidence has sustained many false findings, and the investigator must resist this temptation.

A failure to replicate should prompt an honest reassessment of the evidence, including the conditions of the original and the replication and the precision of both estimates. The investigator should consider whether the original result was an artefact, a chance finding or an effect that depends on conditions, and should report that assessment. Cumming (2014) argues that the intervals of the original and the replication should be compared directly, since the question is whether they are consistent with a common effect.

Errors discovered in the original work must be corrected promptly, and the correction should state what was wrong and what the correct result is. A correction is a sign of a self-correcting science, and the investigator who makes one protects the field and their own reputation. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the readiness to correct is a basic obligation of the researcher.

New evidence may qualify a contribution, showing that it holds under some conditions and not others, or that its magnitude is smaller than first reported. The record must be updated, and the investigator should report the qualification with the same care as the original claim. Peng (2011) argued that the record of a contribution, including its revisions, should be kept so that its history can be followed.

The investigator should continue to provide the materials and data that support the tests of others, and should recognise the contributions of those who replicate and extend the work. The willingness to revise one's own account as the evidence accumulates is the mark of a scientist who serves the truth rather than the reputation of a result, and it is the standard against which an original contribution is ultimately judged.
