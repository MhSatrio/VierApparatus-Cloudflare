A surprising finding is not necessarily a contribution. Surprise is a reaction to an unexpected result, and many surprising results prove to be artefacts, errors or effects too small to matter. A genuine contribution changes what the field knows or what it can do, and the researcher must be able to say what would be different after the result is accepted. Ioannidis (2005) described how the most surprising early findings are frequently the least reliable, since surprise attracts attention before the result has been tested.

The test of a contribution is whether it resolves an important question or enables a capability that did not previously exist. A result that answers a question the field has long wished to answer, or that makes possible an experiment or an application that was impossible, is a genuine advance. Cumming (2014) argues that the importance of a result is best stated through the size of the effect and the precision with which it is known, since these determine whether the answer matters.

A contribution must be separated from an artefact of the method or of the data. A striking result may arise from a miscalibrated instrument, a batch effect, a confounder or an analysis error, and the researcher must eliminate these explanations before claiming the result. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the researcher should actively seek the reasons the result might be wrong, rather than only the reasons it might be right.

The contribution must be likely to survive the replication that its importance will attract, since an important result is tested by many groups. A result that depends on a single lot of reagent, a single population or a single analytic choice is unlikely to survive, and the researcher should test its robustness before claiming it. Peng (2011) argued that the robustness of a result is the measure of its reliability, and this robustness should be examined in the researcher's own work.

Contributions may be conceptual, methodological, empirical or a combination, and the researcher should state which kind their work represents. A methodological contribution that enables new measurements can be as valuable as an empirical finding, and a conceptual advance that reframes a field may be more important than any single result. An honest recognition that a striking result is not yet a contribution, because it has not been tested, is itself a mark of scientific judgement.
