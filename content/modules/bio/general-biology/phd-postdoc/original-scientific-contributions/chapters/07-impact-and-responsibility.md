An original contribution can change the world beyond the field that produced it, through applications in medicine, agriculture, the environment and technology. The investigator who made it bears a responsibility for how it is used, and this responsibility begins with an honest assessment of its uses and its risks. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that openness is a value of science, and it must be weighed against the risks that a contribution may carry, with the reasoning made explicit.

A contribution may enable harm as well as benefit, and the dual-use risk must be assessed before the contribution is published in full. The investigator should consider whether the methods could be misused and should involve those with expertise in security and safety in the decision about what to publish. Ioannidis (2005) described how the consequences of a result depend on its use, and the responsibility for foreseeable misuse should be taken seriously.

The contribution must be communicated to the public and to policy makers in terms that convey its uncertainty and its limits. A striking result presented without its interval invites decisions the evidence does not support, and the investigator should take care that their communication matches the strength of the evidence. Cumming (2014) argues that an honest communication of uncertainty is more useful to decision makers than a confident simplification.

Claims of impact should be supported by the evidence, and the investigator should avoid claims that the contribution will transform a field or cure a disease unless the evidence supports them. Overstated claims damage the credibility of the research and of the field, and they raise expectations that may not be met. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the honest statement of impact protects the reputation of the work.

The investigator should attend to who benefits from the contribution and who bears its risks, since the distribution of benefits and harms is an ethical question. A contribution that helps some groups while exposing others to harm requires consideration, and the investigator should engage with the societal questions that it raises. Peng (2011) argued that the reproducibility of the contribution is the basis on which others can judge its benefits and risks, and the investigator should make that judgement possible.
