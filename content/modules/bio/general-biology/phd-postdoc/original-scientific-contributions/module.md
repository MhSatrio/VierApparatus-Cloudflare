An original scientific contribution is a result, a method or a concept that changes what the field can do or understand. It is the mark of a mature researcher, and it is also the claim most likely to be overstated, because the excitement of novelty tempts the investigator to present the contribution as larger than its evidence allows. Ioannidis (2005) described how the most striking first results are often the least reliable, and the researcher must establish the contribution with the same rigour whatever its appeal.

The evidence that establishes a contribution must be presented with its uncertainty and its replication. A striking effect with a wide interval is a hypothesis, and it becomes a contribution only when its precision is adequate and it has been reproduced. Cumming (2014) argues that the interval of the main estimate should be the centre of the claim, since it tells the field how large the contribution is and how sure it can be.

The contribution must be placed against the existing literature, so that its novelty and its relation to prior work are clear. A contribution that repeats a known result under another name is not original, and one that ignores the prior evidence will be rejected by reviewers who know it. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the honest placing of a contribution in its context is part of the transparency of science.

The contribution must be communicated in forms that allow others to test, replicate and build on it. The methods, the data and the code should be deposited with identifiers, and the description should be complete enough for an independent group to repeat the key result. Peng (2011) argued that a contribution that cannot be reproduced is not yet a contribution to the field, and its communication must make reproduction possible.

The investigator should anticipate the reception of the contribution, including the objections that the field will raise, and should prepare answers to them. The contribution will be tested by others, and its stewardship continues over time as it is replicated, extended or corrected. The chapters that follow examine the identification of the contribution, its evidence, its placement, its communication, the anticipation of objections and its stewardship.
