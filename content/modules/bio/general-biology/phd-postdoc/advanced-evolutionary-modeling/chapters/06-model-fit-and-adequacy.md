A model that has been fitted to data must also be checked for adequacy, since a fitted model can still be a poor description of the process that generated the data. The posterior predictive check simulates data from the fitted model and compares summary statistics of the simulations with those of the observed data. If the observed statistic lies in the tail of the simulated distribution, the model fails to reproduce that feature. Ioannidis (2005) described how a model that is never tested for adequacy can be accepted on the strength of its parameter estimates alone, and the check guards against this.

The summary statistics chosen should capture the features that the model is meant to reproduce, such as the frequency spectrum of variants, the linkage disequilibrium between sites or the branching pattern of the tree. A statistic that the model is designed to fit will always appear to be reproduced, so the check should use statistics that were not used in fitting. Cumming (2014) argues that the discrepancy between the observed and simulated statistics should be reported with its interval.

Misfit may be revealed by a statistic that the model cannot reproduce, and this indicates that the model is missing a process. The misfit should be investigated, since it may point to a missing demographic event, a missing form of selection or a wrong assumption about the sampling. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that misfit should be reported rather than hidden, since it informs the next model.

Competing models are compared by their fit and their complexity, using criteria that penalise the number of parameters. A model with a better fit but many more parameters may not be preferred, and the comparison should be made with a stated criterion. A model that fits well is not necessarily the true model, since the data may be unable to distinguish it from alternatives. Peng (2011) argued that the procedure for comparing models must be reproducible, and the checks and comparisons should be reported together.
