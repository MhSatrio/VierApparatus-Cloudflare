Experimental evolution allows a model to be tested against evolution that is observed directly in the laboratory. Replicate populations are maintained under defined conditions, and their genetic composition and phenotype are tracked over time. The replicates are the essential feature of the design: a single population that changes in one direction is an anecdote, while several that change in parallel under the same conditions provide evidence that selection is driving the change. Ioannidis (2005) described how the absence of replication can leave a striking result unsupported, and the replicate structure is the protection against this.

Time series of allele frequencies and phenotypes allow the direction and the rate of change to be estimated, and the estimates carry intervals that depend on the number of replicates and generations. Cumming (2014) argues that the rate should be reported with its interval, since the change in a few generations may be small relative to the noise of sampling.

Parallel evolution across replicates supports a model of selection, since the same loci or phenotypes changing in each replicate indicate a common cause. The loci that changed can be identified by sequencing the evolved populations, and the sequencing should be designed to detect the changes of interest. Munafò et al. (2017) argued that the sequencing data should be deposited so that the identification of loci can be checked.

Control populations are maintained without the selective condition, and they separate selection from drift and from adaptation to the laboratory itself. A change that occurs in the controls is not caused by the condition under study, and the comparison with controls is essential. Peng (2011) argued that the experimental procedures and the data must be recorded so that the experiment can be repeated, and the predicted rate of change from the model should be compared with the observed rate, with the agreement reported with its interval.
