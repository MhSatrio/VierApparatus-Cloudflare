Air enters the body through the nose and mouth, passes through the pharynx and larynx, and descends through the trachea. The trachea divides into two bronchi, one for each lung, and these branch repeatedly into bronchioles. The walls of the airways are supported by cartilage in the larger tubes and by smooth muscle in all of them, and the airway lining is covered with cilia and mucus that trap particles and move them towards the throat.

The branching continues until the terminal bronchioles open into respiratory bronchioles and then into alveolar sacs. Each sac is made of many alveoli, tiny air sacs that are the site of gas exchange. The human lungs contain several hundred million alveoli, which together provide a very large surface for exchange. The wall of each alveolus is one cell thick, and it is closely surrounded by capillaries, so oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse across a very short distance.

The lungs are enclosed by the pleura, a double membrane with a thin layer of fluid between its layers. The fluid allows the lungs to slide against the chest wall during breathing. The thoracic cavity, the diaphragm and the intercostal muscles together form a bellows that changes the volume of the chest. When the diaphragm contracts and the chest expands, air flows in, and when the muscles relax, air flows out.

Darwin (1859) discussed the respiratory organs of animals, which range from gills in aquatic species to lungs in air-breathing vertebrates. The lungs of mammals are a modification of simpler sac-like organs found in other vertebrates, and their branching structure increases the surface for exchange.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell types of the airway and alveolus, and how the epithelium responds to injury and infection. The respiratory system is studied by lung function tests, by imaging of the airways and by microscopy of tissue samples.
