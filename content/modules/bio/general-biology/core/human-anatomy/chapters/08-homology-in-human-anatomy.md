Homology is the similarity of structures that arise from a common ancestor, even when they have different functions. The human arm, the wing of a bat, the flipper of a whale and the foreleg of a horse all contain the same arrangement of bones: a single upper bone, two bones in the forearm, a set of small wrist bones, and fingers or digits. Darwin (1859) argued that this shared pattern is evidence that the animals descended from a common ancestor who had the same arrangement, modified for different uses.

Dobzhansky (1973) argued that the structures of organisms make sense only when understood in the light of their history, and homology is the clearest example. A designer who built each species separately would have had no reason to repeat the same bones in such different limbs, whereas descent with modification predicts exactly such repetition.

Analogous structures are different. The wing of a bird and the wing of an insect have the same function, but they are built from different parts and arose independently. Analogy is the result of convergent evolution, and it does not indicate shared ancestry. A careful anatomist distinguishes the two, because a wrong judgement would mislead any inference about history.

Vestigial structures are features that have lost most or all of their original function. The human coccyx is a remnant of the tail of our vertebrate ancestors, and the wisdom teeth are reduced relative to the teeth of earlier primates. Some humans also have muscles that move the ears, which are inactive in most people. These structures are explained by inheritance from ancestors that used them, and they are difficult to explain as features designed for their current use.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the genes that specify the limbs and the vertebrae are shared among vertebrates, which gives a molecular explanation for the anatomical homologies. Homology is therefore supported by independent evidence from developmental genetics.

The study of homology uses comparative dissection, the examination of embryonic development and the comparison of genes, and the results are judged by their consistency across all three.
