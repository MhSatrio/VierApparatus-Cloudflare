The human heart is a muscular pump with four chambers. The two upper chambers, the atria, receive blood, and the two lower chambers, the ventricles, pump it out. The right side of the heart receives deoxygenated blood from the body and pumps it to the lungs. The left side receives oxygenated blood from the lungs and pumps it to the body. The two sides are separated by a wall, the septum, so that the two circuits remain distinct in the adult.

Four valves control the direction of flow. The tricuspid and mitral valves lie between the atria and ventricles, and the pulmonary and aortic valves lie at the exit of each ventricle. The valves open and close with the pressure changes of the cardiac cycle, and they prevent blood from flowing backward. Heart sounds are produced by the closure of these valves, and their timing is used to diagnose valve disease.

Blood leaves the left ventricle through the aorta, the largest artery, which branches into smaller arteries that deliver blood to the tissues. Arteries have thick, elastic walls that withstand the pressure of each heartbeat. The smallest vessels, capillaries, have thin walls that allow exchange of oxygen, nutrients and wastes with the tissues. Blood returns to the heart through veins, which have thinner walls and valves that prevent backflow, and which carry blood against low pressure.

The systemic circuit carries blood to the body, and the pulmonary circuit carries it to the lungs. The two circuits are connected in series, so the output of one becomes the input of the other, and any imbalance between them is quickly felt.

Comparative anatomy, the method that Darwin (1859) used to argue for descent with modification, shows that the hearts of vertebrates differ in their number of chambers and in the degree of separation between the circuits. The four-chambered heart of mammals and birds completely separates oxygenated and deoxygenated blood, which supports the high metabolic rates of these animals.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the electrical properties of heart muscle and the cells that generate the rhythm, which are discussed further in the Human Physiology module. The heart is studied by imaging, by electrical recording and by measurement of pressure in its chambers and vessels.
