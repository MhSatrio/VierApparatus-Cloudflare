The alimentary canal is a tube that runs from the mouth to the anus, and food passes along it in one direction. It begins with the mouth, where teeth break food and saliva begins the digestion of starch. The food is swallowed through the pharynx and oesophagus into the stomach, a muscular bag that stores food and mixes it with acid and enzymes. The small intestine, about six metres long in an adult, completes most of the digestion and absorbs nutrients. The large intestine absorbs water and salts and stores the remaining material before it is eliminated.

Accessory organs supply secretions to the canal. The liver produces bile, which emulsifies fats, and it processes the nutrients absorbed from the gut. The gallbladder stores bile and releases it into the small intestine. The pancreas secretes digestive enzymes and bicarbonate, which neutralises the acid from the stomach. These organs connect to the canal through ducts, and their secretions are regulated by hormones and nerves.

The wall of the gut is built in four layers. The innermost mucosa lines the lumen and contains the cells that absorb nutrients and secrete mucus. Beneath it lies the submucosa, a layer of connective tissue with blood vessels and nerves. The muscularis consists of smooth muscle that produces the rhythmic contractions of peristalsis, which move food along. The outermost serosa or adventitia holds the gut in place. The arrangement is similar in many vertebrates, and it reflects a shared plan.

The small intestine has a surface greatly increased by folds, villi and microvilli. The area of the lining is many times that of a plain tube, and this is one of the clearest examples of an anatomical structure that exists for the sake of its function.

The digestive organs also vary among animals according to diet. Herbivores often have longer intestines than carnivores, which reflects the difficulty of breaking down plant material and the longer time that digestion requires.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the epithelial cells of the gut and their continual renewal from stem cells, which replace the lining every few days. The digestive system is studied by endoscopy, by imaging and by the analysis of secretions and stool.
