Plants obtain most of their mineral nutrients from soil through their roots. Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium and sulfur are taken up as ions, and iron and other micronutrients are taken up in small amounts. The concentration of these ions in the soil is often lower than in the root cell, so uptake against the gradient requires energy. Plasma membrane proton pumps create an electrochemical gradient, and transporters use it to bring ions in by co-transport.

The root has a large surface of fine root hairs and fine branches that increases contact with the soil. The uptake of nutrients is regulated by the plant's needs, and transporters are induced or repressed according to the nutrient status of the plant. The process is an example of the regulation of membrane transport described in the Cell Biology module.

Many plants live in partnership with fungi. Mycorrhizal fungi extend their hyphae far into the soil and deliver phosphorus and water to the plant in exchange for sugars. The partnership is ancient and widespread, and it has been important in the colonisation of land by plants. Legumes form nodules with nitrogen-fixing bacteria, which convert atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia that the plant can use.

Ball (2008) pointed out that water is central to the movement of ions in soil and to their availability to roots. The dissolution and transport of nutrients depend on the properties of water described in the earlier chapters of this module.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the transporters and the signals that regulate them. The uptake of nutrients is a membrane process that couples energy to the movement of ions, and its control links the plant's internal state to the soil around it.

Nutrient uptake is measured by supplying labelled ions to roots and following their movement, by analysing the mineral content of tissues and by mutants that lack particular transporters.
