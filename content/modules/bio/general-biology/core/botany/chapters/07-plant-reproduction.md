Plants alternate between two generations. The diploid sporophyte produces haploid spores by meiosis, and each spore develops into a haploid gametophyte that produces gametes by mitosis. The gametes fuse to form a new sporophyte. In flowering plants the gametophytes are very small and live inside the flower and seed, while the sporophyte is the plant that is familiar in the field. The alternation is common to all land plants, although the relative size of the two generations varies among groups.

In a flower, the male gametophyte is the pollen grain, which carries the sperm cells, and the female gametophyte is the embryo sac within the ovule. Pollination delivers pollen to the stigma, and the pollen tube grows down the style to the ovule. Two sperm cells are released into the embryo sac, and one fertilises the egg while the other fuses with the central cell. This double fertilisation is characteristic of flowering plants, and it produces an embryo and a nutritive tissue within the seed.

The seed contains the embryo, a store of food and a protective coat. Seeds can remain dormant for long periods and germinate when conditions are favourable. The fruit that often surrounds the seed aids its dispersal by animals or by wind and water.

Many flowering plants depend on animal pollinators, and the relationship has shaped the diversity of both groups. Dobzhansky (1973) argued that the interactions between organisms are part of their evolutionary history, and the coevolution of flowers and pollinators shows this clearly. Flowers have evolved colours, scents and shapes that attract particular pollinators, and pollinators have evolved behaviours that exploit them.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the developmental genes that specify the parts of the flower, and how the same regulatory logic appears in the development of animal body plans. Plant reproduction is studied by genetics and by imaging of developing flowers, and by observation of pollinator behaviour in the field.
