Plants coordinate their growth and development with hormones, small molecules that are made in one part of the plant and act in another. The principal classes are auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins, abscisic acid and ethylene. Each has several roles, and the response of a tissue depends on the combination of hormones it receives. Koshland (2002) listed adaptability among the organising principles of life, and hormones are one of the means by which plants adapt their development to conditions.

Auxin promotes cell expansion and directs growth. It is transported from the shoot tip down the stem, and its uneven distribution across a stem causes it to bend toward or away from light. Gibberellins promote stem elongation and seed germination. Cytokinins promote cell division and delay ageing in leaves, and their ratio to auxin helps determine whether a tissue forms shoots or roots in culture.

Abscisic acid is the main hormone of stress. It accumulates when plants are dry, and it causes the stomata to close and the seeds to enter dormancy. Ethylene is a gas that promotes fruit ripening and the shedding of leaves, and it is involved in responses to wounding and submergence.

Hormones act by binding receptors that trigger intracellular signalling. Auxin, for example, is perceived by receptors that target proteins for degradation, which releases transcription factors that switch on auxin-responsive genes. The response is therefore an alteration of gene expression, similar in principle to the signalling described in the Cell Biology module.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how hormones in animals act through similar mechanisms of receptor binding and gene regulation, which shows that the basic logic of signalling is shared across eukaryotes. The hormonal control of plant development is studied by applying hormones to tissues, by measuring their concentration and by mutants that are unable to respond.

Hormone levels are measured by mass spectrometry, by bioassays that respond to a particular hormone, and by reporter genes that show where a hormone is active.
