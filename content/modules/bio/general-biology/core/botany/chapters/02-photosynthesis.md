Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy and uses it to make sugars from carbon dioxide and water. It takes place in the chloroplasts of plants and algae, and in cyanobacteria. The process has two stages. The light-dependent reactions take place on the thylakoid membranes, and they use light to split water, release oxygen, and produce ATP and NADPH. The light-independent reactions, known as the Calvin cycle, take place in the stroma and use the ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into sugar.

Nelson and Ben-Shem (2004) reviewed the architecture of the complexes that carry out the light reactions. Photosystem II captures light and uses it to remove electrons from water, and the oxygen released is the source of almost all the oxygen in the atmosphere. The electrons pass through a chain of carriers to photosystem I, which uses a second light absorption to reduce NADP+ to NADPH. The electron flow pumps protons into the thylakoid lumen, and the resulting gradient drives ATP synthesis, in the same way as in mitochondria.

The Calvin cycle begins when the enzyme RuBisCO attaches carbon dioxide to a five-carbon sugar. The resulting six-carbon compound splits into two three-carbon molecules, which are reduced using ATP and NADPH. Most of the resulting sugar is used to regenerate the five-carbon acceptor, and a fraction is exported as sugar or used to make starch and other compounds.

RuBisCO is the most abundant enzyme on Earth, and it is inefficient. It sometimes reacts with oxygen instead of carbon dioxide, a reaction called photorespiration that wastes energy and carbon. Many plants have evolved mechanisms that concentrate carbon dioxide near RuBisCO, which reduce this loss.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the energy captured by photosynthesis enters the metabolism of the plant and supports every other process. The same principles of electron transport and chemiosmosis appear in respiration, and the two processes are closely related.

Photosynthesis is measured by gas exchange, by the fluorescence of chlorophyll that reveals the efficiency of the light reactions, and by isotope labelling of carbon dioxide.
