Plant cells differ from animal cells in three conspicuous ways. They have a rigid cell wall outside the plasma membrane, they contain plastids, especially chloroplasts, and they usually have a large central vacuole that occupies much of the cell's volume. Each feature is linked to the way plants live. The wall gives the cell mechanical support, the plastids capture light, and the vacuole stores water and dissolved substances.

The cell wall is built mainly of cellulose, a polymer of glucose described in the Biological Macromolecules module, arranged in fibres embedded in a matrix of hemicelluloses and pectins. The wall resists the osmotic pressure of the cell's contents, so that the cell can become turgid without bursting. Turgor pressure is what keeps leaves and young stems rigid, and a plant that loses turgor wilts.

The vacuole is enclosed by a membrane called the tonoplast. It stores sugars, ions, pigments and waste products, and it regulates the water content of the cell. Changes in the volume of the vacuole allow a cell to grow quickly by taking up water, without making new cytoplasm. Ball (2008) pointed out that water is an active participant in cell chemistry, and the vacuole is one of the large reservoirs in which that water is held.

Plastids are a family of organelles derived from bacteria, as described in the Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells module. Chloroplasts carry out photosynthesis, chromoplasts store pigments and amyloplasts store starch. The plastids of a cell can interconvert, so that a green chloroplast can become a yellow chromoplast during the ripening of fruit.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cytoskeleton and the membrane systems of plant cells, which move the organelles and direct the deposition of the wall. The plant cell is studied by microscopy of stained sections and by the isolation of organelles, and by mutants that change wall composition or vacuole function.
