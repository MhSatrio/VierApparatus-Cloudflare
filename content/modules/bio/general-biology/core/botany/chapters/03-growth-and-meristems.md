Most animals stop growing when they reach adult size, but many plants grow throughout their lives. The growth is sustained by meristems, regions of undifferentiated cells that keep dividing. Apical meristems at the tips of shoots and roots add length to the plant, and lateral meristems add girth through the cambium. Cells produced by the meristem differentiate into the tissues of the mature plant as they move away from the tip.

Growth has two components. Cell division increases the number of cells, and cell expansion increases their size. Expansion is often the larger contributor. A plant cell takes up water into its vacuole, which stretches the wall as the cell grows, and the wall is loosened by enzymes that allow it to expand without breaking. The rate of expansion depends on the turgor pressure and on the plasticity of the wall, both of which respond to hormones and to the environment.

Plants direct their growth toward resources. Phototropism bends a shoot toward light, which is detected by photoreceptors that trigger the redistribution of the plant hormone auxin across the stem. Gravitropism orients roots downward and shoots upward, using sedimenting starch grains in specialised cells to sense the direction of gravity. These responses allow a sessile organism to find the resources it needs.

Ball (2008) pointed out that water is a central part of cell expansion, because the uptake of water by the vacuole drives the enlargement of the cell. The movement of water through the plant is therefore linked to its growth.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell cycle and the regulation of division, which apply to meristem cells as they do to other cells. The meristem balances division against differentiation, and the pattern of cell fates is controlled by signalling between cells.

Growth is studied by measuring the length and width of organs over time, by tracking the division of labelled cells in the meristem and by genetic and hormonal experiments that alter the direction of growth.
