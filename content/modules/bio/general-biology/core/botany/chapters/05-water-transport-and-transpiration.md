A tall tree raises water from its roots to its leaves, sometimes more than a hundred metres. The water moves through the xylem, a system of dead, hollow cells whose walls are reinforced by lignin. The mechanism is the cohesion-tension theory. Water evaporates from the leaves through the stomata, and the loss creates a negative pressure, or tension, that pulls water upward through the column. The cohesion of water molecules holds the column together, because hydrogen bonds between molecules resist breaking under tension.

Ball (2008) described the hydrogen-bond network of water, and cohesion depends on exactly that network. The strength of the bonds allows the water column to bear tension without separating, and the surface tension of water at the walls of the xylem helps keep the column connected to the walls as it is pulled upward. The reliance of plants on water's cohesive properties is one of the clearest examples of a physical property shaping biology.

Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from the leaf, and it occurs mostly through stomata, small pores flanked by guard cells. When the guard cells swell, the pore opens and carbon dioxide can enter for photosynthesis. The same opening allows water to escape. Plants balance these two demands by regulating the stomata, closing them when water is short and opening them when carbon dioxide is needed.

Embolism is a risk to the system. Air bubbles can form in the xylem under tension, and they block the flow of water. Some plants can refill embolised vessels, while others accept the loss of vessels and rely on redundancy.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the membrane transporters and ion channels that control the guard cells, and the signals, including abscisic acid, that trigger their response to drought. The regulation of stomata is therefore a cellular process as well as a physical one.

Water transport is studied by measuring the pressure and flow in stems, by imaging xylem under microscopy and by isotope tracers that follow water from soil to leaf.
