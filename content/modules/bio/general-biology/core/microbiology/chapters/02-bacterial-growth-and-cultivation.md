Bacteria reproduce mainly by binary fission, in which a cell replicates its chromosome, grows and divides into two identical daughters. The population therefore doubles at regular intervals, called the generation time, which ranges from about twenty minutes in fast-growing species to many hours in slow ones. Meselson and Stahl (1958) grew Escherichia coli through several generations to follow the fate of its DNA, and their experiments depended on the regular doubling of the population.

A batch culture of bacteria passes through a characteristic growth curve. In the lag phase, the cells adjust to the new medium and synthesise the enzymes they need, and little division occurs. In the exponential phase, the population doubles at a constant rate, and the cells are most uniform. The stationary phase begins when nutrients run low or waste products accumulate, and the rate of division balances the rate of death. In the death phase, the population declines.

The growth rate is calculated from the number of cells at two times during exponential growth. The generation time is the time needed for the population to double, and it can be used to compare the effects of different media, temperatures or drugs on the same strain.

Bacteria are counted in several ways. Plating a dilution series on solid medium gives colonies, each derived from one cell, which allows the number of viable cells to be estimated. Direct counting with a microscope counts all cells, living or dead. Measuring the turbidity of a liquid culture gives a rapid estimate that must be calibrated against a count.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how bacterial growth depends on the availability of nutrients and on the regulation of the genes for their use, a topic discussed in the Molecular Biology module. Cultivation is therefore a way of observing the regulatory and metabolic networks of the cell in action.

Cultivation methods are chosen according to the organism. Some bacteria require oxygen, some are damaged by it, and some need particular nutrients, so the growth conditions must be matched to the species being studied.
