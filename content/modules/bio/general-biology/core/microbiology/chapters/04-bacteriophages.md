Bacteriophages are viruses that infect bacteria. Each phage recognises a specific receptor on the surface of its host, injects its genome, and uses the host's machinery to make copies of itself. They are the most abundant biological entities on Earth, and they are present wherever bacteria live. Their interactions with bacteria shape microbial populations, and they are studied both for their own biology and as tools.

In the lytic cycle, the phage takes over the host, makes many new phage particles, and then lyses the cell to release them. The host dies in the process. In the lysogenic cycle, the phage genome integrates into the host chromosome, where it is copied with the host's DNA and passed to daughter cells. The prophage remains silent until a signal, such as DNA damage, triggers it to enter the lytic cycle. The choice between the two cycles depends on the phage and on the conditions in the host.

Phages influence bacterial populations in several ways. Lytic infection kills susceptible bacteria and can prevent any one strain from dominating. Lysogenic conversion can change a bacterium's properties when a prophage carries a gene, such as a toxin, that the host did not previously have. This is one of the ways in which phages contribute to the virulence of pathogens.

Hug and colleagues (2016) reported the genomes of many uncultured organisms, and the phages infecting them are part of the diversity that remains to be described. The phage community of an environment is itself a large and largely unknown part of the microbial world.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular events of phage infection, including the way phage genes take control of host transcription and translation. The study of phages has contributed much of the basic knowledge of gene regulation, as well as tools such as restriction enzymes that cut DNA at defined sequences.

Phages are studied by plaque assays, which count infectious particles, by genome sequencing and by electron microscopy, which shows their structure. Their use in therapy is being explored as an alternative to antibiotics, but the methods require careful control.
