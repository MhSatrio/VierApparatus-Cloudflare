Antibiotics kill or inhibit bacteria by attacking specific targets, such as the synthesis of the cell wall, the ribosome or the replication of DNA. Resistance arises when a bacterium changes its target so that the drug no longer binds it, inactivates the drug with an enzyme, or pumps the drug out of the cell before it reaches its target. Each mechanism can arise by mutation, and each can be spread by gene transfer.

The spread of resistance is an evolutionary process. Dobzhansky (1973) argued that the outcome of selection depends on the variation available, and in a bacterial population exposed to a drug, the rare cells that carry a resistance mutation or gene survive and reproduce. Within a few generations, the population may consist mainly of resistant cells. Because bacteria divide quickly, selection can act very rapidly, and this speed is one reason resistance has become a serious medical problem.

Horizontal gene transfer moves resistance genes between bacteria, often on plasmids that carry several resistance genes at once. A resistance gene that arose in one species can spread to others in the same environment, including pathogens that cause disease in humans. Woese and colleagues (1990) placed bacteria and archaea in separate domains, but the transfer of mobile elements crosses those boundaries in some cases.

The rate of resistance depends on the use of antibiotics. Treatment that kills most of a population while leaving a few resistant cells can select for those cells, and incomplete courses or the use of antibiotics in agriculture may increase the pressure. Strategies to slow resistance include restricting the use of drugs, combining drugs with different targets and developing new compounds.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular basis of drug action, which is essential for understanding how a target changes. The study of resistance combines laboratory evolution experiments, in which bacteria are exposed to increasing concentrations of a drug, with genomic surveillance of clinical isolates.

Resistance is measured by determining the minimum concentration of a drug that inhibits growth, by sequencing resistance genes and by tracking the frequency of resistant strains through time.
