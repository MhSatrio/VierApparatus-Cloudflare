Microorganisms are found in both the bacterial and archaeal domains, and in the eukaryotic domain as protists and fungi. The bacteria are the most diverse group of prokaryotes that have been studied, with many phyla defined by sequence. Archaea were once thought to live only in extreme environments, but they are now known from soils, oceans and the guts of animals. Woese and colleagues (1990) established the division of prokaryotes into these two domains, and the division has been confirmed by much larger datasets.

Protists are a heterogeneous group of eukaryotes, many of them unicellular, that include algae, amoebae and parasites. They are not a natural group in the strict sense, but the name is used for convenience. Fungi that grow as single cells, such as yeasts, are microbes in the same sense, although they belong to a different lineage from bacteria.

The main limitation of cultivation has long been known. Most microbes cannot be grown in the laboratory, because their nutritional needs or their partners in the environment are unknown. Hug and colleagues (2016) used the sequences of genomes recovered directly from environmental samples to place many uncultured lineages in the tree of life, and their work showed that a large part of microbial diversity had been missed.

Sequencing has also revealed that microbes live in communities that are difficult to separate. Many bacteria depend on others for nutrients or for protection, and the community as a whole can behave in ways that no single member does alone.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the biology of microbes is studied through the molecular machinery they share with other cells, and how their differences reflect their environments. The combination of cultivation, sequencing and imaging has made it possible to study both cultured and uncultured organisms.

Microbial diversity is measured by sequencing marker genes, such as ribosomal RNA, from environmental samples, and by culturing organisms that can be grown in the laboratory.
