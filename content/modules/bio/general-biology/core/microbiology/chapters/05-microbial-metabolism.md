Microorganisms have a metabolic range much wider than that of animals and plants. Their energy can come from light, from the oxidation of organic compounds or from the oxidation of inorganic substances such as hydrogen, iron or sulfur. Their carbon can come from organic compounds or from carbon dioxide. Organisms are named by combining these choices: a phototroph uses light, a chemotroph uses chemical energy, a lithotroph uses inorganic electron donors and an organotroph uses organic ones. An autotroph fixes carbon dioxide and a heterotroph uses organic carbon.

Respiration with oxygen as the final electron acceptor is the most energetically favourable option where oxygen is available. Many microbes respire with other acceptors, such as nitrate, sulfate, iron or carbon dioxide itself. These anaerobic respirations yield less energy than aerobic respiration, but they allow life in environments where oxygen is absent, such as sediments, the deep subsurface and the guts of animals.

Lane and Martin (2010) argued that the energy available per gene constrains the complexity of cells, and microbial metabolism shows how energy determines the range of possible lifestyles. An organism that obtains only a small amount of energy from its environment must use it efficiently, and the microbes with the smallest genomes often depend on others for many of their needs.

Fermentation is the use of organic compounds as both electron donors and acceptors, and it produces little energy. It is widespread among bacteria and is important in food and industry. Methanogenesis, carried out by some archaea, reduces carbon dioxide to methane, and it is a key process in anaerobic environments.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the metabolic pathways of microbes are built from the same enzymes and chemistry as those of other organisms, with differences in the carriers and electron acceptors that each lineage uses. The diversity of microbial metabolism is largely a matter of which reactions are combined.

Microbial metabolism is studied by growing organisms on defined substrates, by measuring the gases and products they release, and by genomic analysis that identifies the pathways present.
