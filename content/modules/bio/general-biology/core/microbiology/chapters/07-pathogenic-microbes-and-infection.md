Most microbes that live on and in the human body are harmless or helpful. A pathogen is a microbe that can cause disease, and the process begins when it colonises a host, establishes itself, and causes damage. Colonisation is the presence of a microbe on a surface, such as the skin or gut. Infection is the establishment of a microbe inside the host, where it multiplies. Disease is the damage that follows and that causes symptoms. These stages are distinct, and many microbes colonise without causing infection.

Pathogens use virulence factors to overcome the host's defences. Adhesins allow them to attach to host tissues, and capsules protect them from phagocytes. Toxins damage host cells or disrupt their signalling. Some bacteria secrete enzymes that break down tissues, and others inject proteins into host cells through specialised secretion systems. Many virulence genes are carried on mobile elements, which is one reason pathogens can acquire new factors by horizontal gene transfer, as described in the earlier chapter.

The host responds with an immune system that recognises the invader and attacks it. The response can cause damage of its own, and some of the symptoms of infection are the consequence of inflammation rather than of the microbe. The balance between the host response and the pathogen's strategies determines the outcome of infection.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the reliability of research findings depends on the design and reporting of studies, and the study of pathogenesis is no exception. Claims about the role of a virulence factor should be supported by experiments that remove the factor and show a reduction in disease, and by replication in independent models.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular basis of host-pathogen interactions, including the receptors that microbes use to enter cells and the signalling pathways that the host uses to respond. The mechanisms are studied in cell culture, in animal models and, where possible, in human samples.

Pathogens are identified by culture, by molecular tests that detect their genes, and by sequencing that reveals the genetic basis of their virulence and resistance.
