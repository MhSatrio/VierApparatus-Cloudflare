Microbes rarely live alone. A typical environment contains many species that depend on one another for nutrients, detoxify each other's waste, or regulate each other's growth. The community is structured by these interactions, and it can behave as a single unit in processes such as the breakdown of organic matter in soil or the treatment of sewage.

Interactions among microbes include exchange of metabolites, in which the waste of one species is the food of another, and cross-feeding, in which a compound made by one organism supports the growth of another. Some microbes produce signalling molecules that allow them to sense the density of their neighbours and to change their behaviour collectively, a process called quorum sensing. Competition for nutrients and space also shapes communities, and some microbes produce antibiotics that inhibit their rivals.

Microbes drive the cycles of carbon and nitrogen in the environment. Photosynthetic bacteria and algae fix carbon dioxide, decomposers break down dead material, and nitrogen-fixing bacteria add nitrogen that other organisms can use. Nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonia to nitrate, and denitrifying bacteria return nitrogen gas to the atmosphere. Without these processes the chemistry of the planet would be very different.

Hug and colleagues (2016) showed that the microbial diversity of the environment is far larger than the cultured species suggest, and that many of the organisms that drive the cycles have never been grown in the laboratory. Their genomes reveal metabolic capacities that are inferred from sequence data.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular mechanisms of communication and metabolism that underlie these interactions. The study of communities combines these mechanisms with ecological measurements of abundance and activity.

Microbial communities are studied by sequencing the DNA or RNA of the whole community, by measuring the chemical products they release and by experiments in which members are removed or added to see how the others respond.
