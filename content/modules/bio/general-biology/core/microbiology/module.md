# Microbiology

Microbiology studies the organisms too small to see without magnification: bacteria, archaea, many protists, fungi in their unicellular forms, and viruses. Microorganisms are the oldest and most numerous forms of life, and they drive the cycles of carbon and nitrogen, live in and on every other organism, and cause much of the disease that humans have faced. The field is central to medicine, agriculture, industry and environmental science.

The module begins with the diversity of microbes and the methods used to study them, including the fact that most microbial species cannot be cultured. It then covers bacterial growth, genetics, gene transfer, the bacteriophages that infect them and the metabolic range of microbes. The later chapters cover microbial ecology, the causes and mechanisms of infection, and the spread of resistance to antimicrobial drugs.

The module draws on the classification described in Biological Classification, because the diversity of microbes is best understood through the tree of life that Woese and colleagues (1990) and Hug and colleagues (2016) established.

Readers should keep in mind that microbes are not a single group. The term describes size and lifestyle rather than ancestry, and the chapters point out where the differences between bacteria and archaea matter.
