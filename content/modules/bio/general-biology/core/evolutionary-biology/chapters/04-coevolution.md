Coevolution is evolution in which the traits of one species change in response to the traits of another, and the change in the second species in turn affects the first. The process is reciprocal. A predator that becomes faster exerts stronger selection on its prey to escape, and the prey that becomes harder to catch selects for faster predators. Each species is part of the environment of the other, and their evolution is coupled.

Antagonistic coevolution occurs between predators and prey, parasites and hosts, and competitors. The arms race between a plant that produces toxins and an insect that tolerates them is a well-studied example. Each new defence by the plant selects for a new tolerance in the insect, and the plant's chemistry diversifies in response. Dobzhansky (1973) noted that such interactions have shaped the evolution of both partners, and the arms race is one of the clearest cases.

Mutualistic coevolution occurs when both partners benefit. Flowering plants and their pollinators have evolved together, with the shape of flowers matching the mouthparts of pollinators, and the nectar and pollen rewards matching the needs of the animals. Mutualism can be stable, but it can also change if one partner gains an advantage from the relationship without giving a corresponding benefit.

Coevolution can produce diversification. A plant that is attacked by a specialised herbivore may evolve a defence that another herbivore cannot overcome, and the two lineages may diverge as each specialises. The diversity of some groups, such as some plant and insect lineages, has been linked to coevolutionary interactions.

Darwin (1859) discussed the close relationships among species that depend on each other, such as the flowers of plants and the insects that visit them, and he argued that such relationships shape the form of both partners. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular interactions between hosts and pathogens that drive such coevolution. Coevolution is studied by comparing the traits of interacting species, by experimental evolution with pairs of organisms and by phylogenetic analysis of the lineages involved.
