Altruism is behaviour that reduces the fitness of the individual who performs it while increasing the fitness of another. Worker bees that die defending the hive, and birds that give alarm calls that attract predators, appear to be disadvantaged in the sense of individual selection. Darwin (1859) recognised the problem, and it was a serious challenge to natural selection for a century afterwards.

Hamilton (1964) resolved it by considering the genes rather than the individual. A gene that causes altruism can spread if the benefit it confers to relatives, who are likely to carry the same gene, outweighs the cost to the altruist. The gene's fitness is counted across all the copies it makes, including those in relatives, and this inclusive fitness can increase even when the individual's direct fitness falls.

Hamilton's rule states that an altruistic act is favoured when the relatedness between the actor and the recipient, multiplied by the benefit to the recipient, exceeds the cost to the actor. Relatedness is the probability that the two share a gene by descent, and it is one half for siblings and parents and offspring, and one eighth for cousins. The rule predicts that altruism should be directed toward close relatives and should be more common when relatedness is high.

The rule is a prediction that can be tested. Studies of social insects, where the relatedness of workers to their sisters is high, have examined whether the pattern of altruism matches the rule. The interpretation of the results has been debated, and some researchers have argued that other mechanisms contribute, but the principle remains central to the study of social behaviour.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular basis of behaviour and the genes that influence social interaction. Kin selection is studied by measuring relatedness, by experiments that manipulate the benefit to relatives and by comparisons across species with different social systems.
