Sexual selection is the selection that arises from differences in mating success. Darwin developed the concept in *The Descent of Man* (1871) to explain traits that seemed to have no survival advantage, such as the long tails of peacocks and the bright plumage of many birds. The idea was foreshadowed in the 1859 Origin of Species. He recognised two processes. Intrasexual selection is competition between members of one sex, usually males, for access to mates. Intersexual selection, or mate choice, is the preference of one sex for certain traits in the other.

Competition for mates often favours larger size, weapons and aggressive behaviour. Antlers and tusks are examples of traits that are used in direct contests. Such traits can be very costly to produce and to carry, but their benefit is access to mates, and their evolution depends on whether the benefit outweighs the cost.

Mate choice favours traits that signal quality. The female preference for a trait may be arbitrary in origin, or it may reflect an association with good genes or with the ability to provide resources. Two models compete to explain these preferences. The good genes model holds that a trait indicates genetic quality that will be passed to offspring. The runaway model holds that a preference and a trait can evolve together through a positive feedback, and that the trait becomes exaggerated even if it does not indicate quality.

The persistence of costly traits is a puzzle. Traits that reduce survival must be compensated by mating success, and the balance between the two can maintain them at a stable level. Hamilton (1964) was concerned with social behaviour rather than mate choice, but his general approach of asking how traits affect the spread of genes is the framework within which these trade-offs are analysed.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the genetic and molecular bases of the traits involved, including the hormones and signals that control their expression. Sexual selection is studied by manipulating the traits of males and measuring mating success, by experiments that change the choices available to females and by comparisons across species.
