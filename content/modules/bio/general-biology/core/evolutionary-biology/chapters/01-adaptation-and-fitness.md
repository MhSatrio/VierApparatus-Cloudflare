Fitness is the expected contribution of an individual or genotype to the gene pool of future generations. It is measured by the number of offspring that survive to reproduce, and it summarises both survival and reproduction. The phrase survival of the fittest is often linked to Darwin, but it entered his work only in later editions, and the modern meaning is precise. Fitness is an expectation over the life of an organism and over the environments it may encounter, not a measure of strength or health at one moment.

Absolute fitness is the actual number of surviving offspring of a genotype. Relative fitness compares the absolute fitness of a genotype with that of others in the population, and it is the quantity that determines how allele frequencies change under selection. A genotype with higher relative fitness increases in frequency, regardless of the absolute number of offspring in a particular generation. The distinction matters because a population can grow while its genotypes change in relative proportion.

Adaptation is inferred from the advantage that a trait confers. A trait is an adaptation if it increases fitness in the environment where it is found, and if its prevalence can be explained by selection rather than by chance or by a correlated trait. Dobzhansky (1973) argued that the adaptations of organisms make sense only in terms of their history, and the inference of adaptation requires evidence that the trait improves survival or reproduction.

Fitness is hard to measure in natural populations. Lifetime reproductive success requires long-term study, and the environment changes from generation to generation. Biologists therefore use proxies, such as survival over a season or the number of eggs laid, and they test whether the proxy predicts lifetime success.

The concept of fitness connects population genetics to ecology. Each environment imposes a set of selective pressures, and the fitness of a genotype is a property of its interaction with that environment. Changing the environment can change which genotype has the highest fitness.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how genetic variation in traits is produced by the molecular mechanisms of gene expression, and how that variation can be linked to differences in fitness. Fitness is estimated by field studies, by experimental evolution and by statistical analysis of the relationship between traits and reproductive success.
