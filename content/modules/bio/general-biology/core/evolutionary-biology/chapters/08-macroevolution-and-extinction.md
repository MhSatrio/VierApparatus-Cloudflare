Microevolution is the change in allele frequencies within populations over a few generations. Macroevolution is the pattern of change among lineages over long periods, including the rise and fall of groups, the timing of diversification and the extinction of species. A central question is whether macroevolution can be fully explained by microevolution extended over time, or whether processes operating among species add something new. Darwin (1859) assumed that the patterns of the fossil record were produced by the same processes that operate in living populations, and most biologists still hold that view.

Knoll and Carroll (1999) reviewed the evidence for the early diversification of animals, and their analysis is an example of macroevolutionary inference. The sequence of appearance of body plans in the fossil record is interpreted in relation to environmental change and to the genetic toolkit that animals share. Their work shows how the patterns of the deep past are read from fossils, rocks and comparative biology.

Extinction is a major part of macroevolution. Background extinction occurs continually at low rates, while mass extinctions remove a large fraction of species in a short time. The causes of the best-known mass extinctions include volcanism, asteroid impacts and changes in ocean chemistry, and the debate over the relative importance of these causes continues. Extinction opens ecological space, and the survivors often diversify into the vacant niches.

Species selection is one proposed mechanism for macroevolution, in which species with particular traits leave more descendant species than others. Its importance is debated, because the evidence for it is less clear than the evidence for selection within populations. The debate illustrates the value of testing claims about patterns against the processes that could produce them.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular basis of the traits and developmental programmes that change over long periods. The relation between molecular change and macroevolutionary pattern is an active area of study, and it connects the processes of the cell to the history of life.

Macroevolution is studied by the fossil record, by molecular dating, which estimates the times of divergence from sequence differences, and by statistical analysis of the diversity of lineages through time.
