Adaptive radiation is the rapid diversification of a lineage into many species, each adapted to a different way of life. The species that result differ in their diet, their habitat or their morphology, and the differences are linked to the use of the environment. Darwin recorded the birds of the Galapagos Islands during the voyage of the Beagle, and the finches he collected there are now a classic example of radiation. Their beaks differ in size and shape according to the food they eat.

Radiation is usually triggered by ecological opportunity. A lineage that colonises an area with few competitors, or with unused resources, can spread into those niches and diverge. Islands, lakes and newly exposed habitats often provide such opportunity, and the radiation of cichlid fishes in African lakes is another well-known case. The number of species can rise quickly, and the diversification can be linked to changes in the environment.

Dobzhansky (1973) argued that the diversity of organisms is a result of the adaptation of populations to their environments, and radiation shows that adaptation can produce many species from one ancestor. The differences among the species in a radiation are often traits that affect feeding, mating or the choice of habitat, and these traits can diverge rapidly under selection.

The reproductive isolation that defines a species, discussed in the Evolution Fundamentals module, can arise during a radiation as populations adapt to different niches. Divergence in mating behaviour or timing can reduce gene flow even when the populations live in the same area.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the genetic basis of the differences among species, including the regulatory changes that alter the form of beaks, fins and other traits. Radiations are studied by comparing the traits and ecology of closely related species, by sequencing their genomes and by reconstructing the timing of divergence.
