Convergent evolution is the independent evolution of similar traits in lineages that are not closely related. It occurs when different organisms face similar selective pressures and find similar solutions. The wings of bats and birds, the streamlined bodies of fish and dolphins and the eyes of vertebrates and cephalopods are examples. In each case the similarity reflects the physics or the chemistry of the problem rather than common ancestry.

Convergence can be striking at the molecular level. Some proteins that have evolved independently in different lineages have similar structures, because the physical requirements of the task are similar. Antifreeze proteins in fish from polar seas and in some insects are an example. Their origin in different lineages is shown by the differences in their sequences, even though their function is the same.

Distinguishing convergence from homology requires care. Darwin (1859) noted that external similarity can be misleading, and that the characters used to infer relationships must be examined closely. Molecular data and developmental origin are the main tools. If two traits share a developmental origin or a genetic basis that cannot be explained by a common ancestor with the trait, they are homologous. If they arise from different developmental pathways, they are convergent.

Convergence limits what can be inferred from a single trait. A tree built on morphology alone may group convergent species together, and this error was one of the reasons that molecular data changed systematics. The use of many independent characters reduces the risk.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the same molecular solutions can recur because the chemistry of biological molecules is constrained. Convergence is studied by comparing the structures and sequences of similar traits in different lineages and by testing whether they arose independently.
