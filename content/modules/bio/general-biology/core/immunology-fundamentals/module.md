# Immunology Fundamentals

The immune system protects the body against infection, and it also removes damaged and abnormal cells. It consists of cells and molecules spread throughout the body, organised into an innate response that acts within hours and an adaptive response that takes days to develop but is highly specific and remembers past encounters. The two responses work together, and the balance between them determines whether an infection is controlled or becomes disease.

The module begins with innate immunity and the receptors that recognise microbes. It then covers inflammation, the adaptive response of lymphocytes, the structure and action of antibodies, the presentation of antigens to T cells, and the memory that makes vaccination possible. The chapters connect the cells described in the Histology module to the molecular recognition described in the Biological Macromolecules module.

The immune system is a clear example of a biological system that must distinguish self from non-self. The chapters explain how that distinction is made, and what happens when it fails.

Immunology is also a field in which claims are often tested clinically, and the reliability of evidence matters. Where the mechanism is established, the chapters say so, and where it is still debated, they describe the competing views.
