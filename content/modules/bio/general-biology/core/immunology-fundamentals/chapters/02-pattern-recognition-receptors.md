Pattern recognition receptors are proteins that bind molecules characteristic of microbes. They are present on the surface of many cells and inside them, and each family recognises a particular class of pattern. Toll-like receptors on the cell surface and in endosomes detect bacterial lipopolysaccharide, bacterial lipoproteins, flagellin and nucleic acids. Receptors in the cytoplasm detect bacterial peptidoglycan and viral RNA and DNA. Medzhitov and Janeway (2002) argued that this kind of recognition explains how the innate system can respond to a very wide range of microbes with a limited set of receptors.

The patterns are conserved among microbes and are not produced by the host. Lipopolysaccharide, for example, is a feature of the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, and double-stranded RNA is produced during the replication of many viruses. A host cell that expresses no double-stranded RNA of its own can therefore treat double-stranded RNA in the cytoplasm as a sign of infection. The receptor and the pattern are matched by the evolution of both.

Binding triggers signalling. Toll-like receptors recruit adaptor proteins and activate kinase cascades that lead to the activation of transcription factors. These factors switch on genes for inflammatory cytokines, chemokines and interferons, which recruit more immune cells and make neighbouring cells resistant to viruses. The response is coordinated, so that the signal produced by a few infected cells can spread through a tissue.

The specificity of the system is for classes of molecule, not for individual microbes. The response to a bacterium and a fungus may differ, because each activates a different combination of receptors, but the system cannot distinguish two closely related strains with the same patterns. The trade-off is speed and breadth in exchange for precision.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling pathways downstream of the receptors, including the nuclear factor that controls inflammatory genes. The receptors are studied by binding assays with purified ligands, by genetic deletion in mice and by measurement of the cytokines produced by cells exposed to each pattern.
