The first line of defence against microbes is the body's surface. The skin is a dry, keratinised barrier that resists penetration, and the mucous membranes of the gut, lungs and urinary tract are covered by mucus and by cilia that trap and move microbes away. Secretions such as tears, saliva and gastric acid contain antimicrobial molecules, and the normal flora of bacteria on the surface compete with pathogens for space and nutrients.

Microbes that breach the barrier meet the cells of innate immunity. Macrophages and neutrophils engulf and destroy them by phagocytosis, and dendritic cells sample the microbes and carry information about them to the lymph nodes. Natural killer cells recognise and kill cells that are infected by viruses or that have lost the markers of healthy cells. These cells are present before any infection, and they do not depend on previous exposure.

Medzhitov and Janeway (2002) described how innate immune cells recognise patterns that are shared by large groups of microbes but absent from the host. These patterns include components of bacterial walls, bacterial DNA with features that differ from host DNA and double-stranded RNA produced by viruses. The recognition is through a limited number of receptors encoded in the genome, so it is fast but not highly specific.

Innate immunity does not produce the highly specific memory of the adaptive response, although some innate cells can be altered by an earlier infection and respond more strongly the next time. Its speed is its strength. Most infections are controlled by innate mechanisms within hours, before the adaptive response can develop.

The soluble molecules of innate immunity include the complement system, a cascade of proteins that coats microbes and damages their membranes, and antimicrobial peptides that disrupt bacterial membranes. Their activity depends on the chemistry of the membranes they attack, which differs between microbes and host cells.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the receptors and signalling pathways of innate cells. Innate immunity is studied by infecting animals with defined pathogens, by identifying the receptors that respond to them and by measuring the cells and molecules recruited to the site of infection.
