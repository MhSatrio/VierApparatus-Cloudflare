The adaptive immune system responds to specific antigens, which are molecules that can be recognised by a particular receptor. Unlike the innate system, it does not depend on conserved patterns. Its receptors are generated by a process of gene rearrangement that creates a vast number of variants. Each lymphocyte carries a receptor of a single specificity, and the population contains receptors for an enormous range of antigens, many of which may never have been encountered before.

The receptors are produced in lymphocytes by recombination of gene segments. Segments encoding the variable region of the receptor are joined in a random combination, and additional diversity is introduced at the junctions. The result is that each developing lymphocyte makes a receptor that differs from the receptors of its neighbours. Medzhitov and Janeway (2002) distinguished the fixed recognition of the innate system from this variable, adaptive recognition, and the contrast explains why the two systems complement each other.

Lymphocytes that react strongly to the body's own molecules are removed during development, a process that prevents autoimmunity in most cases. Lymphocytes that encounter their antigen in the presence of signals from innate cells are activated, proliferate and differentiate into effector cells.

The adaptive response has two arms. The humoral arm is mediated by B cells, which secrete antibodies that bind antigens in the body fluids. The cellular arm is mediated by T cells, which recognise antigens presented on the surfaces of other cells and either kill infected cells or help other immune cells. The arms are coordinated, and T helper cells provide signals that B cells need to produce high-affinity antibodies.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular and molecular basis of lymphocyte development and activation, and the signalling pathways that follow receptor engagement. The adaptive system is studied by measuring the frequency of antigen-specific lymphocytes, by sequencing their receptors and by testing their response to defined antigens.
