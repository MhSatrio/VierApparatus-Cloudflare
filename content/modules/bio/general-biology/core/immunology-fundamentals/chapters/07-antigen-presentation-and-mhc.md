T cells do not recognise free antigen. They recognise peptides displayed on the surface of other cells by major histocompatibility complex molecules, often called MHC. The peptides are fragments of proteins that have been processed inside the cell. A T cell receptor binds both the peptide and the MHC molecule, and its specificity depends on both. Presentation is therefore the step that links a foreign protein to the T cell response.

Class I MHC molecules are present on almost all nucleated cells. They bind peptides derived from proteins made inside the cell, such as viral proteins or abnormal proteins from cancer cells. The peptides are generated by the proteasome, a large protease complex, and they are transported into the endoplasmic reticulum, where they are loaded onto the MHC molecule. The complex then travels to the surface, where cytotoxic T cells can inspect it.

Class II MHC molecules are present on specialised antigen-presenting cells, including dendritic cells, macrophages and B cells. They bind peptides derived from proteins that the cell has taken up from outside, such as the contents of an engulfed bacterium. The loading occurs in a compartment that receives material from the lysosomal pathway, and the complex is then displayed to helper T cells.

Medzhitov and Janeway (2002) described how the innate system recognises microbes and signals danger, and antigen presentation is the point at which that information is passed to the adaptive system. Dendritic cells that have encountered a microbe present its peptides more efficiently and express the co-stimulatory molecules needed to activate T cells.

MHC genes are highly polymorphic, meaning that different individuals carry different versions. The variation allows a population to present a wider range of peptides, which improves its defence against pathogens. It also explains why transplanted tissues are rejected unless the donor and recipient are closely matched, because the T cells of the recipient recognise the foreign MHC molecules as antigens.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the intracellular pathways of protein degradation and the transport of peptides into the endoplasmic reticulum. Presentation is studied by mass spectrometry of the peptides bound to MHC, by structural studies of peptide-MHC complexes and by functional assays with T cells.
