B cells and T cells are the two main lymphocyte lineages. Both arise from haematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow, as described in the Histology module, but they mature in different places. B cells mature in the bone marrow in mammals, and T cells mature in the thymus. The two lineages carry different receptors. The B cell receptor is a membrane-bound antibody, which recognises the antigen in its native form in the body fluids. The T cell receptor recognises antigen only when it is presented on the surface of another cell, bound to a molecule of the major histocompatibility complex.

Helper T cells, which carry the CD4 marker, recognise antigens presented by MHC class II molecules, and they release cytokines that direct the responses of other cells. They are required for most antibody responses, and they activate macrophages to destroy the microbes they have engulfed. Cytotoxic T cells, which carry CD8, recognise antigens presented by MHC class I molecules, which are found on almost all nucleated cells. A cytotoxic cell that recognises an infected cell kills it by releasing enzymes that trigger apoptosis, the programmed death described in the Cell Biology module.

Medzhitov and Janeway (2002) described the innate signals that are needed to activate lymphocytes in many responses. The activation of a lymphocyte requires the recognition of its antigen together with co-stimulatory signals from innate cells, and this requirement prevents the adaptive system from responding to harmless material without danger signals.

Lymphocytes circulate continually between the blood and the lymphoid tissues, where they encounter antigens that have been carried there. Most lymphocytes are not activated and survive for weeks or months, and only a small proportion encounter their specific antigen during any given period.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling that follows receptor engagement and the transcription factors that control lymphocyte differentiation. Lymphocytes are studied by flow cytometry, which identifies each lineage by its surface markers, and by culture with specific antigens.
