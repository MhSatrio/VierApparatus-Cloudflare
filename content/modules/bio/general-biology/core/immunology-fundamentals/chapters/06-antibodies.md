An antibody is a protein that binds a specific antigen with high affinity. Each antibody molecule has a Y-shaped structure built from two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains, joined by disulfide bonds. Each chain has a variable region at its tip and a constant region at its base. The variable regions of the two arms form the antigen-binding sites, and their sequences differ between antibodies, which gives each one its specificity. The constant region determines the class of the antibody and how it interacts with other components of the immune system.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the folding of a protein is determined by its sequence, and the immunoglobulin fold of the antibody is an example of a structure that is stabilised by a conserved pattern of sheets and disulfide bonds. The variable regions differ in the sequence of their loops, which form the antigen-binding surface.

The main classes of antibody are IgM, IgG, IgA, IgE and IgD. IgM is the first antibody made in a response and forms pentamers that bind antigens efficiently. IgG is the most abundant in blood and crosses the placenta to protect the fetus. IgA is found in secretions such as saliva, tears and milk, where it blocks microbes at the mucosal surface. IgE binds to mast cells and triggers the release of mediators in allergic responses and in defence against parasites. IgD is less well understood, and its function on B cells is still being studied.

Antibodies neutralise microbes by binding to their surface and blocking their attachment to host cells. They promote phagocytosis by coating microbes, which allows macrophages and neutrophils to recognise and engulf them. Their constant regions activate the complement cascade, which damages the membranes of microbes and recruits more cells.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how antibody genes are rearranged and how the constant regions are switched during an immune response. Antibodies are measured by enzyme-linked immunoassays, which detect binding to a specific antigen, and by structural studies that reveal how the antigen-binding site contacts its target.
