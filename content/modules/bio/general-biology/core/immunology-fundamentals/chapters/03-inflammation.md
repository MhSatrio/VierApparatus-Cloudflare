Inflammation is the local response of tissue to injury or infection. Its classical signs, described since antiquity as redness, heat, swelling and pain, arise from the changes that take place in the small blood vessels of the affected area. Mediators released by damaged cells and by innate immune cells cause the vessels to dilate, which increases blood flow and warmth. The walls of the vessels become more permeable, so fluid and proteins leak into the tissue, producing swelling. Pain results from the sensitisation of nerve endings by the same mediators.

White cells are recruited from the blood to the site of inflammation in a sequence of steps. Endothelial cells lining the vessel express adhesion molecules, and passing neutrophils slow down by rolling along the surface. Chemokines released by the tissue activate the cells, which then firmly adhere and squeeze between the endothelial cells into the tissue. This process, called extravasation, is controlled by the balance of signals that the tissue produces. Medzhitov and Janeway (2002) described how the recognition of microbes by innate cells triggers the production of these signals.

The neutrophils that arrive first engulf microbes and release enzymes and reactive oxygen species that kill them. Macrophages arrive later and clear dead cells and debris, and they also produce signals that bring in other cells. The inflammatory response is therefore a coordinated sequence of arrival and clearance.

Inflammation normally resolves once the infection is controlled. Anti-inflammatory signals are produced, the recruited cells die or leave, and the damaged tissue is repaired. When resolution fails, inflammation becomes chronic, and this persistent response contributes to diseases such as atherosclerosis and rheumatoid arthritis.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling molecules and the adhesion receptors that control the response. Inflammation is studied by measuring cell recruitment in tissues, by quantifying cytokines in blood and fluid, and by imaging the movement of labelled cells in living animals.
