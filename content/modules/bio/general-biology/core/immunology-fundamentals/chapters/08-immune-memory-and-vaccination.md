The first encounter with an antigen produces a primary response. Lymphocytes that recognise the antigen are activated, expand and differentiate, and antibodies appear in the blood after a delay of days. Most of the activated cells die when the infection is cleared, but a population of memory cells persists. A second encounter with the same antigen produces a secondary response that is faster, larger and more effective, because the memory cells are already present in greater numbers and respond more readily.

Memory B cells produce antibodies with higher affinity for the antigen than those of the primary response, because the antibody genes have been refined by somatic hypermutation and selection during the response. Memory T cells respond to the antigen more quickly and can act directly on infected cells. The memory is therefore qualitative as well as quantitative, and it is the basis of long-term protection against many diseases.

Vaccination exploits this memory. A vaccine presents an antigen, or a harmless form of a pathogen, to the immune system so that the primary response occurs without disease. The memory cells that result protect against the pathogen itself. Medzhitov and Janeway (2002) described how the innate signals that accompany an antigen determine whether an adaptive response is mounted, and most effective vaccines include adjuvants that provide these signals.

Vaccine effectiveness depends on several factors. The antigen must be recognised by antibodies or T cells that protect against the pathogen. The response must be strong enough and durable enough to outlast the period of risk. The pathogen must not be able to escape the response by changing its antigens, which is why some vaccines must be updated, as with influenza. Herd immunity arises when enough of a population is immune that the pathogen cannot spread effectively.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular mechanisms of memory formation. Vaccines are tested by measuring antibody and T cell responses in trial participants, and by following the incidence of disease in vaccinated and unvaccinated groups. Evidence from both kinds of measurement is needed before a claim of protection is accepted.
