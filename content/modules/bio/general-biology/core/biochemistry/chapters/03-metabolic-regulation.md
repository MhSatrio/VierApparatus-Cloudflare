Metabolism must respond quickly to changes in the cell, and much of this response happens through the regulation of enzymes that are already present. Allosteric regulation is the most direct mechanism. An allosteric enzyme has a second binding site, separate from its active site, where a small molecule can bind and change the enzyme's activity. Activators increase activity and inhibitors reduce it. Because the regulator binds at a different site, its effect is not competitive with the substrate.

Feedback inhibition is a special case of allosteric regulation in which the end product of a pathway inhibits one of its early enzymes. When the product accumulates, the pathway slows. When the product is used, the inhibition is relieved and the pathway speeds up. Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein's conformation depends on its sequence and environment, and allosteric regulation depends on the ability of a regulator to change that conformation in a defined way.

Covalent modification is a faster mechanism for changing enzyme activity over a longer span. Phosphorylation by kinases and dephosphorylation by phosphatases can switch an enzyme on or off within seconds to minutes. Hormones often act through these changes, because a hormone can trigger a cascade of phosphorylations that alters many enzymes at once.

Changing the amount of an enzyme is the slowest form of regulation. The transcription of a gene for a metabolic enzyme can be increased or decreased in response to hormones or nutrients, and the change takes hours to build up or to decline. This mechanism is used to adapt metabolism to long-term changes such as fasting, exercise or a new diet.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how these mechanisms work together, so that a cell responds to a sudden change through covalent modification and allosteric control, and to a sustained change through altered enzyme synthesis. The combination gives the cell both speed and stability.

Regulation is measured by the activity of enzymes in extracts from cells under different conditions, by the phosphorylation state of enzymes, and by the amount of enzyme protein and its messenger RNA.
