Metabolism is the set of chemical reactions that a cell carries out, and it is organised into pathways. A pathway is a series of enzyme-catalysed steps in which each product becomes the substrate of the next. Pathways are often grouped into two classes. Catabolic pathways break large molecules down into smaller ones and release energy. Anabolic pathways use energy to build large molecules from small ones.

The two classes are linked by shared intermediates and by common carriers. ATP stores energy in a form that can be used to drive anabolic reactions. The reduced electron carriers NADH and NADPH carry electrons from catabolic reactions to the reactions that need reducing power. The carrier molecules are used repeatedly, so a small pool can support a large flux of reactions. Alberts et al. (2022) describe these carriers as the means by which cells move energy and electrons around the metabolic network.

The design of pathways reflects the economy of energy. Lane and Martin (2010) argued that the energy available to a cell limits the number and size of the proteins it can maintain, and metabolic pathways are one of the main consumers of that energy. A pathway that yields a large amount of energy per step, or that reuses its intermediates efficiently, has an advantage in a resource-limited cell.

Pathways are also regulated. The first committed step of a biosynthetic pathway is often inhibited by its end product, which prevents the cell from making more of a compound than it needs. Catabolic pathways are often activated when energy is low, and anabolic pathways when energy is high. The balance between these controls keeps the cell's metabolism matched to its needs.

Metabolism is studied by measuring the concentrations of intermediates, by labelling substrates with isotopes and following their path through the network, and by measuring the fluxes through each step.
