Biochemistry depends on methods that separate, identify and measure molecules in complex mixtures. Most of these methods were developed to answer questions about the structure and activity of individual molecules, and they are now applied to whole cells and tissues. A biochemist moves from a crude extract to a purified protein, then measures its activity and its structure, and the sequence of steps determines the quality of the result.

Protein purification combines methods that separate molecules by different properties. Ion exchange chromatography separates proteins by their charge, size exclusion separates them by size, and affinity chromatography exploits a specific binding interaction. Each step reduces the number of contaminating proteins, and the purity is checked by gel electrophoresis and by measuring the activity of the protein after each step. Anfinsen (1973) used purified proteins to show that a sequence determines the structure, and purification was essential to that work.

Mass spectrometry identifies molecules by their mass to charge ratio. Proteins are digested into peptides, and the masses of the peptides are matched against sequence databases to identify the protein. Metabolites are identified by their masses and by their fragmentation patterns, and quantitative methods measure how much of each is present.

Activity assays measure the rate of an enzyme by coupling its product to a detectable signal, such as the reduction of a coloured dye or the change in absorbance of NADH at 340 nanometres. The assay must be sensitive, specific and carried out under conditions where the rate is proportional to the enzyme concentration, which is why the kinetic analysis described earlier is needed.

Structure determination uses the methods described in the Biological Macromolecules module. Crystallography, nuclear magnetic resonance and cryo-electron microscopy each reveal the atomic arrangement of a protein or complex.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how these techniques are combined in research. A single method rarely answers a biochemical question alone, and the strongest conclusions come from combining purification, activity measurements and structural data.
