Fatty acids are the richest store of energy in most organisms, because they are highly reduced and yield more energy per gram than carbohydrates. They are broken down by beta-oxidation, a cycle of four reactions that removes two carbon atoms at a time from the end of the chain, producing acetyl-CoA, NADH and FADH2. Each turn of the cycle shortens the fatty acid by two carbons, and a long chain yields a large number of acetyl-CoA molecules that enter the citric acid cycle.

Beta-oxidation takes place in the mitochondrion, and its entry is controlled by carnitine, which carries the fatty acid across the inner membrane. The regulation of this entry is one of the ways that cells decide whether to use fat or carbohydrate as their main fuel. Fatty acids are also synthesised from acetyl-CoA by a separate pathway in the cytosol, which uses NADPH as a reducing agent.

Amino acids are broken down when the cell has more of them than it needs for protein synthesis. The amino group is removed by transamination, in which it is transferred to an alpha-ketoglutarate, forming glutamate, and by the release of ammonia from glutamate. The ammonia is converted into urea in mammals and excreted. The remaining carbon skeleton is converted into an intermediate of glycolysis or the citric acid cycle, so that its energy can be used.

Kendrew and colleagues (1960) showed that the structure of a protein determines its function, and the enzymes of amino acid metabolism follow the same principle. Each one binds its substrate in a specific pocket that positions the reactive groups correctly.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how these pathways are linked to the synthesis of amino acids from the intermediates of glycolysis and the citric acid cycle. The same intermediates serve as starting points for building and breaking down molecules, so the network can switch direction depending on need.

These pathways are studied by isotopic tracing of fatty acid and amino acid carbons, by measuring the enzyme activities in tissue extracts, and by analysing the metabolites that accumulate when a step is blocked by a genetic defect.
