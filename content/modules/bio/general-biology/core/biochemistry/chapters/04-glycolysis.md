Glycolysis is the pathway that breaks glucose into two molecules of pyruvate, a three-carbon compound. It takes place in the cytosol of almost all cells and does not require oxygen. The pathway has ten enzyme-catalysed steps, and it can be divided into two phases. In the first phase, two molecules of ATP are invested to phosphorylate glucose and split it into two three-carbon sugars. In the second phase, these sugars are oxidised and their phosphate groups are transferred to ADP, producing four molecules of ATP. The net yield is two molecules of ATP and two of NADH for each glucose.

The pathway is one of the oldest in biology, and it is found in organisms across the tree of life. Its near universality suggests that it arose early, and its energetic yield is small compared with respiration. Lane and Martin (2010) argued that energy availability constrains the complexity of cells, and glycolysis illustrates the constraint: a cell that depends on it alone has a limited energy supply per unit of fuel.

Three steps are irreversible under cellular conditions and are the main control points. These are the phosphorylation of glucose by hexokinase, the phosphorylation of fructose six-phosphate by phosphofructokinase, and the final transfer of a phosphate group to ADP by pyruvate kinase. Each of these enzymes is regulated by the energy status of the cell. ATP and citrate inhibit phosphofructokinase, while AMP activates it, so the pathway speeds up when energy is low.

The oxidation step involves the transfer of electrons from an aldehyde to NAD+, producing NADH. The electrons carried by NADH can later be used in respiration to generate much more ATP. The pyruvate formed at the end of glycolysis can be reduced to lactate in the absence of oxygen, which regenerates NAD+ so that glycolysis can continue.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the chemistry of each step and the way the enzymes are shaped to bind their substrates and stabilise their transition states. The details of these reactions are an example of the enzyme kinetics described in the earlier chapter.

Glycolysis is studied by measuring the intermediates in cells under different conditions, by isotope tracing of glucose carbons, and by the kinetics of individual enzymes.
