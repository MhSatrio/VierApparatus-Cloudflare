Pyruvate from glycolysis is transported into the mitochondrion, where it is converted into acetyl-CoA. The acetyl group enters the citric acid cycle, also called the Krebs cycle or the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and is combined with oxaloacetate to form citrate. Over eight reactions, citrate is converted back to oxaloacetate, and in each turn two carbon atoms leave as carbon dioxide. The cycle makes no net carbon compound, but it is a central hub of metabolism.

In one turn the cycle produces three molecules of NADH, one of FADH2 and one of GTP, which is readily converted into ATP. The NADH and FADH2 carry electrons to the respiratory chain, where most of the energy of the glucose molecule is ultimately captured. Each turn therefore contributes to the energy that the cell makes by oxidative phosphorylation.

The cycle is also a source of building blocks. Its intermediates are used to make amino acids, such as glutamate and aspartate, and they feed the synthesis of heme and of some fatty acids. Removing an intermediate for biosynthesis reduces the flux through the cycle, so the pathway must be replenished, usually by anaplerotic reactions that add carbon to it.

Ball (2008) pointed out that water participates in many of the reactions of cellular metabolism, and the citric acid cycle includes hydration and hydrolysis steps. The reactions depend on the aqueous environment of the mitochondrial matrix.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the cycle is regulated by the energy status of the cell and by the availability of substrates. High levels of ATP and NADH inhibit key enzymes of the cycle, while calcium and ADP stimulate them, so the cycle speeds up when energy is needed.

The cycle is studied by isotopic labelling of its carbons, by measuring the activities of its enzymes in mitochondrial preparations and by measuring the concentrations of intermediates in cells.
