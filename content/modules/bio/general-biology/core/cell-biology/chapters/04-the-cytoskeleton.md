The cytoskeleton is a network of protein filaments that extends through the cytoplasm. It has three main components. Microtubules are hollow tubes built from tubulin subunits, about 25 nanometres in diameter. Actin filaments are thinner, about 7 nanometres across, and are built from actin subunits. Intermediate filaments are a diverse group of fibrous proteins, typically about 10 nanometres across, that provide mechanical strength.

The filaments are dynamic. Microtubules grow and shrink by adding or losing subunits at their ends, a behaviour called dynamic instability. Actin filaments polymerise and depolymerise in response to signals, and their assembly drives the protrusion of the leading edge of a moving cell. Intermediate filaments are more stable and form networks that resist stretching, which is why they are abundant in cells that bear mechanical load.

Motor proteins use the cytoskeleton as tracks. Kinesins and dyneins walk along microtubules, carrying vesicles and organelles toward the cell's periphery or toward its centre. Myosins walk along actin filaments and generate the contraction of muscle and the movement of cell surfaces. Each motor uses the energy of ATP hydrolysis to move in a defined direction along its track.

Betzig and colleagues (2006) showed that the detailed organisation of proteins in cells can be imaged at resolutions beyond the diffraction limit, which has revealed the fine structure of cytoskeletal networks. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the cytoskeleton organises the interior of cells, positions organelles and determines the shape of the cell surface.

The cytoskeleton is studied by fluorescent labelling, by electron microscopy of its filaments, and by the use of drugs that stabilise or disrupt specific filaments, which reveal the roles of each system.
