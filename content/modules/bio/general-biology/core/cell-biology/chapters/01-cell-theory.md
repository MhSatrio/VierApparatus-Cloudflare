Cell theory is the principle that all living things are composed of cells, that the cell is the basic unit of structure and function, and that new cells arise only from existing cells. Its classical form was established in the nineteenth century, when botanists and zoologists compared the tissues they examined under the microscope. Hooke (1665) had seen the cell walls of dead cork, and later observers found living contents inside them.

The classical theory had three parts. The first was that organisms are made of cells. The second was that the cell is the unit of life, meaning that the properties of organisms can be explained by the properties of their cells. The third was that cells arise from other cells by division. The third part was the most difficult to accept, because earlier ideas of spontaneous generation had been widely held, and it required experiments to establish that cells come only from cells.

The modern form of the theory keeps these principles and adds to them. Cells carry genetic information, they are the unit of inheritance, and they are the smallest units that can carry out the processes of life on their own. The theory also recognises that cells can be very different from one another, and that the cells of an organism share a genome but differ in what they express.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell theory as the foundation of modern biology, and they show how the principle that cells arise from cells underlies the study of cell division and inheritance. The theory is a generalisation supported by a large body of evidence, not a law that can be checked by a single test.

Koshland (2002) described the organising principles of living systems, and the cell theory provides the structural framework for those principles. The theory explains why the features of life are found together in units of cells.
