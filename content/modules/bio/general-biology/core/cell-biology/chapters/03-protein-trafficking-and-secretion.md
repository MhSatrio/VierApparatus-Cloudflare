Many proteins must travel from the place where they are made to the place where they act. A protein destined for the plasma membrane, the lysosome or the outside of the cell begins its life on ribosomes in the cytoplasm, or on the rough endoplasmic reticulum if it has a signal sequence. The signal sequence directs the ribosome to the membrane of the endoplasmic reticulum, where the growing chain is threaded into the lumen.

Once inside the endoplasmic reticulum, the protein folds, and it may be modified by the addition of sugars or by the formation of disulfide bonds. Properly folded proteins are packaged into vesicles that bud off and travel to the Golgi apparatus. The Golgi modifies the proteins further, sorts them according to their destination and packages them into new vesicles. Koshland (2002) described compartmentalisation as one of the organising principles of life, and the secretory pathway is a clear example of how the cell moves material between compartments.

Vesicles are targeted by molecular tags on their surfaces and by the proteins that recognise them. Coat proteins shape the vesicle as it buds, and tethering factors and SNARE proteins guide it to its target, where the membranes fuse. The specificity of this system keeps the contents of each compartment distinct.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the same machinery operates in the endocytic pathway, which brings material into the cell from the outside. The two pathways form a continuous flow of membrane and content, and the cell regulates the balance between them.

Trafficking is studied by tagging proteins with fluorescent markers and following them in living cells, by electron microscopy of the membrane compartments, and by genetic screens that identify the components required for each step.
