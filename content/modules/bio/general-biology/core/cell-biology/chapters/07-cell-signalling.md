Cells respond to signals from their environment and from other cells. A signal can be a small molecule, a peptide, a protein, a gas or a physical force. The cell detects the signal with a receptor, usually a protein on the surface or inside the cell. The binding of the signal changes the receptor's shape or activity, and the change is passed on through a series of intracellular events called a signalling pathway.

Pathways often involve a cascade of protein modifications. Phosphorylation is the most common, in which a kinase adds a phosphate to a target protein, changing its activity. Second messengers, such as calcium ions or cyclic nucleotides, carry the signal rapidly through the cytoplasm. The signal can reach the nucleus and change gene expression, or it can act locally on the cytoskeleton or the metabolism. Koshland (2002) listed the capacity to respond to changing conditions among the features of living systems, and signalling is the means by which cells achieve it.

The same signal can produce different responses in different cells. A hormone that stimulates muscle to contract may stimulate a gland to secrete, depending on which receptors and downstream components each cell expresses. The response is therefore a property of the receiving cell as well as of the signal.

Signalling is precise and can be turned off. Receptors are internalised after binding, phosphatases remove phosphate groups, and feedback loops adjust the pathway to the level of the signal. These mechanisms prevent a response from continuing after the signal has ended, and they limit the spread of the signal to appropriate targets.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how signalling pathways are studied by measuring the activity of their components in cells exposed to defined signals, by genetic knockouts that remove individual components, and by quantitative models that predict the response to a given input.
