The eukaryotic cell cycle consists of four phases. In G1 the cell grows and prepares for DNA replication. In S phase the genome is copied, and each chromosome becomes two sister chromatids. In G2 the cell continues to grow and checks that replication is complete. In M phase the chromosomes are separated and the cell divides. Interphase, which includes G1, S and G2, occupies most of the cycle in most cells.

The copying of DNA follows the principle that Watson and Crick (1953) established: each strand of the double helix serves as a template for a new partner, so replication yields two identical duplexes. Replication begins at many origins along each chromosome, and it must be completed exactly once per cycle, since copying a region twice would produce a damaging excess of DNA.

The cycle is driven by proteins called cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases. Cyclins are produced and destroyed at defined times, and each one binds a kinase and activates it. The kinase then phosphorylates target proteins that move the cell into the next phase. The rise and fall of each cyclin therefore set the timing of the cycle.

Checkpoints halt the cycle when conditions are not right. A checkpoint before the start of DNA replication tests whether the cell is large enough and has sufficient nutrients, and a checkpoint before chromosome separation tests whether all chromosomes are correctly attached to the spindle. Damaged DNA activates checkpoints that delay the cycle while repair takes place. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how these controls prevent the propagation of errors into daughter cells.

The cycle is studied by flow cytometry, which measures the DNA content of individual cells and reveals the proportion in each phase, by time-lapse imaging of dividing cells and by genetic and biochemical tests of the regulators.
