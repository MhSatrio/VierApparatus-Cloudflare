Cells in multicellular organisms are held together by adhesion molecules, which are proteins that bind to one another or to the surrounding matrix. Adhesion allows tissues to form and to resist mechanical stress. In epithelia, for example, cells are linked by junctions that form a sheet, and the sheet can cover the surface of an organ or line a cavity. Hooke (1665) observed the boxlike cells of cork, which are held together in a regular array by their walls, a simple example of an adhesive tissue.

Cell-cell junctions take several forms. Tight junctions seal the space between cells and limit the passage of molecules across the sheet. Adherens junctions link the actin cytoskeleton of one cell to its neighbour. Desmosomes attach intermediate filaments to the membrane and distribute mechanical load. Gap junctions form channels that allow small molecules and ions to pass directly between cells, which coordinates their behaviour.

The extracellular matrix is a network of proteins and polysaccharides secreted by cells. Collagen provides tensile strength, elastin allows stretching and proteoglycans hold water and resist compression. The matrix also binds growth factors and signals, and it presents them to cells in a defined way. Cells attach to the matrix through integrins, which are receptors that bind matrix proteins and link them to the actin cytoskeleton.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the matrix and its receptors influence cell shape, movement and survival. Cells that lose their attachment may die or change their behaviour, and this dependence on attachment is one of the reasons that tissues are stable.

Adhesion is studied by measuring the force required to separate cells, by staining for the molecules that form junctions, and by experiments in which the matrix composition or stiffness is varied to see how cells respond.
