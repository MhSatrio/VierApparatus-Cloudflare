Cells can die in two broad ways. Necrosis is the uncontrolled death that follows injury, such as a burn, a lack of oxygen or a toxin. The cell swells, its membrane ruptures and its contents leak into the surroundings, causing inflammation. Apoptosis, or programmed cell death, is a controlled process in which the cell dismantles itself in an orderly way. Its membrane remains intact until the end, and the cell is packaged into small fragments that neighbouring cells or phagocytes remove without inflammation.

Apoptosis is essential for normal development and for the maintenance of tissues. The fingers of a human embryo are initially joined by webbing, and the webbing disappears because the cells in it die. The nervous system produces more neurons than it needs and removes the surplus. Cells that are damaged, infected or no longer needed are removed by apoptosis, which keeps the tissue in balance.

Apoptosis is executed by proteases called caspases, which are produced as inactive precursors and activated in a cascade. The cascade can be triggered by signals from outside the cell, such as the binding of a ligand to a death receptor, or by internal stresses such as DNA damage. The mitochondrion plays a central role in the internal pathway by releasing proteins that activate the caspases. Koshland (2002) described the organising principles of living systems, and the regulated death of cells illustrates how a population of cells is controlled by its members.

Failures of apoptosis contribute to disease. Cancer cells can evade death, allowing damaged cells to survive and divide. Some autoimmune diseases involve the failure to remove self-reactive cells. Conversely, excessive apoptosis can contribute to degenerative diseases of the nervous system and heart.

Apoptosis is studied by staining for characteristic changes such as the exposure of phosphatidylserine on the outer membrane, by measuring caspase activity and by genetic experiments that identify the regulators of the pathway.
