Homeostasis is the maintenance of a stable internal environment. The body must keep its temperature, the pH and ion concentrations of its fluids, its blood pressure and its blood glucose within ranges that its cells can tolerate. Koshland (2002) listed adaptability among the principles of living systems, and homeostasis is the means by which the human body adapts its internal state to a changing external world.

Every homeostatic system has three components. A receptor detects a change in the variable being regulated, such as a rise in body temperature. A control centre, typically in the brain or in an endocrine gland, compares the signal with a set point and decides on a response. An effector carries out the response, such as the sweating or the dilation of blood vessels that cools the body. The three parts are connected by nerves or hormones, and the arrangement can be drawn as a loop.

Most control systems use negative feedback. The response opposes the change that triggered it, so that the variable returns toward the set point. Blood glucose falls when cells take up glucose after a meal, and the rise in glucose triggers insulin release, which lowers glucose again. Negative feedback keeps a variable in a range rather than fixing it at a single value, and it can be fast or slow depending on the system.

Positive feedback amplifies a change and is used less often. Childbirth is an example, in which contractions stimulate the release of oxytocin, which strengthens the contractions until the baby is delivered. Positive feedback is useful for rapid, self-limiting processes, and it can be dangerous when it is not stopped.

Ball (2008) pointed out that the chemistry of body fluids depends on the properties of water and its solutes, and the regulation of these fluids is a central part of homeostasis. The control of osmotic pressure and pH illustrates how physical chemistry underlies physiological control.

Homeostasis is studied by measuring the response of the system to a controlled change, by determining the set point and the range, and by identifying which receptor and effector are responsible for the change.
