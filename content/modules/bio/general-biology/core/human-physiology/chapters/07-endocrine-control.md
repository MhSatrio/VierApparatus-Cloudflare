The endocrine system regulates the body by releasing hormones into the blood. Endocrine glands, such as the thyroid, adrenal glands, pancreas and gonads, secrete hormones directly into the circulation, and the hormones travel to target cells that have receptors for them. The response of a target cell depends on its receptors and on the signalling pathways that they activate, as described in the Cell Biology module. Koshland (2002) included signalling and response among the organising principles of living systems, and the endocrine system is one of the main ways the body coordinates its responses.

Hormones fall into several chemical classes. Steroid hormones, such as cortisol and oestrogen, are lipid-soluble and cross the membrane to bind receptors in the cytoplasm or nucleus, where they regulate transcription. Peptide hormones, such as insulin and glucagon, are water-soluble and bind receptors on the cell surface, triggering intracellular cascades. The chemical class determines how quickly a hormone acts and how long its effect lasts.

The hypothalamus and pituitary form a central control system. The hypothalamus releases hormones that stimulate or inhibit the release of hormones from the pituitary, and the pituitary releases hormones that act on target glands. Thyroid hormone provides a clear feedback loop. The hypothalamus releases a hormone that stimulates the pituitary to release thyroid-stimulating hormone, which stimulates the thyroid to make thyroid hormone. The thyroid hormone then inhibits both the hypothalamus and the pituitary, so the level of the hormone is kept within a range.

Endocrine disorders often result from disturbances in these loops. An overactive gland produces too much hormone, and an underactive gland produces too little. Feedback can also fail, so that the gland is driven continuously by an abnormal signal. Diagnosis depends on measuring the hormone and its regulators together.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the receptors and signalling pathways of hormones, which are studied by binding assays, by genetic deletion of receptors and by measurement of the responses of target cells.
