A skeletal muscle fibre contracts when a motor neuron releases acetylcholine onto its surface. The chemical signal opens channels that depolarise the fibre, and the depolarisation spreads into the interior along invaginations of the membrane called transverse tubules. There it activates a voltage-sensing protein that opens calcium channels in the sarcoplasmic reticulum, a store of calcium surrounding the myofibrils. Calcium released into the cytoplasm binds troponin, which moves tropomyosin off the binding sites on actin, and the myosin heads can then attach and pull, as described in the Zoology module. This sequence is excitation-contraction coupling.

Relaxation occurs when calcium is pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum by an ATP-driven pump. The rapid removal of calcium is necessary, because the contraction continues as long as calcium remains bound to troponin. The speed of relaxation therefore depends on the activity of the pump, and it differs among muscle types.

Each contraction cycle uses ATP, and the muscle must regenerate it continually. At rest, ATP is supplied by the oxidation of fatty acids and glucose in the mitochondria. During intense activity, the demand exceeds the supply, and ATP is generated quickly from the phosphocreatine stored in the muscle and then from glycolysis. Glycolysis produces lactate when oxygen is limited, and the lactate is removed by the liver and heart after exercise.

Fatigue is a decline in the force a muscle can produce. It has several causes, including the depletion of energy stores, the accumulation of hydrogen ions and phosphate, and changes in calcium handling. Lane and Martin (2010) argued that the energy supply of a cell constrains its activity, and muscle fatigue is the clearest everyday demonstration of that constraint.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the proteins that regulate the release and binding of calcium, and the signalling pathways that adjust muscle to training. Muscle physiology is studied by measuring force, by recording electrical activity and by biopsies that reveal the fibre type and its enzymes.
