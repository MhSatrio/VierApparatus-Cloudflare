A nerve impulse is a brief change in the voltage across the membrane of a neuron. At rest the inside of the cell is negative relative to the outside, because potassium leaks out more readily than sodium leaks in. When the membrane is depolarised past a threshold, sodium channels open, and sodium flows into the cell, making the inside positive. Potassium channels then open, and potassium flows out, restoring the resting potential. The sequence takes about a millisecond.

Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) measured the currents in the giant axon of the squid and showed quantitatively how this sequence arises. They held the membrane at fixed voltages and recorded the current, and they separated the current into its sodium and potassium components. Their analysis showed that the conductance of each ion depends on the voltage and on time, and they described the changes with a set of differential equations. The model reproduced the shape and speed of the impulse, and it has remained one of the most successful models in physiology.

The model describes the sodium conductance as rising quickly and then inactivating, and the potassium conductance as rising more slowly and remaining elevated. These two processes give the impulse its characteristic peak and its return to rest. The refractory period that follows an impulse arises from the inactivation of the sodium channels, which prevents a new impulse from starting immediately.

Impulses travel along the axon because the depolarisation at one point spreads to the next and triggers a new impulse there. The speed of conduction depends on the diameter of the axon and on whether it is myelinated. In myelinated axons, the impulse jumps between gaps in the myelin called nodes of Ranvier, which greatly increases the speed.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular structure of the channels and the proteins that generate the gradients on which the impulse depends. The Hodgkin-Huxley framework connects these molecules to the electrical behaviour of the cell.

Impulses are recorded by electrodes in individual neurons and by techniques that record voltage in populations of cells. The model is tested by predicting the response of the membrane to new stimuli and by comparing the predictions with the recordings.
