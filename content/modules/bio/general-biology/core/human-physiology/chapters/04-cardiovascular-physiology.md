The heartbeat begins in the sinoatrial node, a group of specialised cells in the wall of the right atrium that generate electrical impulses spontaneously. The impulse spreads through the atria, causing them to contract, and it reaches the atrioventricular node, which delays it briefly to allow the ventricles to fill. The impulse then travels down the bundle of His and the Purkinje fibres, and the ventricles contract from the apex upward. The pacemaker cells depolarise slowly, and the rate of their depolarisation sets the heart rate.

The electrical events of the heart share the ionic basis that Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) described in nerve. The pacemaker cells have calcium channels that contribute to the upstroke of the impulse, and the working cells of the ventricles have a plateau phase maintained by calcium influx, which prolongs the contraction. The electrocardiogram records the summed electrical activity of these cells on the body surface.

Cardiac output is the volume of blood pumped by each ventricle per minute. It is the product of heart rate and stroke volume, the volume ejected with each beat. Blood pressure depends on cardiac output and on the resistance of the arterioles, which is adjusted by the autonomic nervous system and by local signals. The pressure in the arteries is highest at systole, when the ventricles contract, and lowest at diastole, when the heart relaxes.

The circulation adjusts flow to the needs of each tissue. Active muscle dilates its arterioles in response to metabolites such as carbon dioxide and potassium, increasing blood flow to match its demand. Other tissues receive less blood at rest, and the distribution of cardiac output changes between rest and exercise.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular basis of the contractile proteins in the heart, which are similar to those of skeletal muscle. Cardiovascular function is measured by pressure and flow recordings, by imaging of the heart and by the electrical activity recorded in the electrocardiogram.
