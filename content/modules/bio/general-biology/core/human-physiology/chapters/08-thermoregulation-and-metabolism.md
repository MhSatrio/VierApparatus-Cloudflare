Human body temperature is held near 37 degrees Celsius by balancing heat production and heat loss. Heat is produced mainly by the metabolism of the tissues, especially the liver and skeletal muscle, and by shivering, which uses muscle activity to generate heat. Heat is lost by radiation, conduction, convection and evaporation of sweat. The hypothalamus compares the core temperature with its set point and triggers responses that increase heat production or loss as needed.

The thermoregulatory responses are effectors of the homeostatic system described in the Homeostasis chapter. In the cold, blood vessels in the skin constrict to reduce heat loss, muscles shiver, and hormones increase metabolic rate. In the heat, the vessels dilate to bring warm blood to the surface and the sweat glands secrete. Evaporation of sweat removes a large amount of heat, and it is effective only when the air is not saturated with water.

Metabolic rate is the rate at which the body uses energy. The basal metabolic rate is the energy used at rest, after fasting, in a neutral environment. It is set largely by the mass of metabolically active tissue, and it accounts for most of the energy used each day. Total metabolic rate adds the energy of activity, digestion and growth. Thyroid hormone raises basal metabolic rate, and its effect is one reason that thyroid disorders alter weight and temperature.

Lane and Martin (2010) argued that the energy available to a cell constrains its complexity, and the human body operates within a similar energetic budget. The energy used by the body is supplied entirely by food, and the balance between intake and expenditure is maintained over time.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biochemical pathways that generate heat and the mitochondrial processes that release energy as heat rather than ATP. Thermoregulation and metabolism are studied by calorimetry, by measurement of oxygen consumption and by monitoring of body temperature under controlled conditions.
