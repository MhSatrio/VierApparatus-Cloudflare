The kidneys filter the blood and produce urine, which removes waste products and regulates the volume and composition of body fluids. Each kidney contains about a million nephrons, the functional units that carry out three processes. Filtration takes place in the glomerulus, where blood pressure forces water and small solutes through the capillary wall into the tubule. Reabsorption returns most of the filtered water and useful solutes to the blood, and secretion adds waste products and some ions from the blood into the tubule. The urine is what remains.

Reabsorption is selective and energy-consuming. Glucose and amino acids are reabsorbed almost completely through co-transporters that use the sodium gradient maintained by ATP-driven pumps. Sodium is reabsorbed along most of the tubule, and water follows osmotically. Ball (2008) described the role of water in cell biology, and the movement of water across the tubule depends on the osmotic gradient that the transport of salts creates.

Water balance is controlled by antidiuretic hormone, released by the posterior pituitary when the blood becomes concentrated. The hormone increases the insertion of water channels into the membrane of the collecting duct, which allows more water to be reabsorbed and produces concentrated urine. When the blood is dilute, less hormone is released, and the kidney excretes a large volume of dilute urine.

The kidneys also regulate the acid-base balance of the body. They excrete hydrogen ions and reabsorb bicarbonate, which complements the action of the lungs in controlling pH. The same chemistry of buffers described in the Water, Acids, Bases and pH module underlies this regulation.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the transporters that move ions and water across the epithelium of the nephron, and their regulation by hormones. Renal function is measured by the clearance of substances from the blood, by analysis of urine and by imaging of the kidney.
