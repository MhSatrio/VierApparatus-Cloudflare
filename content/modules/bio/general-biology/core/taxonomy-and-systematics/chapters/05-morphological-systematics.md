Morphological systematics uses the structure of organisms as characters. Before molecular data were available, it was the only source of evidence for most relationships, and it remains essential for groups that cannot be sequenced, including most fossils. Morphological characters include features of the skeleton, the organs, the external form and the development of the embryo. Each character must be defined clearly, so that two observers would score it the same way in the same organism.

Coding a character means assigning each organism a state. A character such as the number of legs has states of zero, four or six, and the states are chosen to reflect the biology. Characters with many states, or with states that are hard to define, introduce subjectivity, and systematists try to minimise it by using explicit definitions and by recording the reasons for each decision.

Darwin (1859) argued that the homologies of structures, such as the bones of the forelimb in vertebrates, reveal shared descent, and morphological systematics uses these homologies. The same reasoning applies to fossils. A fossil that shares derived characters with a living group is evidence for its place in the tree, and fossils can date the branches of the tree by showing when a lineage first appeared.

Fossils present problems. They are incomplete, their preservation affects which characters can be scored, and they may show combinations of features that do not match any living group. Including fossils can change the placement of living organisms, because a fossil may connect groups that look distinct today.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the developmental basis of many morphological features, which helps to decide whether a similarity is homologous or the product of convergence. Development provides independent evidence for the characters used in a tree.

Morphological data are collected by measurement, by observation of specimens, by imaging such as micro-computed tomography and by the examination of fossils in collections.
