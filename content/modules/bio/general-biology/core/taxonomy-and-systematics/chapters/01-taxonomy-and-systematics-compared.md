Taxonomy is the science of naming and classifying organisms. It assigns each organism to a group, gives the group a name, and records the rules by which the name and the group are defined. Systematics is the study of the diversity and relationships of organisms, and it uses evidence about their history to decide which groups are real. Taxonomy depends on systematics for its groups, and systematics depends on taxonomy for the names by which its results are communicated.

Darwin (1859) argued that the natural system of classification is one that reflects common descent. A group defined on that basis would include an ancestor and all its descendants, and it would tell the reader something about the history of its members. Systematics provides the evidence that identifies such groups, and taxonomy makes them usable.

The distinction matters because classifications can be built for different purposes. A classification based on the appearance of organisms may be useful for identification in the field, but it may split or merge lineages that are not closely related. Woese and colleagues (1990) showed that the division of prokaryotes into bacteria and archaea was not visible in their appearance, and that a classification based on ribosomal RNA gave a different and more accurate picture.

In practice the two activities are linked. A new species is described by taxonomists who compare its features with those of known species and place it within a group. Systematists test whether the group reflects a clade by building trees from independent data. If the tree supports a different grouping, the classification is revised.

The chapters that follow describe how the evidence is collected and analysed, and how the rules of naming are applied.
