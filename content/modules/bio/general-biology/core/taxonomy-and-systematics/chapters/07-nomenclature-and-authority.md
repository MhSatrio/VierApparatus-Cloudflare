Scientific names are governed by international codes, separate for animals, plants, fungi, bacteria and viruses. The codes exist so that a name refers to the same group for all users, and they set out how names are formed, published and changed. Each code has its own rules, although they share principles, and a name that is valid under one code may not be recognised under another.

The principle of priority is the central rule in most codes. When a group is named more than once, the earliest valid name is used, and later names become synonyms. Priority ensures that names do not change arbitrarily, but it can produce changes when an older name is found for a well-known group. The codes provide exceptions to preserve names that are in common use.

A type specimen is the specimen to which a name is permanently attached. When a species is described, the describer designates a holotype or other type, and this specimen fixes what the name means. If later research shows that the group is different from what was assumed, the name still refers to the type. The type concept gives stability to names, and it is one of the main reasons museum collections are important to taxonomy.

The authority of a name is the person or persons who published it, and it is often written after the name, as in Homo sapiens Linnaeus, 1758. The authority allows the name to be traced to its original publication, and it helps readers find the description on which a name rests.

Alberts et al. (2022) use scientific names in the description of model organisms, which allows readers to identify the organism exactly. The rules of nomenclature are the means by which that precision is achieved in the literature.

Names are checked against the databases maintained by the codes and by taxonomic authorities, and the literature is searched for earlier names and for descriptions of type specimens.
