Classifications are provisional. A new fossil, a new sequence or a new analysis can show that a group is not natural, or that two groups should be combined. Revision is the normal way that systematics improves, and each revision must be argued from evidence and accepted by other investigators. The process is slower than the arrival of the data, because proposed changes are tested and debated before they are adopted.

Hug and colleagues (2016) showed how new genomic data could change the picture of the tree of life, adding lineages and altering the relationships among groups that had been assumed. Their revision was based on a large number of genes, and it was judged by its consistency across them. A revision that rests on one gene or on one analysis is more vulnerable to error.

Support values are part of the argument. Felsenstein (1985) introduced bootstrap values as a measure of confidence in a branch, and a proposed revision is more persuasive when the branches it depends on are well supported. A revision that changes a well-supported branch must explain why the earlier support was misleading, and this explanation is itself a test.

Databases record the information on which classifications rest. Nomenclatural databases store the names and their authorities, specimen databases link names to the physical specimens that define them, and sequence databases store the genes from which trees are built. The databases allow other researchers to check claims, reanalyse data and build on earlier work.

Woese and colleagues (1990) based their classification on ribosomal RNA sequences that other investigators could compare and check. The same principle applies today: a classification is only as trustworthy as the records on which it rests, so keeping those records accurate and accessible is part of the scientific process, not an administrative task.

Revisions are judged by the same standards as other scientific claims: the evidence must be reproducible, the analysis must be transparent and the conclusion must be consistent with independent data.
