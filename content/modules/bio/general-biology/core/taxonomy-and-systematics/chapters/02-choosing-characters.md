A phylogeny is built from characters, which are features that can be compared among organisms. Characters can be morphological, such as the presence of a shell, or molecular, such as the base at a particular position in a gene. The choice of characters is the most important decision in a systematic study, because the tree can only reflect the history recorded in the characters chosen.

A homologous character is one inherited from a common ancestor, and it is the kind of character that carries information about history. An analogous character is similar in two organisms but was evolved independently. Wings of birds and insects are analogous, because they arose separately, while the forelimb bones of birds and mammals are homologous. Only homologous characters can be used to infer ancestry, and the distinction is therefore central to systematics.

Within homologous characters, a shared derived character is one that appeared in a recent ancestor and was passed to its descendants. A shared primitive character was present in the ancestor of a larger group and is shared with others outside the group. Only shared derived characters support a grouping. A group defined by a shared primitive character may include organisms that are not closely related.

Homoplasy is the appearance of similar characters through evolutionary convergence or reversal, and it is common. Hug and colleagues (2016) used sequences that are less prone to convergence to place many lineages in the tree, which reduces the risk of grouping organisms that share a feature for independent reasons. Darwin (1859) noted that the appearance of organisms can be shaped by their way of life, and similarity in outward form is an unreliable guide to relationship.

Good practice is to use many characters from independent sources, to examine each character for signs of homoplasy and to report the evidence for each proposed grouping. Molecular characters are often numerous and less susceptible to convergence, but they must still be checked, because the same base may change more than once in a lineage.

Characters are assessed by comparing their distribution among organisms, by examining their developmental or genetic basis and by testing whether the grouping they suggest is supported by other data.
