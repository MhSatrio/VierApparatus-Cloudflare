The sequences of genes and proteins are now the main characters used in systematics. Each site in a sequence is a character, and the bases or amino acids at each site can be compared across organisms. Molecular data have several advantages. They are available in large numbers, they can be compared across all organisms, and they are less affected by the environment than many morphological features.

The choice of gene depends on the depth of the relationship being studied. Woese and colleagues (1990) used the small subunit of ribosomal RNA, which is conserved enough to link the domains of life. For relationships within a genus, faster-evolving genes or non-coding regions may be more informative, because the slower genes have not changed enough to distinguish close relatives. The rule is to choose a gene whose rate of change matches the time since the groups diverged.

Several problems can mislead molecular phylogenies. Multiple substitutions at the same site can hide the true number of changes, and this saturation makes distant relationships hard to resolve. Different genes can have different histories, because of recombination, horizontal transfer or incomplete lineage sorting, so that the tree of one gene differs from the tree of another. Hug and colleagues (2016) reported the difficulty of placing lineages that are represented only by fragmentary genomes, where the data for some genes are missing.

Alignment of sequences is another source of error. The sequences must be aligned so that homologous positions are compared, and small differences in the alignment can change the tree. Biologists check alignments by eye and by testing whether the result is stable when the alignment is varied.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular basis of mutation and selection, which determines the rate of change of each gene and the constraints on its sequence. Understanding these processes helps the systematist to choose appropriate genes and models.

Molecular systematics is practised by sequencing genes from many organisms, aligning the sequences, building trees by the methods of the previous chapter and comparing the results across genes.
