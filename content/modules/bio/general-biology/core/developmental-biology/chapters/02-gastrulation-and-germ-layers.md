Gastrulation is the process that transforms the simple blastula into an embryo with three germ layers. Cells move from the surface into the interior, rearrange themselves and form the layers that will give rise to every tissue of the body. The movements are coordinated by changes in cell shape and adhesion, and by signals that tell each cell where it is. Gastrulation is one of the most dramatic events of development, and errors at this stage are usually lethal.

Several cell movements contribute. Invagination is the folding of a sheet of cells into the interior, as in the formation of the archenteron of a sea urchin. Ingression is the individual detachment of cells from the surface into the interior. Epiboly is the spreading of a sheet of cells over the surface of the embryo. Convergent extension narrows a tissue in one direction and lengthens it in another. Each movement reshapes the embryo, and many occur together in the same embryo.

The germ layers are the ectoderm, which lies outside and gives rise to the epidermis and nervous system, the mesoderm, which lies in between and gives rise to muscle, bone, blood vessels and the kidneys, and the endoderm, which lies inside and forms the lining of the gut, the lungs and associated glands. These assignments are broadly conserved among animals, and they reflect the evolutionary origin of the tissues.

Darwin (1859) described the similarity of embryos among vertebrates, and the layers of the embryo show that similarity. Animals that differ greatly in their adult form pass through similar stages, and the layers provide the common scaffold on which the adult body is built.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling and adhesion molecules that control gastrulation. The movements are studied by labelling cells and tracing their paths, by live imaging of the embryo and by mutation of the proteins that control cell shape.
