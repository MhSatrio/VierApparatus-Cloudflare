A stem cell is defined by two properties. It can divide to produce more stem cells, which is called self-renewal, and it can produce differentiated cells of one or more types. These properties allow tissues to be maintained throughout life. A stem cell that divides asymmetrically gives rise to one stem cell and one committed daughter, which keeps the stem cell population stable while supplying new cells to the tissue.

The niche is the local environment that maintains stem cells. It contains supporting cells, extracellular matrix and signals that keep stem cells in their undifferentiated state. When a stem cell moves out of the niche, or when the signals change, the cell begins to differentiate. The niche therefore acts as a control point, and its disruption can cause loss of stem cells or uncontrolled growth.

The haematopoietic stem cells of the bone marrow, described in the Histology module, are the best-known example. They produce all the blood cell types throughout life, and their numbers are maintained by signals from the marrow environment. Medzhitov and Janeway (2002) described how the innate immune cells that arise from these stem cells recognise microbes, and the production of these cells must respond to infection.

Differentiation involves the switching on of a specific set of genes and the silencing of others. Transcription factors and chromatin changes stabilise the new state, so that a differentiated cell maintains its identity through divisions. The process is reversible in some cases, as shown by the reprogramming of cells to a pluripotent state, but it is normally stable.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling pathways and transcription factors that control the balance between self-renewal and differentiation. Stem cells are studied by lineage tracing, which follows the progeny of labelled cells, by sorting cells according to surface markers and by transplantation experiments.
