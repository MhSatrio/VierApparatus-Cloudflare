The limbs of vertebrates develop from buds that form on the flanks of the embryo. A bud is a small outgrowth of mesoderm covered by ectoderm, and it grows by cell proliferation driven by signals from the tissue around it. The limb has three axes: proximal to distal, from the body outward; anterior to posterior, from the thumb side to the little finger side; and dorsal to ventral, from the back of the hand to the palm. Each axis is specified by a distinct source of signals.

The proximal-distal axis is controlled by a specialised ectoderm at the tip of the bud, the apical ectodermal ridge, which secretes signals that keep the underlying mesoderm growing. As the bud extends, cells that were formed earlier are left behind and become the more proximal parts of the limb, so the order of structures follows the timing of their formation. The anterior-posterior axis is set by a region of mesoderm called the zone of polarising activity, which secretes a signal that specifies the identity of the digits, with the first digit forming nearest the source.

Darwin (1859) argued that the limbs of vertebrates share a common arrangement despite their different functions. The bones of the forelimb of humans, bats, whales and horses follow the same plan, which suggests a common developmental programme that has been modified in each lineage. The development of the limb supports this view, because the same signals and genes operate in all vertebrate limbs.

The number of digits varies among vertebrates, and changes in the signals that specify digit identity can produce extra or missing digits. These changes provide a developmental route for the evolution of limbs with different numbers of digits, and they illustrate how regulatory changes can alter form.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling molecules of the limb and the transcription factors that respond to them. Limb development is studied by grafting tissues from one part of the bud to another, by genetic manipulation of the signals and by imaging the growing bud.
