Regeneration is the regrowth of a lost or damaged part. Some animals regenerate extensively. Planarian flatworms can regrow a whole body from a small fragment, and salamanders can regrow limbs, tails and parts of the eye. The capacity depends on stem cells or on the ability of differentiated cells to return to a proliferative state. The cells that form the new tissue come either from a population of resident stem cells or from the dedifferentiation of cells at the wound.

In planarians, a population of pluripotent stem cells is distributed throughout the body and provides the cells needed for regrowth. The position of the cut determines which structures are rebuilt, because positional information in the surrounding tissue directs the new growth. In salamanders, cells near the wound dedifferentiate and form a mass called the blastema, which then grows and differentiates into the missing parts, following the patterning rules of the original limb.

Mammals regenerate some tissues, such as the liver, which can restore its mass after partial removal, and the skin, whose epidermis is renewed continually. They regenerate poorly in other tissues, such as the heart and the central nervous system, where injury typically leaves scar tissue. Scarring is part of the response to injury, and it prevents the tissue from recovering its original form.

Medzhitov and Janeway (2002) described how innate cells recognise microbial patterns and respond to danger, and the inflammation that follows injury can influence whether regeneration or scarring follows. The balance of signals from innate cells is one of the factors that determine the outcome of repair.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the stem cells of the adult tissues that renew them. The mechanisms of regeneration are studied by amputation and tracing of labelled cells, by genetic manipulation of the signals and by comparison of regenerating and non-regenerating species.

The lesson of regeneration is that the capacity to rebuild is a property of the organism's cell types and signals. Understanding why some animals regrow and others do not is one of the central questions of developmental biology.
