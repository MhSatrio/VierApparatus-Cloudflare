A morphogen is a signalling molecule that is produced at a source and spreads through a tissue, forming a gradient of concentration. Cells at different distances from the source experience different concentrations, and they respond by switching on different sets of genes. The gradient therefore gives each cell information about its position, a principle called positional information. The idea was developed to explain how a uniform field of cells can become a structured embryo.

The gradient forms through diffusion or transport. A molecule secreted from one end of a tissue spreads, and it is degraded or taken up as it moves, so that its concentration falls with distance. The shape of the gradient depends on the rate of diffusion, the rate of degradation and the position of the source. Mutations that change any of these parameters alter the pattern of the embryo.

Cells interpret the gradient by thresholds. A cell that experiences a concentration above a certain value switches on one gene, and one that experiences a lower concentration switches on another. The thresholds divide the tissue into bands, and each band adopts a distinct fate. The boundaries between bands are sharp, even though the gradient is smooth, because each cell makes a binary decision.

Nüsslein-Volhard and Wieschaus (1980) identified many of the genes that establish the gradients in the fruit fly embryo. Their screen revealed maternal genes that set up the axes and the gradients of signalling molecules that define the segments. The work showed how a genetic screen can reveal the logic of a developmental system.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular nature of morphogens, which include proteins of several families, and the receptors and transcription factors that read them. Gradients are measured by fluorescent labelling of the morphogen, by quantifying the response of target genes and by experiments that alter the source.
