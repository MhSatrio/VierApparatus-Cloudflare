The body of the fruit fly larva is divided into fourteen segments, each with a characteristic pattern of structures. The segments are established in the early embryo by a hierarchy of genes. Maternal genes deposited in the egg set up the axes and the first gradients. Gap genes are expressed in broad domains that divide the embryo into large regions. Pair-rule genes are expressed in stripes that mark every second segment, and segment-polarity genes refine the stripes and define the anterior and posterior edge of each segment.

Nüsslein-Volhard and Wieschaus (1980) carried out a systematic screen for mutations that changed the number or polarity of segments. They examined the cuticle of the larvae, which preserves the segmental pattern, and identified mutations that deleted segments, duplicated them or reversed their polarity. The genes they found were then assigned to classes according to the pattern of their defects, and the hierarchy of classes followed from the way the mutations interacted.

The hierarchy works because each class of gene regulates the next. A gap gene product switches on a pair-rule gene in a stripe, and the stripe of pair-rule expression then activates segment-polarity genes in a narrower domain. The cascade converts a smooth maternal gradient into a sharp, repeated pattern, and each step increases the resolution of the pattern.

The discovery was remarkable because the genes could be placed in a logical sequence, and their molecular identities were later found to encode transcription factors and signalling molecules. The pattern was shown to be conserved across many animals, including vertebrates, which suggests a shared origin of the segmentation mechanism.

Darwin (1859) described the segmentation of animal bodies as a common feature among groups with very different forms, and the Drosophila screen provided a mechanistic explanation for it. The identity of segments is controlled by regulatory genes, and changes to those genes can alter the number and form of segments in evolution.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the transcription factors and signalling pathways that implement the cascade. The pattern is studied by staining embryos for gene products, by mutagenesis and by live imaging of the stripes as they form.
