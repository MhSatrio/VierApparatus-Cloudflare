Organs form when cells from different germ layers interact. Organogenesis depends on reciprocal induction, in which one tissue sends a signal that changes the fate of a neighbouring tissue, and the neighbour sends a signal back. The interaction between tissues specifies the organ's position, its size and its structure. The sequence of inductions is precise, and an error at one step can prevent the organ from forming or change its shape.

The heart develops from a tube of mesoderm that folds and loops. Precursor cells from two sides of the embryo fuse at the midline, and the tube then bends into a looped shape, which places the chambers in their final positions. The valves and septa form from the cushions of tissue within the tube, and the coordination of these processes produces a four-chambered heart in birds and mammals.

The eye develops from a bulge of the forebrain that makes contact with the surface ectoderm. The lens forms from the ectoderm in response to a signal from the optic cup, and the optic cup itself forms from the neural tissue. The interaction between the two tissues specifies the form of each, and the lens and retina are formed by reciprocal inductions.

The kidney develops from two sources in the mesoderm. The ureteric bud grows into a mass of metanephric mesenchyme, and the interaction between the two induces the formation of nephrons. Each branch of the bud induces nearby mesenchyme to form a nephron, and the tips of the bud continue to branch, which produces the complex tree of the collecting duct system.

Darwin (1859) described the development of organs as the modification of structures from common ancestors, and the similarity of organ development across vertebrates supports this view. The eyes and kidneys of different animals follow similar developmental routes, which suggests common origins.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling molecules and transcription factors that control organ formation. Organogenesis is studied by tissue recombination experiments, in which tissues are combined to see whether one can induce the other, and by genetic mutations that disrupt organ formation.
