Fertilisation activates an egg that has been arrested in meiosis. The sperm contributes its genome and a centriole, and it triggers a wave of calcium release within the egg. The calcium signal completes the meiotic division, and it triggers a block to polyspermy, which prevents additional sperm from entering. In many animals the block is fast, through a change in membrane potential, and then slow, through the release of enzymes that alter the egg coat. The egg then becomes competent to divide.

The first divisions after fertilisation are called cleavage. Unlike ordinary cell divisions, cleavage divides the cytoplasm without growth, so the cells become progressively smaller. The embryo remains roughly the size of the egg while the number of cells increases. The cell cycle during cleavage is rapid, with few gap phases, and it is driven by stored proteins and mRNAs that the egg received during oogenesis. The embryo uses these maternal stores before its own genome is activated.

The pattern of cleavage varies among animals. In sea urchins it is radially symmetrical, while in many other animals the planes of division follow the distribution of yolk and the position of the egg's internal structures. The cleavage pattern sets up the arrangement of cells that will later form the germ layers, and the differences reflect the amount and position of yolk.

Nüsslein-Volhard and Wieschaus (1980) reported mutations in Drosophila that changed the number and polarity of segments, and their work showed that the early embryo is organised by genes acting from the egg onward. The maternal and zygotic genes they identified define the axes of the embryo.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular events of egg activation and the regulation of the cell cycle during cleavage. Early development is studied by imaging the embryo with fluorescent markers, by microinjecting molecules and by mutations that alter the division pattern.
