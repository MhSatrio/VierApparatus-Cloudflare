Translation converts the nucleotide sequence of messenger RNA into the amino acid sequence of a protein. The ribosome moves along the messenger RNA, reading it in groups of three bases called codons. Each codon specifies one amino acid or a signal to stop. Transfer RNAs carry the amino acids and have anticodons that pair with the codons, so the correct amino acid is brought to the ribosome for each position in the chain.

The genetic code has sixty-one codons that specify amino acids and three that signal the end of the chain. The code is described as degenerate, because most amino acids are specified by more than one codon. Degeneracy often falls in the third position of the codon, so that synonymous mutations at that position do not change the protein. The code is nearly universal across the tree of life, which is a strong sign of a common origin of the cellular machinery.

The ribosome has three sites for transfer RNA. The incoming aminoacyl transfer RNA binds to the acceptor site, a peptide bond forms between the new amino acid and the growing chain, and the ribosome then moves one codon along the messenger. The large subunit catalyses the peptide bond, and the reaction is driven by the energy stored in the transfer RNA.

Initiation requires a start codon, usually AUG, and an initiator transfer RNA that carries methionine in bacteria and eukaryotes. Termination occurs when a stop codon enters the acceptor site, and a release factor triggers the hydrolysis of the completed chain from the transfer RNA.

Woese and colleagues (1990) used the ribosomal RNA of the ribosome to build the tree of life, and the conserved structure of the translation machinery supports the view that the code and its machinery originated once. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how translation is regulated at initiation, which is where most control is exerted.

Translation is measured by incorporating labelled amino acids into newly made proteins, by profiling ribosome-bound messenger RNA, and by structural studies of the ribosome in complex with its substrates.
