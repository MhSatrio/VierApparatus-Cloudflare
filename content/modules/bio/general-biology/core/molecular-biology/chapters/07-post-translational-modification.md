After a polypeptide is synthesised, it is often chemically modified. The modifications can change its activity, its location, its interactions with other molecules, and the time it survives in the cell. More than two hundred types of modification are known, and many proteins carry several at once. The modification is added or removed by enzymes, so the state of the protein can change in response to signals.

Phosphorylation is the most widely studied. A kinase adds a phosphate group to a serine, threonine or tyrosine residue, and a phosphatase removes it. The added phosphate carries two negative charges, which alter the local structure of the protein and create or destroy binding sites. Many signalling pathways are organised around phosphorylation cascades.

Other modifications include glycosylation, in which sugars are attached to proteins, usually in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus, and ubiquitination, in which the small protein ubiquitin is attached to a target to mark it for destruction or for a change of function. Acetylation of lysine residues changes the charge of histones and influences how tightly DNA is packed. Proteolytic cleavage removes part of a precursor to produce an active protein, as in the activation of digestive enzymes.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the sequence of a protein determines its native fold, and modifications add a further layer of information. Modified residues can change the fold, and the modified form may be the one that is correctly folded and active.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how cells use modifications to respond quickly to change, since a phosphorylation or cleavage can happen in seconds, while the synthesis of a new protein takes minutes. The speed of these switches makes them central to cell regulation.

Modifications are detected by mass spectrometry, which identifies the mass shift produced by each modification, by antibodies that recognise a modified residue, and by genetic mutation of the modified site to test its function.
