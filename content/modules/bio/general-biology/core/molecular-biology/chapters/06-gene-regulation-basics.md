Cells control which genes they express. A bacterium that lives in an environment rich in lactose does not need to make the enzymes that break it down, and it saves energy by keeping those genes off until lactose appears. Jacob and Monod (1961) studied this control in Escherichia coli and described the lac operon, a cluster of genes for lactose metabolism that is regulated as a single unit.

The operon is controlled by a repressor protein. In the absence of lactose, the repressor binds to a sequence called the operator, which lies next to the promoter, and blocks RNA polymerase from transcribing the genes. When lactose or a related compound is present, it binds the repressor and changes its shape, so the repressor releases the operator and transcription proceeds. This is negative control: the regulator stops transcription, and the inducing signal removes the block.

The lac operon is also subject to positive control. When glucose is scarce, the level of a signalling molecule rises, and the complex it forms with a regulatory protein binds to a site near the promoter. This complex helps RNA polymerase bind efficiently. Positive control allows the cell to produce the enzymes most effectively when they are most needed.

Gene regulation in eukaryotes is more elaborate. Transcription factors bind to enhancers, which may be far from the promoter, and chromatin modifications alter how accessible the DNA is. Many genes are controlled by combinations of signals, so that the expression pattern reflects the state of the whole cell.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the basic logic of the lac operon, of a signal that changes the activity of a regulator, appears throughout biology. The same logic applies to hormone responses, development and the immune system.

Regulation is studied by fusing the promoter of a gene to a reporter, by measuring transcription under different conditions and by mutating the regulatory sites to see which ones are required.
