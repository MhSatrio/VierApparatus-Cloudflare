Watson and Crick (1953) proposed that each strand of DNA could serve as a template for a new partner. This implies that replication produces two double helices, each containing one old strand and one new strand. Meselson and Stahl (1958) tested the idea directly. They grew Escherichia coli in a medium containing a heavy isotope of nitrogen, transferred the cells to a normal medium, and used density gradient centrifugation to separate DNA molecules by weight. The hybrid molecules that appeared after one generation confirmed that replication is semiconservative.

Replication begins at specific origins, where the two strands are separated locally. The separation creates a replication fork, which moves along the chromosome. Helicase unwinds the double helix ahead of the fork, and single-strand binding proteins keep the strands apart so they can be copied. The helix is relieved of its torsional strain by topoisomerases, which cut and rejoin the strands.

DNA polymerase adds nucleotides to the growing strand, reading the template and selecting the complementary base. It can add nucleotides only to an existing chain with a free three-prime hydroxyl, so each new strand begins with a short primer made by primase. Because the two strands run in opposite directions, one strand is copied continuously and the other in short fragments that are joined later by DNA ligase.

Copying is accurate because the polymerase checks each base it adds, and a separate proofreading activity removes incorrect nucleotides. Mismatch repair systems correct errors that escape proofreading. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how these steps combine to give an error rate far lower than the rate at which the polymerase would make mistakes on its own.

Replication is studied by labelling DNA with isotopes, by electron microscopy of replication intermediates, and by biochemical reconstitution with purified enzymes.
