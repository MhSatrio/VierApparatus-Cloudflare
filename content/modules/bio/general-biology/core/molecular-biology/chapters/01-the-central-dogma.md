The central dogma of molecular biology describes how genetic information is used in cells. DNA is copied to make more DNA in replication. Its sequence is copied to make RNA in transcription. The RNA sequence is then read to make protein in translation. Information flows from nucleic acid to protein, and it does not flow back from protein to nucleic acid. Watson and Crick (1953) established the structural basis for the first step, and the later work on the genetic code established the rest.

Jacob and Monod (1961) gave an account of how the synthesis of proteins is regulated, showing that the production of messenger RNA can be switched on and off by molecules that bind to DNA. Their model of the lac operon described a mechanism that allowed bacteria to adjust their enzyme production to the available food, and it provided the framework for much of later gene regulation.

The dogma has exceptions. Retroviruses carry RNA genomes and use the enzyme reverse transcriptase to copy them into DNA, which is then integrated into the host genome. Some RNA viruses replicate their genomes directly from RNA. Prions are proteins that can change the conformation of other proteins without any nucleic acid involved in the transmission of the altered state. These exceptions show that information can flow in more than one direction under certain conditions, but the general pattern holds for the great majority of cellular genes.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the central dogma as a useful organising framework that is refined by each new discovery. The dogma does not describe the whole of gene expression, since regulation, RNA processing and modification of proteins add layers that the simple scheme leaves out.

The dogma is tested by tracing the steps of the pathway with labelled precursors, by sequencing the nucleic acids and proteins involved, and by genetic experiments that block one step and observe the consequences for the next.
