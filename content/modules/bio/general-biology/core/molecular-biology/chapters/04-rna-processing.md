The RNA made by transcription in eukaryotes is not ready to be translated. The primary transcript, or pre-mRNA, is modified in three ways. A cap of modified guanine is added to the five-prime end, which protects the RNA from degradation and helps the ribosome recognise it. A tail of adenine residues is added to the three-prime end in a process called polyadenylation, which also protects the RNA and promotes its export. The most extensive change is splicing, in which the intervening sequences called introns are removed and the coding sequences called exons are joined.

Splicing is carried out by the spliceosome, a large complex of proteins and small nuclear RNAs. Signals at the intron boundaries and within the intron direct the spliceosome to cut and rejoin the RNA with great precision. The chemistry of the reaction involves the attack of a hydroxyl group on a phosphodiester bond, followed by a second attack that joins the exons, and it does not require external energy beyond what is stored in the RNA.

Alternative splicing allows a single gene to produce several different messenger RNAs by including or excluding particular exons. The same pre-mRNA can therefore encode several proteins, which differ in their domains or regulation. Alternative splicing is common in animals and plants, and it contributes greatly to the diversity of proteins without an equivalent increase in the number of genes.

Watson and Crick (1953) established the base-pairing rules that govern the recognition of RNA sequences by complementary molecules, and splicing uses the same type of pairing between the spliceosomal RNAs and the pre-mRNA. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how these base-pairing interactions are combined with protein binding to achieve the specificity that the process requires.

Processing is studied by comparing the sequences of nuclear and cytoplasmic RNA, by sequencing the junctions between exons, and by biochemical reconstitution of the spliceosome from purified components.
