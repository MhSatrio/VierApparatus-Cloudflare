Molecular biology became a practical discipline when methods were developed to manipulate and measure nucleic acids. This chapter introduces the main techniques, their principles and the questions each one answers. The details of individual protocols are learned in the laboratory, but the logic of each method is what allows a result to be interpreted.

Polymerase chain reaction copies a specific segment of DNA exponentially. Two short primers flank the target, and repeated cycles of heating to separate the strands, cooling to let the primers bind and extension by a heat-stable polymerase double the amount of target at each cycle. The method can detect a few copies of a sequence, and it is the basis of diagnostic tests and of many research experiments. It relies on the base-pairing rules that Watson and Crick (1953) established.

Cloning inserts a DNA fragment into a vector, such as a plasmid, which is then introduced into bacteria. Each bacterium that takes up the vector makes copies of the insert, so a single molecule can be amplified into a large population. Meselson and Stahl (1958) showed that bacteria copy their DNA semiconservatively, which is the process that cloning vectors rely on each time a host cell divides.

Blotting methods detect specific nucleic acids or proteins. In a Southern blot, DNA fragments are separated by size, transferred to a membrane and detected with a labelled probe. Northern and western blots apply the same principle to RNA and protein.

DNA sequencing reads the order of bases. Modern methods sequence millions of fragments in parallel, and the data are assembled into genomes. Gene editing uses enzymes that cut DNA at a chosen site, allowing a sequence to be inactivated or replaced.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe these methods in the context of the experiments that they made possible. Each technique answers a specific question, and combining several of them provides the evidence needed to establish a molecular mechanism.
