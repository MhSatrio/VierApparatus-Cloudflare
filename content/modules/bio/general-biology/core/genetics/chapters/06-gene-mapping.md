Gene mapping determines the position of genes on chromosomes. The earliest maps were linkage maps, built from the recombination frequencies between pairs of genes, as described in the chapter on linkage and recombination. Genes that recombine rarely are placed close together, and genes that recombine often are placed far apart. The resulting map gives the order and relative distance of genes, but not their physical positions in base pairs.

Physical maps locate genes in terms of the actual length of DNA. They are built by cutting the genome into fragments, identifying overlapping clones and ordering them along the chromosome. Physical maps can be made more precise than linkage maps and can reveal gene positions that the recombination data cannot resolve. They also show the physical structure of regions where recombination is suppressed.

The International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium (2001) reported the first draft sequence of the human genome, and this sequence provides the most detailed physical map of any human chromosome. The sequence allows the position of every known gene to be given in base pairs, and it makes it possible to compare linkage distances with physical distances. The two differ, because recombination rates vary along each chromosome.

Maps are used to find genes responsible for inherited disease. A family in which a disease segregates is genotyped for markers across the genome, and the markers that travel with the disease in the affected individuals point to the region that contains the gene. The region is then narrowed by further markers and sequencing.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the genome is organised into genes, regulatory regions and repetitive sequences, and how these features are identified in a physical map. The mapping of genes is therefore a bridge between genetics and the molecular structure of the genome.

Maps are constructed from pedigrees and crosses, from physical clones and from sequence data, and their accuracy is checked by comparing the independent methods.
