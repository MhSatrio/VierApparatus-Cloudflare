Heredity follows rules that can be predicted from counting. The principle of segregation states that each individual carries two copies of each gene, one from each parent, and that the two copies separate when gametes are formed, so each gamete receives one copy. When an individual carrying two different alleles is crossed with another such individual, the offspring appear in predictable proportions. In the classic monohybrid cross, three quarters show the dominant trait and one quarter show the recessive trait, and the ratio is one to two to one for the genotypes.

The principle of independent assortment states that the segregation of one gene does not influence the segregation of another gene on a different chromosome. A dihybrid cross between two heterozygous parents produces nine offspring of one kind for every sixteen, in the common ratio of nine to three to three to one. The ratio arises because each gene segregates independently of the other, so the four possible gamete types appear in equal proportions.

These rules were derived from careful counting of progeny. They are a prediction that can fail, and the failures are informative. A deviation from a predicted ratio suggests that the genes are linked, that the trait depends on more than one gene, or that some offspring did not survive. Statistical tests such as the chi-square test are used to decide whether a deviation is larger than chance.

Dobzhansky (1973) described how the study of inheritance became the foundation for understanding evolution, since the transmission of variation determines which variants can be selected. The principles of segregation and assortment explain how new combinations of traits arise in each generation.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how these rules have molecular explanations. Segregation corresponds to the separation of homologous chromosomes at meiosis, and independent assortment corresponds to the random orientation of chromosome pairs at metaphase I.

The rules are tested by performing controlled crosses, by counting offspring and by comparing observed ratios with the predictions of the model.
