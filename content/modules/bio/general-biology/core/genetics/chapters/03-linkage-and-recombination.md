Genes that lie on the same chromosome are linked. They tend to be inherited together, because the chromosome is passed on as a unit, and they do not assort independently as the Mendelian dihybrid ratio assumes. Linkage was discovered when crosses produced more parental combinations of traits than independent assortment predicted, and the observation showed that genes are arranged along chromosomes.

Recombination breaks linkage. During meiosis, homologous chromosomes pair and exchange segments in a process called crossing over. The exchange produces chromosomes that carry new combinations of the alleles of linked genes. The probability that two genes are separated by crossing over depends on the distance between them, and genes that are far apart recombine freely, appearing as if they assorted independently.

The recombination frequency is therefore a measure of distance. A frequency of one per cent is taken as one map unit, or one centimorgan. Genes can be ordered and mapped by measuring the recombination frequency between each pair, and three-point crosses allow the order of three genes to be determined from the frequencies of the double crossovers.

Dobzhansky (1973) noted that the arrangement of genes on chromosomes and the recombination that shuffles them are central to the variation on which evolution acts. Recombination creates combinations of alleles that selection can then test, and the rate of recombination varies among regions of the genome.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular events of crossing over, including the formation and repair of double-strand breaks and the exchange of strands between homologous chromosomes. The molecular mechanism explains why recombination occurs more often in some regions than others.

Maps are constructed from crosses in model organisms and from population data in humans, where recombination is inferred from the patterns of inheritance across families and from sequence data.
