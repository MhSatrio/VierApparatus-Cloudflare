The Hardy-Weinberg principle describes the genotype frequencies expected in a large, randomly mating population in the absence of evolutionary forces. If the frequency of allele A is p and of allele a is q, with p plus q equal to one, the expected frequencies of the genotypes are p squared for AA, two pq for Aa, and q squared for aa. The principle was stated independently by Hardy and Weinberg in 1908, and it is the baseline against which evolution is measured.

The principle makes a strong claim: the allele frequencies remain the same from generation to generation unless something acts on them. A population in which the frequencies do not change is said to be in equilibrium. Random mating, large population size, no mutation, no migration and no selection are the conditions for equilibrium, and each condition can be violated in nature.

Departures from equilibrium point to the forces at work. Selection changes the frequencies of alleles that differ in their effect on survival or reproduction. Mutation adds new alleles, and migration introduces alleles from other populations. Drift, which Dobzhansky (1973) discussed in the study of evolution, changes frequencies by chance, and its effect is greatest in small populations. Non-random mating, such as inbreeding, changes the genotype frequencies without changing the allele frequencies, and it increases the frequency of homozygotes.

The principle is useful because it can be tested. Observed genotype frequencies can be compared with the expected frequencies, and a significant departure indicates that one of the assumptions is violated. In human genetics, the principle is used to estimate carrier frequencies for recessive diseases from the frequency of affected individuals.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how allele frequencies are influenced by the molecular processes of mutation and recombination, which provide the source of variation and its reshuffling in each generation.

Population genetic data are collected by genotyping samples, by sequencing and by comparing allele frequencies across generations and across populations.
