An allele is one of the alternative forms of a gene. The genotype of an individual is the set of alleles it carries, and the phenotype is the trait that results. A dominant allele produces its trait even when only one copy is present, and a recessive allele produces its trait only when two copies are present. Dominance is a property of the relationship between an allele and its phenotype, not a property of the allele alone, and it depends on the trait being measured.

The molecular basis of an allele is a difference in DNA sequence. Watson and Crick (1953) showed that the sequence carries information, so an allele that changes a base may change the protein that the gene encodes. Many dominance relations arise because a single functional copy of a gene produces enough protein for normal function, while a missing copy does not. Where one copy is not enough, the heterozygote shows an intermediate phenotype, which is called incomplete dominance.

Codominance occurs when both alleles are expressed in the heterozygote, as in the human ABO blood groups, where the alleles for A and B antigens each produce their own product. Many genes have more than two alleles in a population, and the phenotype of an individual depends on which pair it carries. The ABO system has three alleles, and six genotypes produce four blood types.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the relationship between genotype and phenotype is shaped by the biochemical pathways in which the gene products act. A change in one enzyme may have little effect until the pathway is pushed beyond its reserve capacity, which is why some alleles appear recessive in healthy individuals.

Genotype and phenotype are measured by sequencing for genotype and by careful observation or measurement for phenotype. The two are linked by the biology of the organism, and the link is often less direct than the simple rules suggest.
