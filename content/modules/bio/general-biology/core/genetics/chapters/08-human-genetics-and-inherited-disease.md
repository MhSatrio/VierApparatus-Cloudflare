Humans are difficult to study genetically, because controlled crosses are not possible and generations are long. Geneticists rely on pedigrees, which record the inheritance of a trait through families, and on population studies that compare affected and unaffected individuals. These methods reveal whether a condition is caused by one gene or by many, whether it is dominant or recessive, and whether it is linked to the sex chromosomes.

Single-gene disorders follow Mendelian patterns. Cystic fibrosis is autosomal recessive, meaning that an affected person carries two defective copies of the gene. Huntington disease is autosomal dominant, so one defective copy is enough. Haemophilia is X-linked recessive, which is why it affects males more often than females. The pattern in a pedigree usually allows the mode of inheritance to be inferred with confidence.

Complex conditions, such as heart disease, type 2 diabetes and many cancers, depend on several genes and on the environment. Their risk is spread across many variants of small effect, and no single gene determines whether a person develops the condition. Genome-wide association studies compare the frequencies of many variants in affected and unaffected people, and they have identified loci associated with these conditions.

The International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium (2001) reported the sequence that made systematic study of human genes possible. The sequence provided the reference against which variation in individuals can be compared, and it changed the scale of human genetic research.

Genetic testing is powerful but requires careful interpretation. A variant may be harmless, may increase risk without causing disease, or may cause disease with variable penetrance. Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the effect of a variant depends on the function of the protein it alters, and this is why laboratory and clinical evidence are needed to classify a variant.

Human genetics is studied by pedigree analysis, by association studies, by sequencing of individuals and families, and by functional studies of variants in cells and animal models.
