The chromosome theory of inheritance holds that genes are located on chromosomes and that the behaviour of chromosomes during reproduction explains the patterns of inheritance. Each somatic cell in a diploid organism carries two copies of each chromosome, one from each parent. During meiosis, the homologous pairs separate, so each gamete receives one copy of each chromosome. This separation is the physical basis of the principle of segregation.

Meiosis also explains independent assortment. The orientation of each chromosome pair on the spindle is random, so the combination of maternal and paternal chromosomes that enters each gamete varies. The number of possible combinations increases rapidly with the number of chromosome pairs, which is one reason sexual reproduction produces so much variation among offspring.

Sex chromosomes provide a clear example of chromosomal inheritance. In humans, females carry two X chromosomes and males carry one X and one Y. Genes on the X chromosome show a pattern of inheritance that differs from autosomal genes, because males receive their X from their mother and pass it only to their daughters. Watson and Crick (1953) established the molecular structure of the chromosome's DNA, and the packaging of that DNA is what allows its large size to fit into the nucleus.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how chromosomes are condensed and organised during division, and how the mechanisms that ensure correct segregation operate at the spindle. Errors in these mechanisms can cause aneuploidy, in which a cell has an abnormal number of chromosomes, and aneuploidy is a major cause of miscarriage and developmental disorders.

Chromosomes are studied by staining and microscopy of metaphase spreads, which allows their number and structure to be counted, and by fluorescence in situ hybridisation, which locates specific sequences on chromosomes.
