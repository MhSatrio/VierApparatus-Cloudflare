Blood is a connective tissue in which the cells are suspended in a fluid matrix, the plasma. The cells are red blood cells, which carry oxygen; white blood cells, which defend against infection and disease; and platelets, small cell fragments that take part in clotting. Red cells are the most numerous, with about five million per cubic millimetre of blood in an adult, and they lack a nucleus. White cells make up a much smaller fraction, and they include neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils and basophils.

All blood cells arise from haematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow. A stem cell can divide to produce more stem cells, which maintains the supply, or it can commit to a lineage. The myeloid lineage gives rise to red cells, platelets, monocytes, neutrophils, eosinophils and basophils, and the lymphoid lineage gives rise to lymphocytes. Signals from growth factors, which act on receptors in the progenitor cells, control the balance between renewal and differentiation.

Medzhitov and Janeway (2002) described how the innate immune system recognises patterns that are shared by microbes, and many of the cells that do this are myeloid cells derived from the marrow. Their work shows that the cells of blood act as active defenders of the body, not simply as transporters.

The production of blood cells is continuous. Red cells live about four months, and the marrow replaces them constantly. A disturbance in the production of one lineage can cause anaemia, infection or bleeding, and the examination of blood and marrow is central to the diagnosis of many diseases.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how stem cells are maintained in their niche and how their fate is controlled by signalling and transcription factors. Blood is studied by counting and identifying cells in stained films, by flow cytometry that measures markers on each cell, and by examination of marrow biopsies.
