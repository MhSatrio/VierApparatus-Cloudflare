Epithelial tissue covers the surfaces of the body and lines its cavities and tubes. It forms the skin, the lining of the gut, the airways and the urinary tract, and it also forms glands. Epithelium is composed of cells packed closely together with little extracellular matrix between them, and it rests on a basement membrane that separates it from the underlying connective tissue. The cells are polarised, with one surface facing the outside or a cavity and the opposite surface facing the basement membrane.

Epithelia are classified by two features: the shape of the cells and the number of layers. Squamous cells are flat, cuboidal cells are roughly square in section, and columnar cells are tall. A single layer is simple epithelium, and several layers form stratified epithelium. Pseudostratified epithelium appears to have several layers but is in fact a single layer whose nuclei lie at different heights. The skin is stratified squamous epithelium, which resists abrasion, and the lining of the intestine is simple columnar epithelium, which absorbs nutrients.

The cells of an epithelium are bound together by junctions. Tight junctions seal the space between cells and make the epithelium a barrier that controls what passes between the cavity and the tissue beneath. Adherens junctions and desmosomes provide mechanical strength, and gap junctions allow small molecules to pass between cells. Hooke (1665) described the boxlike cells of cork, which form a similar barrier in plants, and the epithelial junctions of animals perform a comparable role.

Glands are formed from epithelium that has invaded the underlying tissue. Exocrine glands release their products through ducts onto a surface, while endocrine glands release hormones into the blood. The same epithelial cells can secrete different products depending on their location and their signals.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell adhesion molecules and the signalling that keep epithelia intact. Epithelia are studied by staining sections, by immunolabelling junctions and by culturing epithelial cells to examine their polarity.
