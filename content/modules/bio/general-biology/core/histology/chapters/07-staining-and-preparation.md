A tissue must be prepared before it can be examined under the microscope. Fixation preserves its structure by chemically cross-linking its proteins, usually with formaldehyde. Fixation stops decay, but it also changes the tissue, so the observer must know the fixative used. The fixed tissue is dehydrated through a series of alcohols, cleared with a solvent and infiltrated with paraffin wax or resin, which supports it during cutting. Hooke (1665) examined thin slices of cork, and the principle of cutting a sample thin enough to transmit light has changed little since.

Sections are cut with a microtome to a thickness of a few micrometres. The section is mounted on a glass slide, the wax is removed and the tissue is rehydrated. Staining then reveals the structures of the cell. Haematoxylin is a dye that binds nucleic acids and stains the nucleus blue, and eosin binds proteins in the cytoplasm and stains it pink. The combination of the two, known as H and E staining, is the most widely used stain in histology.

Other stains are used for specific purposes. Special stains reveal collagen, mucus, iron, fat or specific microbes. Immunohistochemistry uses antibodies that bind a particular protein, which is then detected by a coloured or fluorescent reaction. The method can identify the cell type that produces a protein, and it is a standard part of the diagnosis of many cancers.

Each step introduces artefacts. Fixation can shrink or swell tissue, cutting can tear or compress it, and staining can deposit dye in the wrong place. An experienced observer compares many sections, examines control tissues and recognises the common artefacts before interpreting a finding.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the chemistry of fixation and the labelling of cell components. Staining protocols are standardised so that results can be compared between laboratories, and quality control checks the sections before they are read.
