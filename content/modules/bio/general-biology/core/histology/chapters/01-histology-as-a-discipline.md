Histology studies the microscopic structure of tissues. A tissue is an organised group of cells with a common function, and histology asks how the cells are arranged, what they contain and how their arrangement serves the tissue. The discipline is the link between cell biology, which studies the single cell, and anatomy, which studies the whole organ.

Hooke (1665) first described the microscopic structure of a tissue when he examined thin slices of cork and found small compartments. His drawings and descriptions showed that living things have structure at a scale that the naked eye cannot resolve. Later anatomists used the microscope to examine animal and human tissues, and they found that the same basic tissue types appear in all organs, arranged in different ways.

The method depends on preparation. Tissues are fixed to preserve their structure, embedded in a supporting medium, cut into thin sections and stained so that cell components can be distinguished. Each step changes the tissue, and the investigator must know how the preparation affects what is seen. The stains used in histology bind specific classes of molecule, such as the nucleic acids and the proteins of the cytoplasm, and they reveal the organisation of the cell within the tissue.

Histology links structure to function. A tissue that absorbs nutrients has a large surface, as in the folded lining of the intestine, and a tissue that contracts has dense bundles of filaments. The observer can often infer what a tissue does from its appearance, and this inference is then tested by measuring the function directly.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the cells of a tissue are held together and organised, and how signals within the tissue control their arrangement. Histology is therefore a window onto the cell biology of the organism.

Koshland (2002) listed compartmentalisation among the organising principles of living systems, and tissues show that principle at the level of groups of cells, each with a defined role within a larger structure.
