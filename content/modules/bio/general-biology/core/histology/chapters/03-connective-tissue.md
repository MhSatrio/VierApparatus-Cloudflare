Connective tissue supports, binds and separates other tissues. Unlike epithelium, it has relatively few cells embedded in an abundant extracellular matrix, and the matrix determines most of its properties. The matrix is made of fibres, such as collagen and elastin, and of a ground substance rich in proteoglycans, which hold large amounts of water. Cells of connective tissue, including fibroblasts, which make the matrix, and macrophages, which defend against infection, live within it.

Loose areolar connective tissue fills the spaces between organs and beneath epithelia. It contains a mixture of collagen and elastic fibres in a hydrated matrix, and it allows organs to move and stretch while holding them in place. Dense connective tissue contains tightly packed collagen fibres and forms tendons and ligaments, which transmit force and resist stretching in one direction.

Cartilage is a firm tissue whose matrix is rich in proteoglycans and collagen. Its cells, chondrocytes, sit in small spaces called lacunae, and the matrix is hydrated so that cartilage resists compression. It covers the ends of bones at joints and forms the structure of the nose and ear. Cartilage has no blood supply, so it receives nutrients by diffusion through its matrix.

Bone is a connective tissue whose matrix is mineralised with calcium phosphate, which gives it stiffness. The mineral is deposited around collagen fibres, so the bone combines the strength of the mineral with the flexibility of the collagen. Ball (2008) pointed out that water is a major component of connective tissue, and the hydration of the matrix contributes to its resilience and its ability to transport solutes.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the synthesis and remodelling of the extracellular matrix, and the enzymes that break it down during repair and growth. Connective tissue is studied by staining for collagen and elastin, by measuring the mechanical properties of tissues and by biochemical analysis of the matrix.
