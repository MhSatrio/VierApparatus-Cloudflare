Muscle tissue is made of cells specialised for contraction. The three types differ in appearance and in the way they are controlled. Skeletal muscle cells are very long, multinucleate fibres, and under the microscope they show a regular pattern of cross-striations. The striations reflect the arrangement of the actin and myosin filaments in sarcomeres, as described in the Muscle Physiology chapter. The nuclei lie at the edge of each fibre, pushed aside by the contractile material.

Cardiac muscle is also striated, but its cells are shorter, branched and joined end to end by intercalated discs. The discs contain gap junctions and strong junctions that allow the cells to contract together as a single unit. The nuclei are central in each cell. The combination of branching and junctional connection is what allows the heart to act as a coordinated pump.

Smooth muscle has no striations. Its cells are spindle-shaped, with a single central nucleus, and the filaments are arranged diagonally, which allows the cell to shorten by a large fraction of its length. Smooth muscle forms the walls of blood vessels, the gut and the airways, and it contracts slowly and rhythmically.

Each muscle type is matched to its job. Skeletal muscle must act quickly and powerfully, so its fibres are long and its sarcomeres are highly organised. Cardiac muscle must contract continuously without tiring, so it has abundant mitochondria and tightly coupled cells. Smooth muscle must hold tension for long periods with little energy, which suits the regulation of the diameter of vessels and tubes.

Hooke (1665) first described microscopic structure in plant tissue, using dead cork. The striations of muscle were identified by later observers, and they became evidence of the organisation of contractile filaments. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the proteins that produce the cross-striations and the regulatory pathways that control smooth muscle. Muscle tissue is studied by staining and microscopy, and by immunolabelling of the contractile proteins.
