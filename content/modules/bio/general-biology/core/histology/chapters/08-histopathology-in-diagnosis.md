Histopathology is the examination of diseased tissue under the microscope. A pathologist reads a section and identifies the changes that distinguish healthy tissue from disease. Inflammation produces an influx of white cells and the swelling of tissue. Degeneration shows as changes in cell shape and staining. Cancer shows abnormal cell growth, with changes in the size and shape of nuclei, loss of the normal arrangement of cells and invasion of surrounding tissue. These changes are the basis of diagnosis, grading and staging.

Diagnosis depends on pattern recognition built from long experience. Two observers may disagree about borderline cases, and the reliability of diagnosis is measured by how often independent pathologists reach the same conclusion. Ioannidis (2005) discussed how the reliability of published findings depends on design and reporting, and the same concern applies to diagnostic judgement, where the criteria must be stated clearly and tested.

The clinical context matters. A section of tissue is interpreted together with the history of the patient, the results of imaging and the findings of other tests. A change that appears alarming in isolation may be harmless in context, and a subtle change may be significant when combined with other evidence.

Histopathology is increasingly combined with molecular tests. Immunohistochemistry identifies the proteins that define a tumour type, and sequencing identifies the mutations that guide treatment. The section remains the starting point, because it shows the relationship of the cells to one another and to the surrounding tissue.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular processes that underlie these changes, including the control of cell division and the mechanisms of inflammation. Histopathology is therefore a bridge between the cell biology of the earlier modules and the practice of medicine.

The method is limited by sampling. A small biopsy may not contain the most abnormal part of a lesion, and the section itself is a thin slice of a larger object. Pathologists address this by examining multiple levels of the sample and by stating the limits of their findings.
