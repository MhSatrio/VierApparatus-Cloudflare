Nervous tissue contains two main cell types. Neurons receive, process and transmit signals, and glial cells support them. A neuron has a cell body containing the nucleus, branching dendrites that receive input, and an axon that carries impulses away. In section, the cell body appears as a stained mass with a large nucleus and a prominent nucleolus, and the processes extend in the tissue as fine threads. The arrangement of cell bodies and fibres distinguishes grey matter, rich in cell bodies, from white matter, rich in myelinated axons.

Glial cells come in several types. Astrocytes support neurons, regulate the chemical environment around them and contribute to the blood-brain barrier. Oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system and Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system form myelin, the insulating sheath around axons. Microglia are the immune cells of the nervous system. Glia outnumber neurons in many regions of the brain, although the relative numbers vary.

Myelin is formed by wrapping the membrane of a glial cell around an axon many times. The sheath is interrupted at regular gaps, the nodes of Ranvier, where the axon membrane is exposed. Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) described the ionic currents of unmyelinated axons, and the myelin sheath changes conduction by confining the current flow to the nodes. The impulse therefore jumps from node to node, which increases the speed of conduction many times over.

The loss of myelin is the basis of several diseases. In multiple sclerosis, the immune system attacks myelin in the central nervous system, and the conduction of impulses is disrupted. The damage can be seen in section as demyelinated plaques.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how glial cells and neurons develop from common precursors and how the myelin membrane is built. Nervous tissue is studied by staining for neurons and myelin, by immunolabelling of specific cell types and by electron microscopy of the axons and their sheaths.
