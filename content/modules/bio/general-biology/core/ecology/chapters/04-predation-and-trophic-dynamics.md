Predation is an interaction in which one organism kills and consumes another, and its effects on populations can be strong. A predator population that increases will reduce its prey, and when prey become scarce the predators decline, which allows the prey to recover. The simplest model of this interaction produces cycles, in which the two populations rise and fall out of phase. Real systems are more complicated, because the rates of reproduction and predation change with density and with the environment.

Trophic dynamics describes how effects pass along food chains. A predator that eats herbivores can indirectly increase the plants on which the herbivores feed. The effect is a trophic cascade, in which changes at the top of a food chain are transmitted down it. Hairston and colleagues (1960) argued that such cascades are a general mechanism for regulating communities, although the strength of the effect varies.

Paine (1966) provided one of the clearest demonstrations of a trophic cascade. On a rocky shore, a predatory starfish fed on mussels. When the starfish were removed, the mussels became dominant and displaced many other species, reducing the diversity of the community. The starfish therefore had an effect much larger than its abundance would suggest. Paine later introduced the term keystone species for organisms of this kind.

Not all predators have strong effects. The strength of a cascade depends on how many links are in the food chain, on how much each predator eats and on whether the prey can be replaced by other species. Paine's work and that of others show that the effects can be large in some systems and small in others, which is why the general theory remains an active area of study.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the physiology of the predator and prey, including the feeding mechanisms and the defences that determine the rates of predation. Predation and trophic dynamics are studied by experimental removal and addition of predators, by long-term monitoring and by models that represent the feeding links.
