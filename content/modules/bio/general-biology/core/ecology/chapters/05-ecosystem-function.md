An ecosystem performs functions: it captures energy, cycles nutrients, decomposes dead matter and maintains the conditions for life. Ecosystem ecology measures these functions and asks what controls them. The most basic measure is primary productivity, the rate at which producers fix carbon into organic matter. It is limited by light, water, temperature and the supply of nutrients, and it sets the amount of energy available to the rest of the food web.

Lane and Martin (2010) argued that energy availability constrains the complexity of cells, and the same logic applies to ecosystems, where productivity limits how much biomass the community can support. Ecosystems with high productivity can support more consumers and more trophic levels than unproductive ones, and the energetic budget therefore shapes the structure of the community as well as its function.

The relation between species diversity and ecosystem function has been debated. Some studies suggest that more species produce more stable productivity, because different species respond differently to changing conditions. Others find that a few dominant species control most of the function. Paine (1966) showed that the removal of a dominant predator can change the composition and productivity of a community, which indicates that the identity of species matters as well as their number.

Ecosystem function is measured by harvesting biomass, by measuring the carbon dioxide exchange of the ecosystem with the atmosphere and by tracking the cycling of nutrients through soils and plants. Experiments that remove species or manipulate their numbers show how each function responds to changes in diversity.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the metabolic processes that underlie the functions of ecosystems. The rates of photosynthesis, respiration and decomposition are rooted in the chemistry of the organisms involved, and understanding that chemistry helps explain their rates in the field.

The relation between diversity and function is central to conservation, because it determines what is lost when species disappear. The evidence suggests that both the number of species and the presence of key species matter, and that the answer depends on the function measured.
