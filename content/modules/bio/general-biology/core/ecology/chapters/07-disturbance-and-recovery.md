A disturbance is an event that removes organisms or changes the environment, and it can be natural or caused by people. Fire, storms, floods, landslides, grazing and the clearing of land are all disturbances. Their effects depend on their frequency, how often they occur, their intensity, how severe they are for the organisms, and their extent, how much of the community they affect. Ecologists study disturbance because it is one of the main forces that shape communities.

Paine (1966) described how the removal of a dominant species on a rocky shore opened space for other species, which increased the diversity of the community for a time. Disturbance of this kind creates opportunities for species that can colonise and grow quickly. The pattern is a common one, and it has been studied in many systems.

The intermediate disturbance hypothesis proposes that diversity is highest at intermediate levels of disturbance. When disturbance is rare, dominant competitors exclude weaker species. When it is very frequent or severe, only the species that can tolerate it survive. At intermediate levels, a range of species can persist, because disturbance prevents competitive exclusion while allowing the colonisation of new space. The hypothesis is consistent with the competitive processes described by Hairston and colleagues (1960).

The evidence for the hypothesis is mixed. Some communities show the predicted peak in diversity, while others do not, and the form of the relationship depends on the type of disturbance and the organisms involved. The hypothesis has been valuable because it makes a specific prediction that can be tested by comparing communities with different disturbance histories.

Recovery after disturbance follows a sequence of colonisation and succession, as described in the Foundations module. The rate of recovery depends on the seed bank, the dispersal of species into the area and the severity of the damage to the soil. Some ecosystems recover in years and others take centuries.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the physiological responses of organisms to stress and injury, which determine their survival in disturbed areas. Disturbance is studied by long-term monitoring before and after events, by experimental disturbance of plots and by comparisons between disturbed and undisturbed sites.
