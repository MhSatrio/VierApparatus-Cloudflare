Biogeography studies the distribution of organisms in space and through time. Darwin (1859) gave considerable attention to the distribution of species, and he argued that the presence of similar species on islands and continents reflects their descent and their dispersal. Modern biogeography combines this historical view with ecological and climatic explanations, and it asks why each species lives where it does.

Dispersal is the movement of organisms from one place to another. A species can occupy only the areas it can reach, and barriers such as oceans, mountains and deserts limit dispersal. The distribution of a group often reflects the routes by which its ancestors colonised new areas. Islands provide clear examples, because the species on them are usually descended from colonists that crossed the sea, and the number of colonists depends on the distance to the source.

Climate limits the distribution of species by tolerance. Each species has a range of temperature, moisture and other conditions in which it can survive and reproduce, and its distribution follows the climate that falls within this range. Climate change moves these boundaries, and species respond by shifting their ranges or by adapting.

History shapes distribution through the events of the past. Continental drift, glaciation and the formation of mountains have divided and joined populations, and the patterns of relationship among species often follow these events. Dobzhansky (1973) argued that the present distribution of organisms is the product of their history, and biogeography is the discipline that reads that history from distributions.

The species-area relationship states that the number of species in an area increases with its size, usually in a predictable way. Larger areas contain more habitats and more individuals, and they receive more colonists. The latitudinal gradient is the increase in diversity from the poles toward the tropics, and it is one of the most widespread patterns in ecology, with explanations that include climate, history and the rate of evolution.

Biogeographers use distribution records, phylogenetic analysis of the colonisation of regions, and models that relate species to climate. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the physiological tolerances of organisms that determine the limits of their distributions, and the links between physiology and distribution are a central part of the field.
