A community is the set of species that live together in one place. Its structure is described by richness, the number of species, and by evenness, how equally the individuals are shared among them. Diversity indices combine the two, and the most common, such as the Shannon index, increase with richness and with evenness. The measures are useful for comparing communities, but they say nothing about why the species are present, which is the question that community theory addresses.

Hairston and colleagues (1960) proposed that community structure is determined by the interaction between predators and prey and by competition among the species that remain. Their argument was that predators keep herbivores below the level at which they would consume all the plants, so the plant community is structured from above. The idea was influential, and it set the agenda for a generation of studies of the forces that shape communities.

Paine (1966) examined how the number of links in a food web affects species diversity. He found that the removal of a predator could change the dominance of the species below it, and that the effects depended on the complexity of the community. His work supported the view that predators can maintain diversity by preventing a single prey species from monopolising the resources.

Competition is a second major explanation. Species that use the same resources can coexist only if they differ in how they use them, a principle called niche differentiation. The number of species that can coexist is limited by the number of resources and by the degree of differentiation, and this limit provides a theoretical account of community size.

Community theory remains debated. Explanations based on predation, competition, disturbance and chance each account for some observations, and the relative importance of each varies among communities. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular and cellular processes that determine the traits of the species involved, which provides a basis for understanding the differences among them.

Community structure is measured by surveys and by manipulation of species presence in experimental plots. The analysis uses statistical methods that compare the observed pattern with what would be expected under alternative explanations.
