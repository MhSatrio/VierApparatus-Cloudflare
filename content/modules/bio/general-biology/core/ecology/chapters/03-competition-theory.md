Competition occurs when two or more organisms use the same limited resource, and each reduces the amount available to the others. Darwin (1859) recognised that the struggle for existence includes competition among individuals of the same and different species, and competition theory formalises that idea. The theory asks how many species can coexist on a given set of resources, and under what conditions one species will displace another.

The niche is the set of conditions and resources in which a species can live and reproduce. The fundamental niche is the full range of conditions a species could occupy in the absence of competitors and predators. The realised niche is the smaller range it actually occupies, because of interactions with other species. The difference between the two is evidence of competition, and it is often measured by comparing where a species lives alone with where it lives with its competitors.

Hairston and colleagues (1960) considered the relation between competition and community structure. Their argument was that the number of species that a community can contain depends on the resources available and on the extent to which species differ in their use of them. The principle of competitive exclusion states that two species that use the same resource in the same way cannot coexist indefinitely, because the one with the higher rate of use will eventually eliminate the other.

Coexistence requires differentiation. Species may use different sizes of food, feed at different times, or occupy different parts of a habitat. The degree of differentiation determines how many species can share a set of resources, and this relation is the basis of the classic models of competition.

Competition can change with the environment. A species that is a strong competitor in one habitat may be a weak one in another, and the outcome may depend on the abundance of resources or on the presence of predators. The evolution of traits that reduce competition, such as differences in beak size among the finches of the Galapagos Islands, is one way that related species coexist.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the metabolic and physiological traits of organisms determine their use of resources, which in turn determines their position in competition. Competition is measured by removal experiments, in which a competitor is removed and the response of the remaining species is measured, and by comparisons of resource use.
