Population regulation is the set of processes that keep a population within limits over time. A population that is not regulated would grow without bound or go extinct, and the question is what determines its typical size. Factors that change in strength with population density, such as competition for food and the spread of disease, are density-dependent. Factors that do not depend on density, such as weather and fire, are density-independent. Regulation requires density-dependent factors, because only they can bring a population back when it is too large or too small.

Hairston and colleagues (1960) argued that herbivore populations are regulated by their predators rather than by the food supply. Their reasoning was that plants are abundant and that herbivores are kept below the level at which they would deplete their food. Predators, which are themselves limited by their own food, would then control the herbivores. The view is called top-down control, and it contrasts with bottom-up control, in which the food supply limits the herbivores.

The debate between the two views has been long and productive. Evidence for top-down control comes from experiments in which predators are removed or added and the herbivore population responds. Evidence for bottom-up control comes from fertilisation experiments, in which increased nutrients raise the abundance of plants and of the herbivores that feed on them. Many systems show both, and the balance depends on the species and the environment.

Dobzhansky (1973) argued that the interaction between organisms is part of the evolutionary history of both, and population regulation shows how the evolution of defences and counter-defences can shape the balance. A predator that evolves better hunting changes the population dynamics of its prey, and the prey's evolution feeds back to the predator.

Regulation is tested by manipulation of predators and resources in the field, by long-term population records and by models that predict the dynamics of each factor. The strongest evidence comes from experiments that test a specific prediction of each explanation.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the physiology of the organisms involved, including the energetic limits that set the reproductive rate. The rate of reproduction and survival are the parameters that the population models require, and they depend on the biology of each individual.
