Animal development begins when a sperm fertilises an egg. The zygote divides repeatedly in a process called cleavage, producing a ball of cells called the blastula. The cells then move and rearrange in gastrulation, a process that forms the three germ layers. The embryo subsequently organises its tissues into organs, and the adult body develops from these layers over many further stages.

Gastrulation establishes the basic architecture of the embryo. Cells migrate inward and form the endoderm and mesoderm, while the ectoderm remains at the surface. The movements are driven by changes in cell shape and adhesion, and they are guided by signals from neighbouring cells. Errors in gastrulation produce severe malformations, which shows how precisely the movements must be coordinated.

Animal bodies are organised along axes. The anterior-posterior axis is specified by a set of genes called Hox genes, which are arranged in clusters on the chromosome. Their order along the cluster corresponds to their expression along the body axis, so that the genes at one end specify the head and those at the other end specify the tail. Knoll and Carroll (1999) discussed the early diversification of animals, and the conservation of Hox clusters across many groups suggests that they were present in ancestral forms.

The segments of the body are specified by these regulatory genes. Each segment receives a combination of Hox gene activities that determines its identity, such as the difference between a leg and a wing or between a thoracic and an abdominal segment. Changes in the expression of these genes can produce dramatic changes in form, and this has been an important route for the evolution of body plans.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular basis of pattern formation, including the gradients of signalling molecules that define positions in the embryo. The interplay of gene regulation and cell movement produces the organised body.

Development is studied by following labelled cells in living embryos, by mutations that alter the pattern and by the expression of genes measured with fluorescent markers.
