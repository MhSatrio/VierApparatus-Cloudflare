Most animals reproduce sexually, producing gametes that fuse to form a zygote. Sperm and eggs are produced by meiosis in specialised gonads, a process called gametogenesis. Sperm are small and motile, and they carry little cytoplasm, while eggs are large and store the nutrients needed for early development. The difference in gamete size is one of the basic distinctions of sexual reproduction, and it has shaped the reproductive strategies of both sexes.

Fertilisation brings the genetic material of two parents together. In many animals the sperm and egg fuse in the external environment, while in others fertilisation occurs inside the female, and the embryo may develop there or be laid as an egg. Specific molecules on the surfaces of the gametes ensure that only sperm of the same species fertilise an egg. This recognition is an important barrier to hybridisation.

Some animals reproduce asexually. Budding, fragmentation and parthenogenesis produce offspring without fusion of gametes. Asexual reproduction allows rapid population growth, but it reduces genetic variation. Dobzhansky (1973) argued that sexual reproduction is a source of the variation on which selection acts, and many animals alternate between sexual and asexual phases according to conditions.

Animals invest in offspring in very different ways. Some produce many small eggs and provide no care, while others produce few young and invest heavily in feeding and protecting them. The balance between the number and quality of offspring reflects the environment and the risks that the young face.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular events of fertilisation and the mechanisms of gamete formation, including the meiotic divisions and the signals that control them. The study of reproduction links cell division to the development of the next generation.

Reproduction is studied by counting offspring, by observing fertilisation in living embryos and by genetic analysis of the contributions of each parent.
