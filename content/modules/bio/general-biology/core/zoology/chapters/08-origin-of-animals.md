Animals are a relatively recent branch of the tree of life. The earliest animals are thought to have appeared in the oceans before the major animal groups of today, but their fossils are rare and hard to interpret. The origin of animals is therefore studied by combining the fossil record, the geological history of the oceans and the comparison of genes among living groups.

Knoll and Carroll (1999) reviewed the evidence from comparative biology and geology, and their work is a standard starting point for the subject. They pointed out that many of the body plans that define modern groups appear in the fossil record during the Cambrian period, about 540 million years ago, and that the preceding Ediacaran fauna contains forms whose relationship to animals is disputed. The sharp appearance of many groups in the Cambrian is sometimes called the Cambrian explosion, although the term describes a diversification that unfolded over several million years.

Molecular data place the origin of animals earlier, because the genes that define animal cell types, such as those for nerve and muscle proteins, are found in their closest relatives among the single-celled eukaryotes. Comparisons of these genes suggest that the basic toolkit of animals existed before the first fossil animals. The timing estimated from molecular data is uncertain, and it depends on the rates of evolution assumed.

Oxygen is often discussed as a factor in the origin of animals. Animals require oxygen for their metabolism, and a rise in oxygen concentration in the ocean may have made the evolution of larger bodies possible. The evidence for the timing of oxygen rise is debated, and the link between oxygen and animal origins remains a hypothesis.

Dobzhansky (1973) argued that the study of the history of life, including the origin of major groups, is essential to understanding the diversity of organisms. The question of how animals arose connects the fossil record to the mechanisms of development and gene regulation.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell types and signalling systems of animals, which provide the basis for comparison with their unicellular relatives. The conservation of these systems across animals suggests a common origin of the toolkit.

The origin of animals is studied through fossils, geochemistry of ancient rocks, the genomes of living relatives and the comparison of developmental genes, and each line of evidence has its own uncertainties.
