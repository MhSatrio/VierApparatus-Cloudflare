Animals are divided into many phyla, each defined by a distinctive body plan. Sponges lack true tissues and have a body organised around water flow through pores. Cnidarians, such as corals and jellyfish, have radial symmetry and stinging cells. Molluscs have a soft body often protected by a shell. Annelids are segmented worms, and arthropods have jointed legs and an exoskeleton. Chordates have a notochord at some stage of life, and vertebrates are the chordates with a backbone. These groups differ in their body plans, but they share the basic features of animals described earlier.

Arthropods are the largest group of animals by number of species, and insects alone make up a large fraction of described species. Their success is linked to the exoskeleton, the segmentation of the body and the diversity of their appendages. Molluscs are also very diverse, from snails and clams to octopuses, and their range of body forms reflects different solutions to the problems of feeding and protection.

Darwin (1859) described the diversity of life as the result of descent with modification, and the groups of animals form a branching tree. The relationships among the phyla were long debated, because their body plans differ greatly, and molecular data have revised many earlier groupings.

Knoll and Carroll (1999) reviewed the timing of the appearance of the major animal groups in the fossil record. Their analysis showed that many of the body plans appeared in a relatively short interval, and they discussed the environmental and genetic factors that may have contributed.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the shared machinery of cells and development provides a common basis for animal diversity, and how changes in regulatory genes produce the differences among groups.

Diversity is studied by comparative anatomy, by molecular phylogenetics and by the fossil record, and the three approaches are combined to build the tree of animals.
