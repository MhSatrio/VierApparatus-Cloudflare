Animal movement depends on muscle, which converts the chemical energy of ATP into mechanical force. Muscle contraction results from the sliding of two filament systems past each other. Thick filaments of myosin have heads that bind to thin filaments of actin, pull them along and release, using the energy of ATP hydrolysis at each cycle. The overlap of the filaments shortens the muscle fibre, and many fibres acting together generate the force for movement.

The cycle of binding, pulling and release is powered by the chemistry of the myosin head, which hydrolyses ATP and changes shape. The head binds actin in one conformation and releases it in another, and the change in angle between the head and the tail of myosin provides the stroke. Calcium ions control the process in striated muscle by exposing the binding sites on actin, so that contraction begins only when a nerve signal arrives.

Animals support their bodies and transmit force in several ways. Hydrostatic skeletons, such as those of earthworms, use fluid pressure in a closed compartment, and the muscles act against this pressure. Exoskeletons, such as the chitin shells of arthropods, provide a rigid surface to which muscles attach. Endoskeletons, such as the bones of vertebrates, grow within the body and allow large size.

Lane and Martin (2010) argued that the energy available to a cell and organism limits its complexity, and muscle is one of the most energy-demanding tissues. Animals that move quickly and continuously need a large supply of ATP, which is provided by respiration in the mitochondria of the muscle cells.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the structure of the sarcomere and the proteins that regulate contraction. The mechanism has been studied in detail by biochemistry, by X-ray diffraction of muscle and by single-molecule measurements of the motor.

Movement is measured by force transducers, by electrical recording of muscle activity and by high-speed imaging of the body and its parts.
