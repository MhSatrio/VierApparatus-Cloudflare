Behaviour is what an animal does, and it results from the interaction of its genes, its nervous system and its experience. Some behaviours are innate, meaning they develop without learning, such as the nest-building of birds or the feeding response of a newborn mammal. Others are learned, such as the song of a bird that must hear adults during a sensitive period. The distinction is useful but not absolute, because even innate behaviours depend on the environment during development.

Behaviour is subject to natural selection, because behaviours affect survival and reproduction. Dobzhansky (1973) argued that the patterns of animal behaviour can be understood only in relation to their evolutionary history. A behaviour that increases the number of offspring that survive will tend to spread, provided it has a genetic basis that varies in the population.

Many behaviours reflect a trade-off. An animal that forages more to gain food risks predation, and one that defends a territory spends energy and may be injured. Behavioural ecology studies these trade-offs, and it predicts behaviours that maximise fitness under particular conditions.

Animal communication is a striking form of behaviour. Signals such as colour, song, scent and movement carry information between individuals, and their form is shaped by the receivers as well as the senders. The signal must be reliable enough to be useful, which means that dishonest signals are often costly or are detected by others.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular and molecular basis of the nervous processes that produce behaviour, and the genes that influence it. Behaviour is studied by observation in the field, by experiments that manipulate the environment and by genetic and neural analysis of its causes.

The study of behaviour brings together ecology, genetics and neuroscience, and it shows how the mechanisms of the nervous system are shaped by their function.
