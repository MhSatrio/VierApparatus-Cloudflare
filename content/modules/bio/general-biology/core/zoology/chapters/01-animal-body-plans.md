A body plan is the basic arrangement of an animal's tissues and organs. The most fundamental distinction is between radial and bilateral symmetry. Animals with radial symmetry, such as jellyfish and sea anemones, have parts arranged around a central axis, with no distinct front and back. Bilaterally symmetrical animals have a left and a right side, a front and a back, and usually a head with sensory organs. Most animals are bilateral, and the body plan is strongly associated with active movement and directed feeding.

Animal embryos form three primary germ layers during early development. The ectoderm gives rise to the skin and nervous system, the endoderm to the lining of the gut and its associated organs, and the mesoderm to muscle, bone, blood and connective tissue. Animals with a mesoderm are called triploblastic, and those without it are diploblastic. The number and arrangement of the germ layers help define the major groups.

The body cavity is another feature that varies. Some animals have no cavity between the gut and the body wall, others have a fluid-filled cavity that is not lined by mesoderm, and others have a coelom, a cavity lined by mesoderm. The coelom allows the organs to move independently and supports complex organ systems.

Knoll and Carroll (1999) reviewed the comparative and geological evidence for the early diversification of animals. They noted that the basic body plans of many groups appeared within a relatively short interval, and their analysis is one of the key sources for understanding the origin of these forms.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe how the germ layers are established by signalling and gene expression in the early embryo, and how the same genes specify tissues across the animal kingdom. Body plans are therefore built by regulatory programmes shared among animals.

Body plans are described from anatomy, from embryology and from the sequences of developmental genes, which reveal the relationships among groups.
