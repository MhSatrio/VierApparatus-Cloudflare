Mitochondria have been proposed to drive ageing for several decades. The best-known version of the idea, the free radical theory, holds that the respiratory chain produces reactive oxygen species as a by-product, that these species damage mitochondrial DNA and proteins, and that the damage accumulates until the organelle fails. A related theory proposes that mutations in mitochondrial DNA accumulate with age and that the resulting defective copies reduce energy supply in tissues that depend on it.

The predictions of these theories are testable. If oxidative damage to mitochondria drives ageing, then reducing it should extend lifespan, and mice with altered antioxidant defences should age differently. If mitochondrial DNA mutations drive ageing, then the mutant fraction should rise in old tissues and reach levels that impair function. Nunnari and Suomalainen (2012) reviewed evidence for both, and the results are mixed.

The evidence for the mutation theory is strong in some tissues and weaker in others. Mutant mitochondrial DNA does accumulate in aged tissues, but the fraction in most tissues remains below the threshold that produces clear dysfunction. The causal link between the mutations and the decline of tissue function is less certain than the correlation.

The free radical theory has been challenged. Some manipulations that increase reactive oxygen species do not shorten lifespan, and antioxidant treatments have generally failed to extend it in humans. The theory has been revised to emphasise signalling by reactive oxygen species rather than only their damage.

Ioannidis (2005) described how weak associations can appear to be causal when studies are small, flexibly analysed or selectively reported. The mitochondrial theories of ageing have been supported by many correlational studies, and the strength of the conclusion depends on interventional tests that change one variable while holding others constant.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the mitochondrial processes of energy production and damage. Ageing research uses lifespan experiments in model organisms, measurements of mutation and damage in tissues and interventions that alter mitochondrial function.
