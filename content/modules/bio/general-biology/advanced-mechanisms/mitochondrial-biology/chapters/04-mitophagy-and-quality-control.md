A mitochondrion that has lost its membrane potential cannot make ATP and cannot import proteins, and it can release harmful reactive oxygen species. The cell removes such damaged organelles by mitophagy, a form of selective autophagy in which the mitochondrion is enclosed by a membrane and delivered to the lysosome. The loss of membrane potential is one of the signals that marks the organelle for removal, because the Mitchell (1961) gradient is the basis of the organelle's function and its loss is a clear sign of damage.

In one well-studied pathway, a kinase on the outer membrane of the damaged mitochondrion accumulates, because the import of the kinase into the inner membrane that normally destroys it depends on the membrane potential. The kinase phosphorylates ubiquitin on the outer membrane, and a ubiquitin ligase is recruited that marks other proteins on the surface. Adaptor proteins bind the ubiquitin tags and the autophagy machinery, which engulfs the organelle.

Nunnari and Suomalainen (2012) noted that the quality control system works together with fission. A damaged portion of the network can be separated by fission and then removed by mitophagy, which allows the rest of the network to continue functioning. Fusion dilutes damage across the network, and the balance between these processes determines how many damaged organelles remain.

Failure of mitophagy has consequences. Mutations in the proteins of the pathway are linked to Parkinson disease, in which dopaminergic neurons are lost, and damaged mitochondria are thought to contribute to their degeneration. The causal relation is not fully established, and the evidence comes from genetic and cellular studies that require further testing.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the autophagy machinery and the lysosome that completes the degradation. Mitophagy is studied by tracking labelled mitochondria into lysosomes with fluorescent reporters, by measuring the proteins of the pathway and by perturbing membrane potential with drugs.
