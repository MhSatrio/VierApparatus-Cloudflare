Mitochondria are not fixed, separate organelles. They form networks that fuse and divide continually, and the balance between the two processes shapes the morphology of the network. Fusion joins two mitochondria, mixing their membranes and their contents. Fission divides a mitochondrion into two. Both are carried out by large GTPases of the dynamin family, which assemble into rings or spirals around the membrane and change its curvature when they hydrolyse GTP.

Nunnari and Suomalainen (2012) described the fusion machinery, which includes proteins that mediate outer membrane fusion and a separate protein that joins the inner membrane. The inner membrane fusion protein is needed to maintain the cristae and the integrity of the respiratory complexes, and its absence reduces the efficiency of respiration.

Fission is driven by a dynamin-related protein that is recruited to the outer membrane from the cytosol. The recruitment is controlled by adaptor proteins on the mitochondrial surface, and constriction at sites where the endoplasmic reticulum contacts the mitochondrion helps position the division. Fission separates damaged parts of the network, allowing them to be removed, and it distributes mitochondria during cell division.

Dynamics serve several functions. Fusion allows mitochondria to share their DNA and proteins, which can buffer the effects of damage in one part of the network. Fission allows the removal of damaged components and the production of new organelles. The balance between the two determines whether the network is elongated, fragmented or tubular, and the morphology affects how efficiently the organelles respond to demand.

Altered dynamics are associated with disease. Mutations in the fusion or fission machinery cause neurodegenerative diseases and other disorders, and the effects reflect the dependence of neurons on healthy mitochondria. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cytoskeletal and membrane processes that move and shape the organelles. Dynamics are studied by live imaging of the network, by labelling the matrix and membranes to measure mixing and by genetic perturbation of the machinery.
