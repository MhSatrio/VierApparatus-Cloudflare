Mitochondria contain their own DNA, a small circular molecule that is present in many copies per organelle. In humans it is about sixteen thousand five hundred base pairs long and encodes thirteen proteins, all of them subunits of the respiratory complexes and of the ATP synthase, together with the ribosomal and transfer RNAs needed to translate them. Watson and Crick (1953) described the double helix, and the mitochondrial genome shares its base-pairing chemistry with nuclear DNA, although the molecule itself is circular rather than split into linear chromosomes.

The genome is small compared with that of the bacteria from which mitochondria are thought to derive, and the reduction reflects the transfer of most genes to the nucleus over evolutionary time. Nunnari and Suomalainen (2012) noted that about a thousand proteins are required for mitochondrial function, and nearly all of them are encoded in the nucleus, synthesised in the cytosol and imported. The genetic system of the organelle is therefore an integrated one, with nuclear and mitochondrial genes working together.

The genetic code of mitochondria differs slightly from the standard code. Some codons specify different amino acids, and a few serve as stop codons in the mitochondrial system. The differences are small in number but they mean that mitochondrial genes cannot be translated with the standard code without adjustment.

Mitochondrial DNA is replicated independently of the nuclear cycle and is present in many copies. The copies are packaged into nucleoids, and the number per cell can vary with the energy demand of the tissue. Mutations in the mitochondrial genome can accumulate because the repair systems are less effective than in the nucleus, and the mutations may affect only some copies, a condition called heteroplasmy.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the translation machinery of the organelle and the genes it contains. The mitochondrial genome is studied by sequencing, by measuring the proportion of mutant copies and by analysing the expression of its genes.
