The mitochondrion is a central regulator of apoptosis, the programmed death described in the Cell Biology module. In the intrinsic pathway, damage or a withdrawal of survival signals leads to the permeabilisation of the outer mitochondrial membrane. Proteins in the intermembrane space, including cytochrome c, are released into the cytosol, where cytochrome c binds an adaptor and triggers the activation of caspases. The release is therefore the point of commitment for many forms of cell death.

The permeabilisation is controlled by the BCL-2 family of proteins. Some members, such as BAX and BAK, oligomerise in the outer membrane to form pores, while others, such as BCL-2 and BCL-XL, prevent this oligomerisation. A third class, the BH3-only proteins, senses the stresses of the cell and activates the pore-forming members, or inhibits the protective ones. The balance among these proteins determines whether a cell survives or dies.

Koshland (2002) listed the regulation of compartments among the principles of living systems, and the release of mitochondrial contents is a regulated breach of a compartment. The cell uses this breach as a decision point, which ensures that death occurs only when the balance of signals requires it.

Nunnari and Suomalainen (2012) noted that the mitochondrial membrane is also the site of the damage that initiates the pathway. Loss of membrane potential, oxidative damage and accumulation of calcium can each contribute to permeabilisation. The organelle is therefore both a sensor and an effector of the death decision.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the caspase cascade and the BCL-2 proteins. The pathway is studied by measuring cytochrome c release in permeabilised cells, by genetic deletion of the BCL-2 family members and by pharmacological inhibition of the protective proteins.
