Mitochondrial DNA is inherited mainly through the egg. The sperm contributes its mitochondria to the zygote only in small numbers, and in many species the paternal mitochondria are actively destroyed after fertilisation. The result is that the mitochondrial genome passes through a single maternal line, which has consequences for how it can be used to study ancestry and for how diseases caused by its mutations are transmitted.

Nunnari and Suomalainen (2012) described the mechanisms that restrict paternal transmission, including the tagging of sperm mitochondria for degradation by the autophagy machinery. The restriction is not absolute, and rare cases of paternal transmission have been reported, but it is sufficient to make maternal inheritance the rule.

Heteroplasmy changes between generations because of a bottleneck in the germline. A female carries many mitochondrial genomes in each cell, and the oocyte receives a sampled subset. The random sampling can shift the proportion of mutant copies, so that one child may inherit a high fraction of mutant copies and another a low fraction. The shift is often large in a single generation, and it explains the variable severity of mitochondrial disease within families.

The maternal line of mitochondrial DNA has been used to trace human migrations and ancestry. Because the genome is not recombined, its lineages form a tree, and the diversity of the tree reflects the history of the female line. The method has been applied to many populations, and its interpretation depends on the rate at which mutations accumulate, which is itself estimated from the same data.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the germline and the processes of gametogenesis. The inheritance of mitochondria is studied by genotyping families, by measuring heteroplasmy in tissues and gametes and by comparing lineages in populations.
