Most of the roughly thousand proteins of mitochondria are synthesised on cytosolic ribosomes and must be imported. Each precursor carries a targeting signal that directs it to the organelle. Many matrix proteins carry an amino-terminal presequence, which forms an amphipathic helix recognised by receptors on the outer membrane. The precursor is then passed through the translocase of the outer membrane and handed to the translocase of the inner membrane, which moves it into the matrix.

The import into the matrix depends on the membrane potential across the inner membrane, which Mitchell (1961) identified as the driving force of the chemiosmotic system. The electrical component of the proton motive force pulls the positively charged presequence through the translocase, and this is one of the reasons that the potential is needed both for ATP synthesis and for maintaining the organelle's protein content.

Proteins destined for the inner membrane or the intermembrane space use other routes. Some are sorted by stop-transfer sequences that arrest them in the inner membrane, and others are folded in the intermembrane space by a mechanism that traps them after import. Such pathways explain how the complexes of the respiratory chain acquire subunits from both genomes.

Once inside, proteins are folded with the help of chaperones and assembled into complexes. The matrix contains chaperones that bind unfolded proteins and prevent their aggregation, and proteases that remove misfolded or unassembled proteins. The quality of the imported protein is maintained by these systems.

Nunnari and Suomalainen (2012) noted that defects in import cause disease, because the organelle cannot maintain its respiratory complexes without the nuclear-encoded subunits. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the general principles of protein targeting and translocation across membranes. Import is studied by in vitro import assays with radiolabelled precursors, by proteomic analysis of isolated mitochondria and by genetic defects in the translocases.
