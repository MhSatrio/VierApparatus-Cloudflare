Sequencing the genomes of pathogen isolates has become a routine tool of public health. Closely related isolates from patients can be compared, and the number of genetic differences between them is used to estimate whether the patients are linked by transmission. The approach can reveal outbreaks that would otherwise go unnoticed, and it can show how a resistance gene or a virulence factor has spread through a population.

The interpretation depends on the rate at which the pathogen accumulates changes. A fast-evolving pathogen accumulates many differences over short periods, which makes transmission links easier to resolve, while a slow one may not differ between isolates that are epidemiologically connected. The rate must be estimated from the data themselves, and the estimate carries uncertainty that affects the conclusions.

Genomic data can also reveal the history of an epidemic. Phylogenetic analysis of many isolates reconstructs the lineages that spread, the times at which they arose and the places where they moved. Such analyses have shown the spread of resistance and the emergence of new variants in several pathogens, and they are increasingly used to guide responses.

Ioannidis (2005) described how inferences from data with many assumptions can be sensitive to the choices made. Transmission inferences should be checked against epidemiological data, and the sampling of isolates must be considered, since a biased sample gives a biased tree.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular evolution of the pathogen and the mechanisms by which it changes. Pathogen genomics is practised by sequencing isolates, by phylogenetic analysis and by integration with epidemiological and clinical data.

The lesson of the module is that host and pathogen are linked by a shared evolutionary history, and that understanding the molecular contest between them is necessary for preventing and treating disease.
