Cells do not depend only on immune cells to resist infection. Each cell can detect viral and bacterial molecules and respond with defences that restrict the pathogen. Medzhitov and Janeway (2002) described the pattern recognition receptors that trigger these responses, and their signals activate the production of interferons. Interferons are secreted proteins that bind receptors on neighbouring cells and induce an antiviral state, in which hundreds of genes are switched on to block the replication of viruses.

Restriction factors are proteins that inhibit specific pathogens. Some block the entry of viruses, others degrade viral nucleic acids or prevent the assembly of new virus particles. Many restriction factors are themselves under evolutionary pressure, because viruses evolve to escape them, and the genes that encode them often show signs of rapid evolution. This arms race is an example of the coevolution described in the Evolutionary Biology module.

Autophagy can target intracellular bacteria and viruses for destruction. The bacteria are enclosed in a membrane derived from the autophagosome and delivered to the lysosome, in a process similar to mitophagy described in the Mitochondrial Biology module. Some pathogens block the process, and the balance between autophagy and evasion determines the outcome.

The inflammasome is a multiprotein complex that detects microbial molecules or cellular damage in the cytoplasm. Its activation produces active caspase-1, which cleaves inflammatory cytokines into their active forms and triggers a form of cell death that releases them. The inflammasome thus links the detection of intracellular infection to inflammation and to the removal of infected cells.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the interferon response and the signalling that induces it. Innate restriction is studied by measuring the induced genes after infection, by testing the effect of individual restriction factors on viral replication and by genetic deletion of the sensors.
