Individuals differ in their susceptibility to infection, and part of this difference is inherited. Variants in genes for pattern recognition receptors, cytokines and the molecules of antigen presentation alter how strongly a person responds to a pathogen. Medzhitov and Janeway (2002) described the receptors whose variation can change the response, and the genetics of these receptors has been studied in infectious and inflammatory diseases.

The classical example is the variation in the genes of the major histocompatibility complex, which influences the peptides a person can present and therefore the responses to many pathogens. Variants that affect the chemokine receptors used by HIV are another well-known case, in which a deletion in one receptor gene greatly reduces susceptibility to infection by some viral strains. The example shows how a change in a host receptor can close a route of infection.

Inherited deficiencies of innate immunity can cause severe infection in childhood. Mutations in the genes for the receptors of interferon or the enzymes of the respiratory burst in phagocytes leave patients vulnerable to particular pathogens, and these rare cases have clarified the function of the genes involved.

Genetic association studies of infectious disease are subject to the difficulties described by Ioannidis (2005). Many candidate gene studies have reported associations that did not replicate, because the sample sizes were small and the analyses were flexible. Genome-wide studies with large samples and independent replication provide much more reliable results.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the genes and their functions. Host genetic susceptibility is studied by genetic association with large cohorts, by sequencing of individuals with severe disease and by functional tests of the variants.
