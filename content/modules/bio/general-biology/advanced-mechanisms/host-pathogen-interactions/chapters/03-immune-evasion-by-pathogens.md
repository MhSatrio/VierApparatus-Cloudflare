The adaptive immune system depends on recognising the surface molecules of a pathogen, and many pathogens have evolved to change or hide them. Antigenic variation is one of the most effective strategies. Trypanosomes, the protozoa that cause sleeping sickness, express a different variant of their surface glycoprotein in successive waves, and each variant is recognised by a new antibody response. The parasite switches among a large repertoire of genes, so the host is always slightly behind. Finlay and Falkow (1997) included such evasion among the common themes of pathogenicity.

Complement is a cascade of proteins that coats microbes and damages their membranes. Many pathogens resist it by expressing molecules that block the cascade or by coating themselves with host proteins that inactivate complement. The resistance is often specific to the host species, which explains why some pathogens infect only humans.

Pathogens also interfere with antigen presentation. Some block the transport of peptides into the endoplasmic reticulum, reducing the MHC class I presentation described in the Immunology module. Others prevent the expression of MHC or of co-stimulatory molecules on infected cells. The loss of presentation makes infected cells less visible to cytotoxic T cells.

Some pathogens secrete molecules that suppress the response of immune cells. Cytokine mimics block the receptors of the signals that activate immune cells, and proteases cleave the signals or the receptors. The result is that the host response is weakened or redirected, and the pathogen survives.

Medzhitov and Janeway (2002) described the innate recognition that these evasion strategies must overcome, and the arms race between recognition and evasion is central to the outcome of infection. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecules of the immune response that the pathogens target. Evasion is studied by measuring antibody and T cell responses to infection, by mutating the evasion genes and by comparing the responses to different strains.
