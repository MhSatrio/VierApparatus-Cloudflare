Hosts and pathogens exert selection on one another. A pathogen that evades a host defence gains an advantage until the host evolves a new defence, and the cycle repeats. Dobzhansky (1973) described the importance of interactions among organisms for their evolution, and host-pathogen interactions are among the most intense examples. The genes that encode the molecules at the interface, such as receptors, adhesins and the molecules that the host uses to recognise the pathogen, often show signs of rapid evolution.

The signature of this selection is an excess of amino acid changes in the interacting proteins. The changes are concentrated on the surfaces that contact each other, because these are the sites where the interaction can be altered. Such patterns have been found in many host-virus systems, and they provide evidence that the interaction has driven the change.

Virulence, the harm a pathogen causes, evolves through a trade-off with transmission. A pathogen that kills its host quickly may reach many new hosts before it dies, but a pathogen that lets its host live longer may spread over a longer period. The balance depends on how the pathogen is transmitted, and it predicts that pathogens transmitted by direct contact with a living host should evolve lower virulence than those transmitted through the environment or by vectors.

Finlay and Falkow (1997) described the pathogenic strategies that such evolution shapes. The arms race and the trade-off together determine which strategies persist in a population.

Coevolution also affects the response to treatment. A drug that targets a pathogen protein creates selection for resistance, and the evolution of resistance parallels the evolution of defences in the host. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular interactions that such evolution alters. The study of coevolution combines molecular evolution, field observation and experimental infection.
