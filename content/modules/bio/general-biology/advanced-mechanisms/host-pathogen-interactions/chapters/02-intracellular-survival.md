A phagocyte engulfs a microbe into a phagosome, which fuses with lysosomes to form a compartment rich in acid and degradative enzymes. Most microbes are killed in this compartment, but intracellular pathogens have evolved ways to survive. Some prevent the fusion of the phagosome with lysosomes, others escape from the phagosome into the cytoplasm before it matures, and others modify the compartment so that it becomes a niche in which they can grow. Finlay and Falkow (1997) described the survival within host cells as a major theme of pathogenicity.

Mycobacterium tuberculosis is an example of a pathogen that persists within macrophages. It arrests phagosome maturation, so the compartment remains less acidic and less hostile, and it can survive for long periods within the host. The bacterium's cell wall lipids and secreted proteins contribute to this block. The persistence explains why tuberculosis can remain latent for decades and reactivate when immunity declines.

Listeria monocytogenes escapes from the phagosome by secreting a pore-forming toxin that disrupts the membrane, and it then moves within the cytoplasm by polymerising host actin behind it. The movement allows the bacterium to spread to neighbouring cells without leaving the intracellular environment. The actin polymerisation is an example of the cytoskeletal dynamics described in the Cytoskeleton module, used by the pathogen for its own purpose.

Some intracellular pathogens are viruses, which replicate within the host cell using its machinery, and others are protozoa, which live in vacuoles that they modify. Each strategy requires the pathogen to match its needs to the compartment it occupies, and the host's response depends on whether it can detect the pathogen in that compartment.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the phagocytic and lysosomal pathways that the pathogens subvert. Intracellular survival is studied by imaging the compartments that contain the pathogen, by mutating the genes that control escape or persistence and by measuring the maturation state of the compartments.
