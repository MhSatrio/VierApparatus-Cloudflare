Entry into a host cell begins with attachment. Many pathogens express adhesins, surface proteins that bind receptors on host cells with high specificity. The specificity determines which tissues a pathogen can infect, and it is one reason that pathogens are often restricted to particular hosts or organs. Finlay and Falkow (1997) described attachment as the first common theme of pathogenicity, and the adhesins are the molecules that carry it out.

Once attached, some pathogens are taken up by the host cell through a process that the pathogen induces. Salmonella and Shigella, for example, inject effector proteins that rearrange the actin cytoskeleton of the host cell, forming membrane ruffles that engulf the bacteria. The process exploits the actin dynamics described in the Cytoskeleton module, and it shows how a pathogen can hijack a normal cellular function.

Many bacteria inject effector proteins through specialised secretion systems. These are multi-protein machines that span the membranes of the bacterium and form a channel through which proteins pass into the host cell. The effectors then modify host signalling, cytoskeleton, membrane trafficking or immune pathways. The number of effectors in a pathogen can be large, and each one may act on a different target.

Viruses enter by different routes. They bind receptors on the cell surface and enter by endocytosis, or they fuse their envelope with the host membrane. The entry process is often the target of antibodies and of drugs, and the receptor specificity determines which cells can be infected.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the membrane trafficking and cytoskeletal processes that the pathogens use. Entry is studied by infecting cultured cells with labelled pathogens, by imaging the uptake and by mutating the adhesins and secretion systems.
