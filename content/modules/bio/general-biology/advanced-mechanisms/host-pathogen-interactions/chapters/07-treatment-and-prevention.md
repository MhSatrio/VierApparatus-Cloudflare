Treatment of infection exploits the differences between the pathogen and the host. Antibiotics target bacterial structures, such as the cell wall, the ribosome or the replication machinery, which differ from those of host cells. Antivirals target viral enzymes, such as polymerases and proteases, which have no close counterparts in the host. The selectivity is not complete, and each drug must be judged by the balance between its activity against the pathogen and its toxicity to the host.

Resistance to treatment evolves, as described in the Core Microbiology module. Pathogens that carry a resistance mutation or gene survive treatment and spread, and the pattern of resistance in a population reflects its use of drugs. Combination therapy aims to prevent resistance by requiring several mutations for survival, and the strategy is used in tuberculosis and HIV infection.

Vaccines prevent infection by inducing the immune memory described in the Immunology module. A vaccine presents the pathogen's antigens in a form that is safe but that triggers a protective response, so that later exposure is met by memory cells. Vaccines are most effective against pathogens whose antigens are stable, and they are less effective against those that vary, as antigenic variation described earlier makes the target a moving one.

Ioannidis (2005) described the caution needed when evaluating new treatments, since early trials of many interventions have overstated their benefits. The evaluation of antimicrobial drugs and vaccines requires randomised trials with clinical endpoints, surveillance of resistance and monitoring of safety after licensing.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular targets of the drugs and the immune responses that vaccines induce. Treatment and prevention are studied by clinical trials, by surveillance of pathogen populations and by laboratory measurement of susceptibility.
