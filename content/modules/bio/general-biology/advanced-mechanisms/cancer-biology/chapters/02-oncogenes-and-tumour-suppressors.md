Cancer genes fall into two broad classes. Oncogenes promote growth when they are active, and they are typically activated by a gain of function: a point mutation that makes a protein constitutively active, an amplification that produces many copies, or a rearrangement that places a gene under a strong promoter. Tumour suppressor genes restrain growth, and they are typically inactivated by loss of function, through deletion, mutation or silencing. A mutation in one copy of an oncogene is often enough, while a tumour suppressor usually requires the loss of both copies.

Hanahan and Weinberg (2000) described how the activation of growth signals and the loss of growth inhibition are among the capabilities acquired by cancer cells, and oncogenes and tumour suppressors provide the means of achieving them. The classes correspond to the two sides of a control: an accelerator that is stuck on and a brake that is removed.

The two-hit model, proposed by Knudson for retinoblastoma and later applied widely, explains why hereditary cancers arise at a young age and in several tissues. A child who inherits one defective copy of a tumour suppressor gene carries the first hit in every cell, and a second somatic mutation in any one of many cells is sufficient to start a tumour. Sporadic cancers require both hits to arise in the same cell, which takes longer and is less likely, and the age of onset reflects this difference.

Vogelstein and colleagues (2013) analysed the mutations in many tumours and identified a set of genes that are mutated in many cancers, including several known oncogenes and tumour suppressors. Their analysis showed that a few genes account for most of the driver mutations, and that many others are mutated in only a few tumours.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling pathways in which these genes act. The classification is used to guide the search for targets, since an oncogene can be inhibited and the loss of a tumour suppressor can sometimes be exploited.
