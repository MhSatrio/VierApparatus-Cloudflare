Genomic instability is a rise in the rate at which a cell acquires genetic changes. Hanahan and Weinberg (2011) classed it as an enabling characteristic, because it does not itself confer growth, but it accelerates the acquisition of the other capabilities. A tumour that is unstable can generate the mutations needed for its hallmarks more quickly than a normal cell, and this acceleration explains part of the rise in cancer incidence with age.

Lindahl (1993) showed that DNA is damaged continually by spontaneous chemistry, and the repair pathways described in the DNA Replication and Repair module keep the damage in check. Defects in those pathways, such as the mismatch repair deficiency that causes one hereditary form of colorectal cancer, raise the mutation rate by orders of magnitude. The tumours that arise carry many small mutations, which is the characteristic signature of the defect.

Chromosomal instability is a different form, in which the chromosomes are gained or lost at high rates during division. It produces aneuploidy, which is common in solid tumours. The cause may be defects in the spindle checkpoint, in the attachment of kinetochores or in the control of cohesion. The chromosome changes produced can be large, and they can alter the dosage of many genes at once.

The two forms of instability differ in their consequences. Point mutations act on single genes, while chromosomal changes can affect whole regions. Both raise the chance that a cell acquires a combination of changes that favours growth, and both can be selected as tumour evolution proceeds.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the repair and segregation processes whose defects produce instability. The rate of instability is measured by the frequency of mutations in cultures over time, by karyotyping and by sequencing of tumours.
