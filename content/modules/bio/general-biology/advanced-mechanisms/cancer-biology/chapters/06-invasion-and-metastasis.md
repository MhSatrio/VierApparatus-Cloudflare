Most cancer deaths result from metastasis, the spread of tumour cells to distant organs, rather than from the primary tumour itself. Metastasis is a sequence of steps. Tumour cells must loosen their attachments to neighbours, degrade or move through the surrounding matrix, enter blood or lymphatic vessels, survive the circulation, leave the vessels at a distant site and grow there. Each step is a barrier, and most cells that begin the process fail at one of them. Hanahan and Weinberg (2011) listed invasion and metastasis among the hallmarks, and the cascade describes how it is achieved.

The early steps depend on a change in the cell's identity known as the epithelial-mesenchymal transition. Epithelial cells lose their polarity and their junctions and gain the migratory properties of mesenchymal cells. The transition is driven by transcription factors such as Snail and Twist, which repress the epithelial genes and activate the mesenchymal ones. The changed cells are better able to move, although the state is reversible, and cells may revert to an epithelial state after they colonise a new site.

Invading cells move through the matrix by forming protrusions and by degrading the matrix with proteases. The machinery is the same as that of normal cell migration described in the Cytoskeleton module, but it is used inappropriately. The matrix provides both a barrier and a set of signals that guide the invading cells.

Survival in the circulation is difficult. Cells in the blood are subject to shear forces and to attack by the immune system, and most die. Those that survive may form clusters with platelets or other cells that protect them. Arrival at a distant site is followed by a long period in which the cells may remain dormant before growing.

The organ in which a metastasis grows depends partly on the tumour type and partly on the chemistry and the cells of the target organ. The determinants of this pattern are a subject of study, and the evidence for specific interactions between tumour cells and host cells is strong in some cases and less clear in others. Ioannidis (2005) described the caution needed when interpreting such associations, which are often based on small series.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell adhesion and migration processes involved. Metastasis is studied in animal models that follow labelled cells through the cascade, in patient samples and by genetic manipulation of the transcription factors that drive the transition.
