Cancer treatment has moved from broadly toxic agents toward therapies that exploit the specific changes of tumour cells. Conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy kills rapidly dividing cells, and it works because tumours divide faster than most normal tissues. The selectivity is partial, which is why the drugs damage the bone marrow, the gut and the hair follicles, and why the therapeutic window is often narrow.

Targeted therapy is based on the driver mutations described by Vogelstein and colleagues (2013). A tumour whose growth depends on an activated kinase can be treated with an inhibitor of that kinase, and some such inhibitors have produced large responses in patients whose tumours carry the target mutation. The approach is selective because it acts on a change that normal cells do not carry, and it illustrates how knowledge of the signalling pathways of the Advanced Cell Signaling module can be turned into treatment.

Resistance is the main limitation. Tumours evolve under treatment, and cells that carry a resistance mutation or that activate a bypass pathway survive and expand. The resistance can be present before treatment in a small fraction of cells, or it can arise during treatment. The evolutionary logic is the same as that described for antibiotic resistance in the Core Microbiology module.

Combination strategies aim to prevent resistance by targeting several pathways at once, so that a cell must acquire several changes to escape. The combinations must be tested carefully, because the toxicity of two drugs can exceed the sum of their effects, and because the benefit of a combination must be shown in controlled trials.

Ioannidis (2005) described how early and small studies can overstate the benefits of treatments, and oncology has many examples of initial responses that did not translate into longer survival. The evaluation of therapy requires trials with survival endpoints, independent replication and attention to the proportion of patients who benefit.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the pathways that are the targets of treatment. Therapeutic response is measured in patient-derived models, by imaging and by the molecular analysis of tumours before and after treatment.
