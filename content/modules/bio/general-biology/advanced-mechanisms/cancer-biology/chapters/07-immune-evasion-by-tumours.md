The immune system can recognise and destroy tumour cells, because tumour mutations often produce novel peptides that are presented on MHC molecules, as described in the Immunology module. Hanahan and Weinberg (2011) added the evasion of immune destruction to the hallmarks, and tumours have several means of achieving it. The first is the loss of the antigens that the immune system recognises, through reduced presentation on MHC or loss of the presented peptide. Medzhitov and Janeway (2002) described the innate recognition of microbial patterns, and tumours escape both innate and adaptive recognition by reducing the signals that alert the immune system.

The second mechanism is the suppression of effector cells. Tumours secrete factors such as transforming growth factor beta and prostaglandins that inhibit cytotoxic T cells and natural killer cells. The tumour microenvironment is often rich in regulatory T cells and in myeloid cells that suppress the response, and these populations are recruited by tumour signals.

The third mechanism involves inhibitory checkpoints. T cells express receptors such as CTLA-4 and PD-1 that dampen their activity when engaged. Tumour cells can express the ligands of PD-1, which switches off the T cells that infiltrate the tumour. Antibodies that block these interactions release the brake on the T cells, and they have produced durable responses in some patients with several cancers.

Checkpoint blockade does not work in all patients. Response depends on the number of mutations in the tumour, which can generate antigens, on the presence of T cells in the tumour and on the balance of suppressive cells. Patients whose tumours lack these features often do not respond, and the search for biomarkers to predict response is active.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the T cell receptor, the checkpoint molecules and the signals that regulate them. Immune evasion is studied by analysis of tumour infiltrates, by measuring the response to checkpoint blockade in animal models and by clinical trials that stratify patients by biomarker.
