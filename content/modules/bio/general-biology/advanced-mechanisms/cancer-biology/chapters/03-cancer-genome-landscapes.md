Sequencing of tumour genomes has revealed that each cancer carries many somatic mutations, most of which have no effect on its growth. Vogelstein and colleagues (2013) distinguished the driver mutations, which contribute to the development of the cancer, from the passenger mutations, which accumulate without contributing. The distinction is central, because the drivers are the targets for understanding and for treatment, while the passengers record the history of the tumour's damage and replication.

Driver genes are identified by statistical analysis. A gene that is mutated more often than expected by chance in a set of tumours, and whose mutations alter its function in a way consistent with a role in growth, is a candidate driver. The expected rate depends on the mutation rate of the tissue and on the length of the gene, so the analysis must correct for these factors. Vogelstein and colleagues used such methods to identify a small number of genes that are mutated in many cancers.

Mutational signatures are patterns of base changes that reflect the processes that damaged the DNA. Exposure to ultraviolet light produces a characteristic pattern of changes at dipyrimidine sites, as the nucleotide excision repair described in the DNA Replication and Repair module would predict. Tobacco carcinogens leave another pattern, and defects in mismatch repair or in homologous recombination leave yet others. The signatures allow the history of a tumour's exposures and repair defects to be read from its genome.

Ioannidis (2005) described how selecting findings from many tests can produce false positives, and genome-wide analyses are subject to this problem. Driver claims require correction for multiple testing, validation in independent cohorts and functional tests that show the mutation alters the cell's behaviour.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the pathways in which driver genes act. Genome landscapes are studied by sequencing tumours and matched normal tissue, by statistical identification of recurrent mutations and by functional experiments in cells and animals.
