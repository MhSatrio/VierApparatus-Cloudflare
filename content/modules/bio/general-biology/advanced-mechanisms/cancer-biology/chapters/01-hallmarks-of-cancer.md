Hanahan and Weinberg (2000) proposed that the diverse cancers share a small set of acquired capabilities. A tumour cell must be able to sustain its own growth signals, to ignore signals that inhibit growth, to avoid programmed death, to divide without limit, to induce blood vessels, and to invade and spread. Each capability is a change that alters a normal cellular control, and the set of changes needed for a cancer is the product of a sequence of mutations and selection.

The 2011 revision extended the list. It added the reprogramming of energy metabolism, which allows tumour cells to sustain growth in the face of their demand for biosynthesis, and the evasion of immune destruction. It also recognised genome instability and inflammation as enabling characteristics that increase the rate at which the other capabilities arise. Hanahan and Weinberg (2011) presented these as the next generation of the framework, and their revision reflected the accumulation of evidence over a decade.

The hallmarks are useful because they connect many different mechanisms to a common purpose. A mutation in a growth factor receptor, the loss of a checkpoint protein and the activation of a metabolic enzyme may all contribute to the same hallmark, and the framework shows that they are alternative routes to the same advantage.

The framework has limits. Not every tumour acquires every hallmark in the same way, and the relative importance of each differs among cancers. The hallmarks describe the cell, not the tissue, and they do not explain why cancer incidence rises with age, which reflects the time needed for the changes to accumulate.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling and control pathways whose disruption produces the hallmarks. The framework is used to design experiments, to classify tumours and to identify therapeutic targets.
