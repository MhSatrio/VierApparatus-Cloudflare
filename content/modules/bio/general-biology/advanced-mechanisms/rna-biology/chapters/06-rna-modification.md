RNA is chemically modified after it is made, and more than a hundred different modifications have been described. The most common in eukaryotic messenger RNA is methylation of adenosine at the N6 position, and the cap at the five-prime end carries a methylated guanosine. Transfer and ribosomal RNAs carry a much larger range of modifications, including pseudouridine, in which the sugar is attached to a different position of uracil. Watson and Crick (1953) described the base pairs of nucleic acids, and each modification alters the chemistry of a base that otherwise pairs in a defined way.

The modifications change function. A methylated adenosine in a messenger RNA can be recognised by reader proteins that affect the stability or translation of the transcript. Pseudouridine stabilises the structure of transfer RNA and ribosomal RNA, which is important for their function in translation. Modifications in the anticodon of transfer RNA alter which codons it can read and how efficiently.

The enzymes that place the marks are the writers, and proteins that recognise them are the readers. Erasers, which remove marks, have also been identified for some modifications, so that the state of an RNA can be changed dynamically. The system parallels the histone code described in the epigenetics module, although the marks and their biology differ.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the modified bases and their roles in translation. Modifications are mapped by sequencing methods that detect the chemical change, by mass spectrometry of digested RNA and by mutation of the enzymes that make them.

The study of RNA modification has expanded quickly, and some reported functions depend on detection methods that are still being refined. The strongest conclusions come from experiments that show a specific change in function when a particular modification is removed.
