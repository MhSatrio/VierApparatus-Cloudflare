Non-coding RNAs are transcripts that are not translated into protein. The classical examples are transfer RNAs, which carry amino acids to the ribosome, and ribosomal RNAs, which form the structure and catalytic centre of the ribosome. Both were known for decades before the others were discovered, and they remain essential to translation. Transfer RNAs are small molecules with a characteristic cloverleaf structure and an anticodon that pairs with the codon of the messenger.

Small nuclear RNAs form complexes with proteins to make the spliceosome, which removes introns from precursor messenger RNAs, as described in the Molecular Biology module. Small nucleolar RNAs guide the chemical modification of ribosomal RNA, directing the enzymes to specific positions by base pairing. Both classes act through the complementarity of their sequence to a target RNA, and both are required for the production of the ribosome.

Carthew and Sontheimer (2009) described the small regulatory RNAs, which include microRNAs and small interfering RNAs. These are typically about twenty to twenty-five nucleotides long, and they regulate gene expression by base pairing with messenger RNAs. Their discovery was a major change in the view of RNA, because it showed that many non-coding transcripts are produced for regulatory purposes.

The number of non-coding transcripts in the genome is large. Many are produced from regions once thought to be silent, and their function is often unknown. Some may be byproducts of transcription with no function, and others may have roles that are only detectable under specific conditions. The question of how many have a function is one of the central problems of genomics.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biogenesis and function of the established classes. Non-coding RNAs are identified by sequencing of small and total RNA fractions, and their function is tested by deletion of the gene or by blocking the RNA with antisense molecules.
