A single strand of RNA can fold back on itself, forming base pairs between complementary segments. The resulting secondary structure contains stems, which are paired regions, and loops, which are unpaired. The stems are built from the same Watson-Crick pairs that hold the DNA double helix together, and additional non-canonical pairs stabilise the more complex folds. Watson and Crick (1953) established the pairing rules, and RNA exploits them within a single molecule as well as between two.

The folding of RNA into tertiary structures produces three-dimensional shapes that can bind other molecules and catalyse reactions. The ribosome is the largest example, and its peptidyl transferase centre is formed from ribosomal RNA. The ribozymes discovered later are smaller RNA molecules that catalyse their own cleavage or the cleavage of other RNAs, and their existence showed that RNA can perform the catalytic role that proteins were thought to carry out alone.

Riboswitches are RNA elements in messenger RNAs that bind a small metabolite and change their structure in response. The binding alters whether the transcript forms a terminator or allows translation to proceed, so the messenger regulates its own expression. Riboswitches are common in bacteria and are found in some eukaryotes, and they provide an example of RNA acting as a sensor.

The structure of RNA is flexible, and many structures depend on metal ions and on proteins that stabilise them. The folding is therefore a dynamic process, and the functional state of an RNA may be only one of several populations.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the structures of the ribosome and the catalytic roles of RNA. RNA structure is studied by chemical probing, by X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy of RNA-protein complexes, and by computational prediction of folding.
