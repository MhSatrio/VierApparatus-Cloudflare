MicroRNAs are small RNAs that regulate messenger RNAs. Most are transcribed by RNA polymerase II from genes that may lie within introns or in intergenic regions. The primary transcript folds into a hairpin, which is cleaved in the nucleus by the enzyme Drosha, releasing a precursor of about seventy nucleotides. The precursor is exported to the cytoplasm, where the enzyme Dicer cleaves the hairpin loop, leaving a short double-stranded RNA about twenty-two nucleotides long.

Carthew and Sontheimer (2009) described how one strand of the duplex is loaded into an Argonaute protein to form the RNA-induced silencing complex, while the other strand is usually discarded. The loaded strand guides the complex to target messenger RNAs by base pairing. The seed region, nucleotides two to eight of the microRNA, is the most important for recognition, and the match between it and the target determines how strongly the target is regulated.

Targets are usually recognised with partial complementarity. A microRNA pairs with the three-prime untranslated region of a messenger RNA with imperfect matches, which is sufficient to recruit the silencing machinery. The effect is to reduce translation and to promote the degradation of the messenger. Because of this partial pairing, a single microRNA can regulate hundreds of targets, and a single messenger can be regulated by several microRNAs.

The consequence of this design is that microRNAs act as tuners rather than switches. They reduce the expression of many genes modestly, which can stabilise a state and buffer variation. Many microRNAs are expressed in specific cell types, and their patterns contribute to the identity of those cells.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the enzymes of the microRNA pathway and the Argonaute complex. MicroRNAs are identified by small RNA sequencing, their targets are predicted computationally and tested by reporter assays and by mutating the target sites.
