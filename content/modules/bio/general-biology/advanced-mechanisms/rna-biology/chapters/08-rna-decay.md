Messenger RNAs do not last forever. Each has a half-life that ranges from minutes to many hours, and the rate of degradation is a major determinant of how much protein a gene produces. The decay of a messenger usually begins with the shortening of its poly(A) tail, a process called deadenylation. The shortened tail reduces the binding of proteins that protect the RNA, and the messenger is then degraded from one end or the other.

Two main pathways follow deadenylation. In one, the cap at the five-prime end is removed by a decapping enzyme, and the RNA is degraded by an exonuclease that proceeds in the five-prime to three-prime direction. In the other, the RNA is degraded from the three-prime end by an exosome, a complex of exonucleases. The two pathways can operate on the same transcript, and the choice depends on the sequence and the proteins bound to it.

Carthew and Sontheimer (2009) described how microRNAs and small interfering RNAs promote the decay of their targets, and the silencing complexes recruit the decay machinery to the messenger. Decay is therefore part of the regulatory system, not only a means of disposing of the RNA. The rate of decay adjusts the amount of protein made from a messenger in response to the activity of these silencing pathways.

Decay rates differ among transcripts, and this variation contributes to the regulation of gene expression. Transcripts that encode regulatory proteins often have short half-lives, which allows them to be changed rapidly. Stable transcripts, such as those for abundant structural proteins, persist longer, and their expression can change more slowly.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the enzymes of the decay pathways. Decay rates are measured by blocking transcription and following the disappearance of the messenger, by metabolic labelling of new RNA and by sequencing approaches that measure synthesis and decay together.
