Long non-coding RNAs are transcripts longer than about two hundred nucleotides that are not translated. They are transcribed from many genomic regions, including promoters, enhancers and introns, and they are often expressed in specific cell types or at specific stages. The class is large and heterogeneous, and its members act through several mechanisms, which makes general statements about them difficult.

One mechanism is the recruitment of chromatin regulators. Some long RNAs bind complexes that modify histones, such as the Polycomb system, and guide them to particular genes. Jenuwein and Allis (2001) described the histone modifications that these complexes deposit, and the RNA is proposed to provide the targeting information. The evidence for this mechanism is strong in some cases and weaker in others.

A second mechanism is the act of transcription itself. The process of making a long RNA can interfere with the transcription of a neighbouring gene, or it can help to open the chromatin, so that the function lies in the transcription rather than in the RNA molecule. This makes it difficult to attribute an effect to the transcript when deleting the locus removes both.

Long non-coding RNAs can also act on other RNAs by base pairing, altering their splicing, stability or translation. Some act as sponges that bind microRNAs, although the importance of this mechanism in vivo is debated, because the molecules must be present at high levels to have an effect.

Ioannidis (2005) pointed out that large numbers of candidate findings are vulnerable to false positives, and the long non-coding RNA field has produced many reports that have not been confirmed. Establishing function requires deleting the transcript without disturbing the locus, showing that the effect is specific and replicating the result in independent systems.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the mechanisms of transcription and chromatin regulation through which these RNAs act. Long non-coding RNAs are identified by sequencing and characterised by their localisation, their interactions and the effects of their depletion.
