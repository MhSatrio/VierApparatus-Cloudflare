Many messenger RNAs are not distributed evenly through the cytoplasm. They are transported to specific regions, where they are translated locally, so that the protein is made where it is needed. This localisation is a means of creating polarity, which is essential in cells that have distinct ends, such as the neurons described in the Histology module and the oocytes of many animals. The transported RNA carries a signal, usually in its three-prime untranslated region, that is recognised by a transport complex.

Nüsslein-Volhard and Wieschaus (1980) identified the maternal genes that establish the axes of the fruit fly embryo, and several of the proteins that define the anterior and posterior ends are encoded by messenger RNAs that are localised to the appropriate pole of the oocyte. The localisation of these messages in the egg creates the first asymmetry that the embryo uses to build its body plan.

Transport is carried out along the cytoskeleton. Motor proteins that walk along microtubules or actin filaments carry RNA-protein complexes to their destinations, as described in the Cell Biology module. The RNA is anchored at the destination, and it is translated only after it arrives, which prevents the protein from being made in the wrong place.

In neurons, localised translation supports the local response of dendrites and axons. Messenger RNAs transported into the dendrites are translated in response to synaptic activity, which allows each synapse to change its protein content independently of the cell body. The process is believed to contribute to the storage of memory, although the details are still being studied.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the transport machinery and the signals in the RNA that direct it. Localisation is studied by labelling RNA in living cells, by imaging of translation at specific sites and by mutating the localisation signals.
