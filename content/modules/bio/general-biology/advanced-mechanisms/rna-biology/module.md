# RNA Biology

For much of the twentieth century, RNA was regarded as a passive intermediate: a copy of the gene that carried its information to the ribosome. The discovery that RNA has catalytic activity, that many transcripts are never translated and that small RNAs regulate other RNAs changed that view. RNA is now understood as a regulator in its own right, active in gene silencing, in the control of chromatin, in the defence against viruses and in the organisation of cells.

The module begins with RNA structure and the functions that structure allows, then covers the classes of non-coding RNA. The central chapters address microRNAs and small interfering RNAs, which act through the same silencing machinery. The later chapters discuss long non-coding RNAs, the chemical modifications that change RNA behaviour, the localisation of RNA within cells and the processes that degrade it.

Watson and Crick (1953) established that RNA, like DNA, carries information in the sequence of its bases, and the base-pairing that makes DNA a template also makes RNA a regulator, since short RNAs can recognise complementary sequences in other RNAs. Carthew and Sontheimer (2009) reviewed the mechanisms by which this recognition is used, and their review structures the central chapters of this module.

Readers should keep the distinction between evidence and interpretation in mind. The number of non-coding RNAs is large, and for many of them the function is not established, which the chapters make clear.
