Many diseases involve changes in the epigenome. The most studied is cancer, in which tumours typically show two patterns at once. Tumour suppressor genes, whose products restrain cell division, often acquire methylation in their promoters that silences them. At the same time, large regions of the genome lose methylation, which can destabilise chromosomes and activate genes that should be silent. Bird (2002) described the importance of methylation patterns for the maintenance of normal cell identity, and the disruption of these patterns is a hallmark of many tumours.

Histone modifications are also altered in cancer. Mutations in the enzymes that write or erase marks can change the balance of activating and repressive states, and some tumours carry mutations in histone genes themselves. The changes are often linked to the altered expression of genes controlling growth, and they can make a tumour dependent on the enzymes that maintain the altered marks, which creates a potential therapeutic target.

Epigenetic drugs have been developed that inhibit DNA methyltransferases or histone deacetylases. Some of these drugs are approved for certain cancers, and they illustrate how a mechanistic understanding can lead to treatment. Their effects are broad, however, because the enzymes act on many genes, and the response of a tumour depends on which of its genes are reactivated and on the context. The side effects can be significant.

Epigenetic biomarkers have been proposed for diagnosis and for the prediction of response to treatment. The methylation state of a gene can be measured in blood or tissue, and some markers are in clinical use. Ioannidis (2005) described how many biomarker studies are small and flexibly analysed, and a marker that performs well in the study that discovered it may fail in an independent population. The evaluation of a biomarker therefore requires prospective studies in independent cohorts.

Jenuwein and Allis (2001) provided the conceptual framework in which these changes are understood, linking histone marks to the states of chromatin. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell biology of the processes that are disrupted. Epigenetic changes in disease are studied by mapping marks in patient samples, by functional tests in cell lines and by clinical trials of epigenetic drugs.
