The tails of the histone proteins that form the nucleosome extend from its surface, and they carry many residues that can be chemically modified. Lysines can be acetylated or methylated, arginines can be methylated, and serines and threonines can be phosphorylated. Each modification is added by a specific enzyme, removed by another, and read by proteins that contain domains recognising it. The combination of modifications on a nucleosome is thought to form a signal that the cell interprets.

Jenuwein and Allis (2001) proposed that these modifications constitute a code, in which specific marks correlate with specific functional states of chromatin and are read by effector proteins. The code is a useful framework, but its rules are not simple. A single residue may be modified in several ways with different consequences, and the consequences depend on the context in which the mark appears.

Acetylation is generally associated with active chromatin. The addition of an acetyl group neutralises the positive charge of the lysine, which weakens the interaction between the histone tail and the negatively charged DNA. Acetylated lysines are also recognised by bromodomains, which are found in proteins that promote transcription. Histone deacetylases remove the marks and are associated with repression.

Methylation has more varied effects. Methylation of histone H3 lysine 4 is associated with active promoters, and methylation of H3 lysine 9 and lysine 27 is associated with repression. The number of methyl groups, whether one, two or three, can change the outcome, and different reader proteins recognise each state. The methylation patterns are therefore a more complex signal than acetylation.

Phosphorylation of histones is linked to chromosome condensation during division and to the response to DNA damage. The phosphorylation of a particular serine in histone H2A, for example, marks sites of double-strand breaks and recruits repair proteins.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the enzymes that modify histones and the structural basis of their recognition by readers. Histone modifications are mapped genome-wide by chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing, and their functions are tested by mutating the modified residues.
