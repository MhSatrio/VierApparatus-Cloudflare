The histone code hypothesis, proposed by Jenuwein and Allis (2001), states that the pattern of modifications on histones is read by proteins and translated into a defined functional state of chromatin. The modifications are the letters, the reader proteins are the readers, and the outcome is a transcriptional state, a repair response or a stage of division. The idea is attractive because it offers a way for a cell to store and transmit information about chromatin in a form that other proteins can use.

Reader domains are the molecular basis of the code. A chromodomain binds methylated lysines, a bromodomain binds acetylated lysines, and a PHD finger can bind either, depending on the context. Each domain has a preferred set of modifications, and many reader proteins contain more than one domain. The binding of a reader can recruit enzymes that add further modifications, so that a mark can spread along the chromatin and reinforce itself.

The evidence for combinatorial action is mixed. Some reader proteins bind a particular combination of marks more strongly than either mark alone, which supports the idea of a code. Other studies show that single modifications can be sufficient for a function, and that the same combination can produce different outcomes depending on the cell type. Ioannidis (2005) pointed out that findings based on flexible analysis of many correlations can fail to replicate, and the search for combinatorial codes is vulnerable to this problem. Correlation between marks and function does not establish which mark causes the function.

Experiments that test the code require perturbation of individual marks. Mutations of the modified residues, inhibitors of the enzymes and reader domains that cannot bind a mark give the most direct evidence. The results often show that a mark is necessary but not sufficient, which means that the code is a set of constraints rather than a complete instruction set.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the structure of reader domains and their binding to modified histone tails. The histone code is studied by structural biology of reader complexes, by quantitative binding measurements and by genetic perturbation of the modifying enzymes in cells.
