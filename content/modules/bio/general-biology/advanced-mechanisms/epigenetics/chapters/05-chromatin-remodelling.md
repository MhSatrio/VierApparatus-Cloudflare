Chromatin remodelling complexes change the position, structure or composition of nucleosomes using the energy of ATP hydrolysis. They belong to several families, including SWI/SNF, ISWI, CHD and INO80, and each has characteristic subunits and activities. The complexes are recruited to specific regions by transcription factors and by the modifications described in the previous chapter, and they can open a region of DNA for transcription, repair or replication.

Remodelling takes three broad forms. Sliding moves a nucleosome along the DNA, exposing or hiding the sequences beneath it. Ejection removes a nucleosome altogether, which creates a free stretch of DNA. Exchange replaces a canonical histone with a variant, which can alter the stability of the nucleosome. Each form changes the accessibility of the DNA in a different way, and the complexes are often specialised for one.

Histone variants add another level of control. The variant H2A.Z, for example, is found at the boundaries of active promoters and is thought to make nucleosomes less stable, so that they are easier to remodel. Other variants are found at centromeres and at sites of DNA damage. The choice of variant therefore sets the readiness of a region to respond.

Jenuwein and Allis (2001) described the modifications that recruit remodelling complexes, and the interplay between modification and remodelling is a central theme. A modification may recruit a remodeller that changes the nucleosome, which then exposes a site for a further modification, creating a sequence of events.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the structure of the remodelling motors and the mechanism of ATP-driven translocation. Remodelling is studied with biochemical assays using purified complexes and defined nucleosomes, by measuring nucleosome positions genome-wide and by inactivating the subunits in cells.
