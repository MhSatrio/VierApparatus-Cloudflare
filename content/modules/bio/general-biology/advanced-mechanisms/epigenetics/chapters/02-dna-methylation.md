In mammals, the most common DNA modification is methylation of the fifth carbon of cytosine, usually in the sequence CpG. The methyl group projects into the major groove of the double helix, where it can be read by proteins and can affect the binding of transcription factors. Watson and Crick (1953) described the double helix and its two grooves, and the accessibility of the major groove is what makes methylation functionally important. Methylation is found across most of the genome, but it is absent from many CpG-rich regions that are the promoters of active genes.

Bird (2002) described the enzymes that establish and maintain methylation. The de novo methyltransferases place methyl groups on unmethylated DNA during development, establishing the patterns that differ among cell types. The maintenance methyltransferase copies the pattern after each replication by recognising CpG sites that are methylated on one strand and unmethylated on the other. The two activities together produce stable patterns through many divisions.

Methylation silences genes by two routes. It can block the binding of transcription factors whose recognition sites contain CpG. It can also recruit proteins that bind methylated DNA and that in turn recruit histone deacetylases and other repressive complexes. The silencing of genes on the inactive X chromosome in female mammals and the imprinting of genes inherited from one parent are classical examples of methylation-dependent silencing.

Methylation patterns are altered in disease. Many cancers show hypermethylation of tumour suppressor gene promoters and a general loss of methylation in the rest of the genome. The changes can silence the protective genes and destabilise the genome, and they are used as markers for diagnosis.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the chromatin context of methylation and the proteins that recognise methylated DNA. Methylation is measured by bisulphite sequencing, which converts unmethylated cytosines to uracil, by enrichment methods that isolate methylated fragments and by enzymatic assays of methyltransferase activity.
