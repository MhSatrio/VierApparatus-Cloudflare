The Polycomb group and the Trithorax group are families of proteins that maintain the repressed and active states of developmental genes. They were first identified in the fruit fly, where mutations in them alter the expression of the Hox genes described in the Zoology module, and the same complexes are conserved in mammals. Their importance lies in their ability to remember: once a gene has been set to a state during development, the Polycomb or Trithorax system keeps it there through the divisions that follow.

Polycomb repressive complex 2 deposits methyl groups on lysine 27 of histone H3, a mark that is associated with repression. Polycomb repressive complex 1 recognises this mark through a chromodomain in one of its subunits and ubiquitinates histone H2A, which compacts the chromatin and blocks transcription. The mark is therefore read and written by the same system, which allows it to spread along the chromatin and to be copied after replication.

Trithorax complexes deposit marks associated with activation, such as methylation of lysine 4 of histone H3, and they oppose Polycomb silencing. The balance between the two systems sets the state of a gene, and it can be switched by developmental signals. The memory is maintained because each system recruits the other's opposing activity less effectively in the appropriate context.

Jenuwein and Allis (2001) discussed the histone code in which these marks are read, and the Polycomb system is one of the clearest cases in which a mark is read by a specific domain and used to recruit further activity. The feedback between the reader, the writer and the mark is the basis of the memory.

Bird (2002) described the general concept of epigenetic memory, and the Polycomb system provides a histone-based example, complementary to the DNA methylation discussed in the earlier chapter. Some genes are maintained by both systems together.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the development and the chromatin context in which these complexes act. The systems are studied by mutation of their subunits, by mapping the marks genome-wide and by tracking the expression of target genes through divisions in living embryos.
