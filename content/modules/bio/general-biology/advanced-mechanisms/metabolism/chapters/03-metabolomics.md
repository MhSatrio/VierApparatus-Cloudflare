Metabolomics measures the concentrations of small molecules in cells, tissues or body fluids. It provides a snapshot of the metabolic state, and it can reveal changes in response to a drug, a diet or a disease. The two main analytical platforms are mass spectrometry, often coupled to liquid or gas chromatography, and nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy. Each has advantages: mass spectrometry is sensitive and detects many compounds, while nuclear magnetic resonance is quantitative and reproducible but less sensitive.

Identification is the main difficulty. A mass spectrometer reports the mass and fragmentation pattern of an ion, and these are matched against libraries of reference compounds. Many peaks cannot be identified with confidence, because the library is incomplete or because isomers have the same mass and similar fragments. Confidence in an identification depends on matching retention time and fragmentation with an authentic standard run under the same conditions.

Quantification requires calibration with known concentrations, and the signal of a compound can be suppressed or enhanced by other molecules in the sample. Internal standards, typically labelled versions of the compounds, correct for this variation. Without them, differences between samples may reflect differences in extraction or ionisation rather than in the cell.

Interpretation is subject to the same risks as other large-scale measurements. A study that measures hundreds of metabolites and reports those that differ is likely to find some differences by chance, and the reported associations may not replicate. Ioannidis (2005) described this problem in general terms, and metabolomics requires correction for multiple comparisons and validation in independent samples.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biochemical pathways that produce the metabolites measured. Metabolomics is most informative when combined with flux measurements, because the concentration of a metabolite alone does not show how fast it is made or consumed.
