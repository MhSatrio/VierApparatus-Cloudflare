Metabolic flexibility is the capacity of an organism or tissue to change its fuel use in response to availability and demand. After a meal, most tissues take up glucose and store it, and the liver converts excess carbohydrate to fat. During fasting, the balance shifts: fat is released from adipose tissue, the liver produces glucose from amino acids and glycerol, and many tissues switch to fatty acids and ketone bodies. The switch is coordinated by hormones and by the sensors described in the previous chapter.

Exercise changes fuel use within minutes. Working muscle increases its uptake of glucose and fatty acids, and the proportion of fuel that is oxidised depends on the intensity of the work. High-intensity activity relies more on glycolysis and the breakdown of stored phosphocreatine, while prolonged low-intensity activity relies more on fat oxidation. Lane and Martin (2010) argued that energy supply limits cellular function, and the flexibility of fuel use allows muscle to sustain activity across the range of demand.

Hypoxia, a low oxygen supply, forces a cell to change its metabolism. The reduced flux through oxidative phosphorylation is compensated by increased glycolysis, and the hypoxia-inducible factor coordinates the response by activating genes for glucose uptake and glycolytic enzymes. The same response is used by tumours, which often outgrow their blood supply and become hypoxic. Hypoxic tumour cells can rely on glycolysis and on the supply of lactate from other cells, which shapes their behaviour and their response to treatment.

Proliferating cells show a metabolic programme that supports the synthesis of new cell material. Nucleotides, lipids and amino acids are needed in large amounts, and the pathways that produce them are upregulated. The programme is linked to the signalling pathways described in the Advanced Cell Signaling module, and its disruption is an important target in cancer treatment.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the hormonal and enzymatic changes that accompany these shifts. Flexibility is studied by measuring substrate uptake and oxygen consumption across tissues, by tracing labelled fuels and by imaging the metabolism of tumours.
