A systems view of metabolism treats the cell as a network whose behaviour emerges from the interaction of its parts. The earlier chapters described the tools: control analysis quantifies how the enzymes share control of a flux, flux balance predicts the flux distribution from stoichiometry, metabolomics measures the state of the network, compartmentation divides it, and nutrient sensors link it to growth. A systems study uses several of these tools together, because each one alone gives an incomplete picture.

A typical systems analysis begins with a model of the network, built from known reactions and their stoichiometry. The model is constrained by measured uptake rates and by the composition of the cell, and its predictions are compared with measured fluxes from isotope tracing. Discrepancies point to missing reactions, wrong assumptions or regulation that the model does not include, and the model is revised accordingly.

The model is a hypothesis rather than a description of reality. Whole-cell models must include thousands of parameters, many of which are unmeasured, and the predictions can be sensitive to values that are poorly known. Ioannidis (2005) described how models with many flexible parameters can fit almost any dataset, and the validity of a whole-cell model depends on predictions tested against data that were not used to build or fit it.

Lane and Martin (2010) provided a broad energetic constraint on what a cell can be, and systems models can test whether the metabolism of a cell is consistent with that constraint. A model that predicts energy use beyond the supply available is wrong, and the check is a useful test of the network's structure.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the metabolic pathways that such models represent. The systems view is valuable because it asks how the parts combine, and it reveals properties that are not visible in any single pathway, such as the distribution of control and the effects of perturbing several enzymes at once.
