Metabolism is not carried out in a single space. Glycolysis occurs in the cytosol, the citric acid cycle and fatty acid oxidation occur in mitochondria, and fatty acid synthesis occurs in the cytosol while its oxidation occurs in mitochondria. The same metabolite may be used in several places, and the cell must control which pool it enters. The division of metabolism among compartments is therefore a source of regulation as well as of efficiency.

The key link between compartments is the transport of metabolites across membranes. Transporters move pyruvate into mitochondria, carry citrate out of mitochondria for fatty acid synthesis, and exchange ATP and ADP across the inner membrane. Mitchell (1961) proposed that the membrane gradient of protons drives the transport of metabolites as well as the synthesis of ATP, and the transporters are part of the chemiosmotic system.

Separate pools of the same metabolite can be regulated independently. The NADH of the cytosol and the NADH of the mitochondrial matrix are maintained at different redox states, and the shuttles that move reducing equivalents between them are regulated by the energy state of each compartment. The separation allows the cell to balance energy production against biosynthesis in different places.

Compartmentation also complicates measurement. A measurement of the whole cell averages over the pools, and a change in one compartment may be masked by no change in another. Methods that label compartments separately, such as the targeted sensors that report metabolite concentrations within a specific organelle, are needed to resolve the pools.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the transporters and the organelles of metabolism. Compartmentation is studied by subcellular fractionation, by isotopic tracing that follows labelled carbons into each pool and by genetically encoded sensors.
