Disease often reflects a change in the regulation of the metabolic network rather than the failure of a single enzyme. In type 2 diabetes, the response of target tissues to insulin is reduced, so that glucose uptake and the suppression of glucose production by the liver are impaired. The consequence is hyperglycaemia, and the altered regulation of lipid metabolism contributes to the accumulation of fat and to the risk of cardiovascular disease. The disease is a systems-level change in how the same enzymes are controlled.

The Warburg effect describes the observation that many cancer cells take up large amounts of glucose and convert much of it to lactate, even when oxygen is available. The shift toward glycolysis reduces the efficiency of energy production per glucose, but it supplies intermediates for the synthesis of nucleotides, amino acids and lipids needed for rapid growth. The explanation is an alteration of the balance between energy production and biosynthesis, and the cause is often a change in oncogenic signalling.

Inherited metabolic disorders show that single enzyme defects can have large effects. Phenylketonuria results from a deficiency of an enzyme that converts phenylalanine to tyrosine, and the accumulation of phenylalanine damages the developing brain. Dietary restriction is an effective treatment because it reduces the flux into the blocked pathway, which illustrates how control analysis can guide intervention.

The study of these diseases requires caution. Many associations between metabolites and disease have been reported, and some have not replicated, as Ioannidis (2005) pointed out. The claims must be tested prospectively and their causal role established by interventions.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the regulatory pathways of glucose and lipid metabolism that are disturbed in these diseases. The changes are studied by measuring fluxes in patients and in animal models, by metabolomic profiling and by controlled interventions.
