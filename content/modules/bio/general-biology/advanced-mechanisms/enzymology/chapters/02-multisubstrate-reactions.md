Most enzymes of metabolism act on two substrates, one of which is often a cofactor such as NAD+ or ATP. The kinetics of such reactions cannot be described by the simple Michaelis-Menten model, because the order in which substrates bind and products leave affects the rate. Three general mechanisms are distinguished. In an ordered mechanism, the substrates bind in a fixed sequence and the products leave in a fixed sequence, so that a ternary complex is formed. In a random mechanism, the substrates can bind in either order. In a ping-pong mechanism, the enzyme is modified by the first substrate and product is released before the second substrate binds.

The distinction is made experimentally. The rate is measured at several concentrations of each substrate, and the results are plotted in double reciprocal form. Lines that intersect at a common point indicate a sequential mechanism, while parallel lines indicate a ping-pong mechanism. Product inhibition studies, in which each product is tested as an inhibitor against each substrate, give additional information about the order of binding.

The mechanisms have different consequences for regulation. In an ordered mechanism, the binding of the first substrate can change the affinity for the second, and the enzyme can therefore act as a sensor that responds to the concentration of one metabolite. In a ping-pong mechanism, a covalent intermediate is formed, which can be a target for regulation and can be more vulnerable to inhibitors.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein's properties depend on its structure, and the mechanism of a multisubstrate enzyme depends on how its active site is arranged to accommodate two substrates. The binding order often reflects the geometry of two adjacent sites that cooperate in catalysis.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the enzymes of metabolism that use cofactors in this way. The kinetic methods are applied in the characterisation of new enzymes and in the design of inhibitors that compete with one of the substrates.
