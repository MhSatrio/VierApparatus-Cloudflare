Enzymes evolve, and the evolution of new catalytic activities is one of the main sources of biochemical diversity. A common route is gene duplication. A duplicated gene is freed from the constraint of its original function, so one copy can keep the ancestral activity while the other accumulates mutations that change its specificity. If the change is advantageous, the new enzyme is retained, and the family of related enzymes expands. Dobzhansky (1973) described the importance of such variation, and enzyme families illustrate it at the molecular level.

Promiscuous activities are side reactions that an enzyme performs weakly. A promiscuous activity can provide a starting point for evolution, because it is already present and can be strengthened by a small number of mutations. Many modern enzymes are thought to have arisen from ancestors with broad specificities, which narrowed as the enzymes became specialised.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the sequence determines the structure, and a change in sequence can therefore change the shape of the active site and the activity it supports. Enzymes that share a fold often share an ancestor, and the fold can be adapted to new substrates by changing the residues that line the pocket.

Ancestral sequence reconstruction is a method that infers the sequence of an ancient enzyme from its living descendants, synthesises the protein and measures its activity. The method has shown that ancestral enzymes can have broader specificities than their descendants, and that a small number of substitutions can change the activity of a protein dramatically. The results support the view that specialisation is a gradual process.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the gene families of metabolism and the mechanisms by which genes are duplicated and diverged. The reconstruction of ancestral enzymes requires phylogenetic analysis, and its conclusions depend on the model of sequence evolution used.

The study of enzyme evolution combines phylogenetics, structural biology and kinetics. The best-supported conclusions come from reconstructions that are tested by measuring the activity of the proteins, not only by inferring their sequences.
