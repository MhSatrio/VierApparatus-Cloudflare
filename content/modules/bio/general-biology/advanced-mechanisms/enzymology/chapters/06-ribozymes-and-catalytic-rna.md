The discovery that RNA can catalyse reactions changed the understanding of the origin of life. Ribozymes are RNA molecules with enzymatic activity, and several classes occur in nature. Self-splicing introns remove themselves from a precursor RNA by catalysing their own excision. RNase P cleaves the precursor of transfer RNA. The ribosome, which catalyses peptide bond formation, has an active centre made entirely of ribosomal RNA, with proteins that stabilise it but do not supply the catalytic groups.

Watson and Crick (1953) established that nucleic acid sequences can pair with complementary sequences, and the folding of RNA into specific three-dimensional shapes allows it to bind substrates and to position reactive groups. The chemistry of the ribozymes uses the hydroxyl groups of the RNA backbone and bound metal ions, and the reactions are often reversible in principle, which is consistent with a role in early metabolism.

The RNA world hypothesis proposes that RNA was the first informational and catalytic molecule, and that proteins and DNA came later. The hypothesis draws support from the catalytic activity of RNA, from the central role of ribosomal RNA in the ribosome and from the fact that many cofactors contain nucleotide groups. Miller (1953) showed that amino acids can form under simple conditions, and the question of how nucleotides and RNA formed is a central problem of the same research.

The hypothesis is not established. Nucleotide synthesis under prebiotic conditions is difficult to reproduce, the replication of RNA by RNA alone is slow and error prone, and the evidence for an RNA-only stage is indirect. Some researchers propose that protein and RNA evolved together, while others argue for an earlier, different genetic polymer.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the structure of the ribosome and its RNA-based catalysis. Ribozymes are studied by in vitro selection, which isolates RNAs with a desired activity from a large random pool, and by structural studies of the active sites.
