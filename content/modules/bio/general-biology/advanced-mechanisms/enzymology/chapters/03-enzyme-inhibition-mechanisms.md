An inhibitor reduces the activity of an enzyme. Reversible inhibitors bind and release, and their effect is described by the way they change the apparent kinetic constants. A competitive inhibitor binds the active site and raises the apparent Km while leaving Vmax unchanged, because high substrate can displace it. An uncompetitive inhibitor binds only the enzyme-substrate complex and lowers both Km and Vmax. A non-competitive inhibitor binds a different site with equal affinity for the free enzyme and the complex, reducing Vmax but not Km. A mixed inhibitor binds at a different site and changes the affinity for the substrate as well as the maximum rate.

The kinetic signatures are obtained by measuring rates at several substrate and inhibitor concentrations and fitting the data to the appropriate equations. The fit distinguishes the mechanisms, but only when the data cover a sufficient range. Poorly designed experiments can give an apparently good fit to the wrong model, and the choice of model should be checked by comparing alternatives.

Irreversible inhibitors form covalent bonds with the enzyme, or bind so tightly that they are effectively permanent. Aspirin acetylates a serine in cyclooxygenase, for example, and the enzyme is inactivated until it is synthesised again. Irreversible inhibitors can be very potent, but they must be designed with care so that they do not react with other proteins.

Inhibition is central to pharmacology. Many drugs are enzyme inhibitors, and their potency is measured by the same kinetic analysis. The choice of inhibitor type matters clinically, because a competitive inhibitor is overcome when the substrate concentration rises, while a non-competitive inhibitor is not.

Ioannidis (2005) described how studies of drug effects can be inflated by flexible analysis and selective reporting. Inhibitor potencies must be reported with the conditions of the assay and confirmed in independent systems, since an inhibitor that appears potent in a crude extract may act through a different mechanism in a purified system.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the enzymes of metabolism and the regulatory inhibitors that control them. Inhibition is studied by kinetic analysis, by structural studies of enzyme-inhibitor complexes and by testing compounds in cells.
