Enzymes are named and classified by the reaction they catalyse. The Enzyme Commission, an international body, maintains a system in which each enzyme is given a four-part number. The first digit identifies one of six main classes, the second and third digits identify subclasses and sub-subclasses, and the last digit is a serial number within the group. The system gives a precise way to refer to an enzyme, and it allows reactions to be compared across organisms.

The six classes are oxidoreductases, which catalyse oxidation and reduction; transferases, which move functional groups between molecules; hydrolases, which cleave bonds by adding water; lyases, which cleave bonds without hydrolysis or form double bonds by elimination; isomerases, which rearrange the atoms within a molecule; and ligases, which join two molecules using energy from ATP or a similar source. Most enzymes fall into one of these groups, and a reaction can usually be assigned to a class from its chemistry alone.

The classification describes what an enzyme does, not how it does it. Two enzymes in the same class may use different mechanisms and have unrelated structures. Kendrew and colleagues (1960) showed that the function of a protein depends on its detailed structure, and two enzymes that catalyse the same overall reaction can differ greatly in their folds and active sites.

The classification has limits. Many enzymes are promiscuous and catalyse more than one reaction, and some are misassigned because the original evidence was weak. The database entries must be checked against the literature, and a reported activity in a new organism should be confirmed with purified protein.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the principal enzymes of metabolism organised by their reactions. The classification is used in databases and in the annotation of genomes, where the function of a gene is often inferred from the enzyme class of its closest characterised relative.
