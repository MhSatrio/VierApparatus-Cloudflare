Amino acid side chains provide a limited range of chemical groups, and many reactions require chemistry that they cannot perform. Enzymes overcome this limit with cofactors, non-protein molecules that participate directly in catalysis. Some cofactors are metal ions bound to the protein, others are organic molecules that are chemically changed during the reaction and then regenerated. Many organic cofactors are derived from vitamins, which is why vitamin deficiencies cause specific metabolic failures.

Metal ions are used in catalysis in several ways. A zinc ion in carbonic anhydrase polarises a water molecule so that it can attack carbon dioxide, and an iron ion in cytochrome carries electrons through changes in its oxidation state. Metal ions can also stabilise negative charges, bind substrates and hold the structure of the active site in position. Kendrew and colleagues (1960) showed that myoglobin binds its haem group in a pocket, and the iron of the haem is the site of oxygen binding, a clear example of a metal-containing cofactor determining function.

Flavin cofactors, derived from riboflavin, are versatile carriers of electrons. They can accept one or two electrons, and the semiquinone intermediate allows them to link two-electron and one-electron processes. Flavoproteins are found in many oxidation reactions, including the citric acid cycle and fatty acid oxidation described in the Biochemistry module.

Nicotinamide cofactors, NAD+ and NADP+, carry hydride ions between reactions. They are reduced at a specific carbon of the nicotinamide ring, and the transfer is stereospecific, which means that enzymes can distinguish the two faces of the ring. The use of NAD+ and NADP+ in separate pathways keeps catabolic and anabolic reducing power separate.

Pyridoxal phosphate and thiamine diphosphate are cofactors that stabilise carbanion intermediates and are used in amino acid metabolism and in the decarboxylation of pyruvate. Alberts et al. (2022) describe these cofactors in the context of the pathways they serve. Their structures and reaction mechanisms are established by spectroscopy, by isotope labelling and by crystallography of enzyme complexes.
