Enzyme engineering changes the properties of an enzyme for a new purpose, such as higher activity on a specified substrate, greater stability at high temperature or the ability to act in an organic solvent. Two strategies dominate. Rational design uses knowledge of the structure and mechanism to choose specific residues to change. Directed evolution creates many variants by random mutagenesis or recombination, screens them for the desired property and repeats the cycle with the best variants. The strategies are often combined.

Rational design depends on the quality of the structural and mechanistic information. A change to an active site residue may improve one property and damage another, because the residues that stabilise the transition state can also influence the folding. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the sequence determines the fold, and a change near the active site can alter the whole structure, so the effects of a design must be checked experimentally.

Directed evolution does not require detailed knowledge of the mechanism. It exploits the principle that random variation followed by selection can find improvements that would be difficult to predict. Dill and MacCallum (2012) described the landscape of folding, and directed evolution explores that landscape by sampling variants and retaining those that fold and function well.

The trade-offs are central. Increasing activity often reduces stability, because the flexibility that allows catalysis also makes the structure more vulnerable to unfolding. Stability and activity are therefore optimised together, and engineers must decide which property matters most for the application.

Ioannidis (2005) described how selection and reporting of results can inflate apparent improvements, and the evaluation of an engineered enzyme requires replicate measurements, controls and independent testing under the conditions of use.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the enzymes of metabolism that are targets of engineering. Engineered enzymes are characterised by kinetic assays, by structural analysis of the variants and by testing their performance in the intended setting.
