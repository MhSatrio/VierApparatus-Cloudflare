# Chromosome Biology

A chromosome is a single very long molecule of DNA, packaged with proteins into a structure that must be replicated, regulated and divided with great precision. Its organisation is therefore a combination of several requirements: the DNA must be compact enough to fit inside the nucleus, accessible enough for transcription and copying, and stable enough for the chromosome to be separated accurately at each division. This module examines how these requirements are met.

The module starts with the folding of chromosomes from the nucleosome to the mitotic structure, then considers the centromere and kinetochore that attach chromosomes to the spindle. The following chapters cover the cohesin and condensin complexes that hold and shape the chromosome, the telomeres at the ends, the process of segregation, the three-dimensional organisation of chromosomes within the nucleus and the aberrations that arise when the organisation fails.

Watson and Crick (1953) described the double helix whose length is the basis of the chromosome problem: a human chromosome contains a DNA molecule many centimetres long, which must be compressed by several orders of magnitude to fit in the nucleus. The chapters show how the cell achieves that compaction without making the DNA inaccessible.

Readers should connect this module to the replication and repair mechanisms of the previous module, since replication and segregation depend on the same chromosome structure.
