In the interphase nucleus, each chromosome tends to occupy a distinct region, called a chromosome territory. The territories were first inferred from the observation that labelled chromosomes stay in separate regions when cells are fixed, and they have since been confirmed by fluorescence in situ hybridisation, which labels a whole chromosome with a set of probes. Territories are not sharply bounded, and chromatin from neighbouring chromosomes intermingles at their borders, but the general arrangement is reproducible from cell to cell.

The nucleus is organised into compartments. Active chromatin tends to lie toward the interior of the nucleus, where transcription factors and polymerases are concentrated, while repressed chromatin tends to lie against the nuclear periphery. The nuclear lamina, a meshwork of intermediate filaments on the inner surface of the nuclear envelope, binds large domains of repressed chromatin, and these lamina-associated domains are a major feature of nuclear organisation.

The position of a gene relative to the periphery or to the interior is associated with its activity, although the association is a correlation rather than a rule. Moving a gene to the periphery can sometimes repress it, and moving it to the interior can sometimes activate it. The experiments show that position can contribute to regulation without being its only determinant.

Jenuwein and Allis (2001) discussed the histone marks that define chromatin states, and the periphery is associated with the repressive marks described in the epigenetics module. The nuclear organisation is therefore part of the epigenetic state of the genome, and the two are linked.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the nuclear envelope and the lamina that form the periphery. Nuclear organisation is studied by fluorescence in situ hybridisation, by chromosome conformation capture that maps contacts between distant regions and by imaging of labelled loci in living cells.
