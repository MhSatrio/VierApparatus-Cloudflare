Chromosome aberrations are changes in the number or structure of chromosomes. Structural aberrations include deletions, in which a piece is lost; duplications, in which a piece is repeated; inversions, in which a segment is reversed; and translocations, in which segments move between chromosomes. Numerical aberrations include aneuploidy, in which a chromosome is gained or lost, and polyploidy, in which whole sets are duplicated. Both kinds can arise from errors in the processes described in the previous chapters.

Structural aberrations usually begin as double-strand breaks. Lindahl (1993) described how DNA is damaged continually, and when two breaks are misrepaired by joining the wrong ends, a deletion, inversion or translocation results. Breaks that are joined by non-homologous end joining can produce translocations between chromosomes that are close in the nucleus, and recombination between repeated sequences can produce deletions and duplications.

Numerical aberrations usually arise from failures of segregation, as described in the chapter on segregation. The most common in humans is trisomy, in which a chromosome is present in three copies. Trisomy 21, which causes Down syndrome, is the best-known example, and most aneuploidies are lethal in early development.

The consequences depend on the chromosome and on the genes it carries. A deletion that removes an essential gene can be lethal, while a balanced translocation may have no effect on the carrier but can cause problems in the offspring. Some aberrations are important in evolution, because a rearrangement that suppresses recombination can keep a set of genes together, which has been proposed as a route to new species.

Cancer cells often carry many aberrations. The changes can activate oncogenes or inactivate tumour suppressors, and the pattern of aberrations is used to classify tumours and to guide treatment. The karyotype of a tumour is therefore a record of the errors that accumulated during its growth.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the repair pathways whose errors produce these changes. Aberrations are detected by karyotyping, by fluorescence in situ hybridisation, by array-based methods that measure copy number and by sequencing that reveals the breakpoints of rearrangements.
