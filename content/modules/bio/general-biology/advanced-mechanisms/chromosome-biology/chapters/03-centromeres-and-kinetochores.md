The centromere is the region of a chromosome to which the spindle attaches during division. It is defined epigenetically in most organisms. A specialised histone variant, CENP-A in humans, replaces histone H3 in the centromeric nucleosomes, and this variant provides the foundation on which the kinetochore assembles. The sequence of the DNA is not the only determinant: centromeres can move to new positions over evolutionary time, which shows that the mark is inherited through the chromatin rather than fixed by the sequence alone.

Jenuwein and Allis (2001) described histone variants and modifications as carriers of information, and the centromeric variant is an example of a mark that defines a functional region. The chromatin at the centromere is maintained through each replication by the loading of new CENP-A, which is coupled to the cell cycle.

The kinetochore is a large protein assembly that forms on the centromere. Its inner part binds the chromatin, and its outer part binds the microtubules of the spindle. Each kinetochore attaches to the plus ends of several microtubules, and the attachment is dynamic. Microtubules grow and shrink, and the kinetochore moves with them, which allows chromosomes to be pulled toward the poles during anaphase.

Attachment errors are detected by the spindle assembly checkpoint. A kinetochore that is not attached to the spindle produces a signal that inhibits the anaphase-promoting complex, which keeps sister chromatids together until all of them are correctly attached under tension. The tension produced by bipolar attachment is sensed by the kinetochore and stabilises the attachment.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the mitotic spindle and the proteins that regulate the attachment. Centromeres and kinetochores are studied by imaging with fluorescent markers, by mapping the CENP-A distribution genome-wide and by experiments that disrupt the checkpoint or the attachments.
