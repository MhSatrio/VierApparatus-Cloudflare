The ends of a linear chromosome are called telomeres. They consist of many copies of a short repeated sequence, rich in guanine on the strand that runs five-prime to three-prime toward the end. The repetitive structure exists because of a problem that the replication machinery cannot solve by itself. Watson and Crick (1953) described the antiparallel strands, and the lagging strand cannot be copied all the way to the end, because the last primer has no template beyond the chromosome end.

Bell and Dutta (2002) described the discontinuous replication of the lagging strand, and the end-replication problem follows from it. Each round of replication shortens the chromosome by the length of the final primer region, and without a correction the ends would erode with every division until essential genes were lost.

Telomerase solves the problem. It is a reverse transcriptase that carries its own RNA template, which contains the sequence that is repeated at the telomere. The enzyme extends the chromosome end by adding repeats, and it is active in germ cells, stem cells and most cancers, but mostly silent in ordinary somatic cells. The shortening of telomeres in those cells is one of the mechanisms that limits the number of divisions they can make.

The shelterin complex protects the telomere. Its proteins bind the telomeric repeats and form a loop structure in which the end of the chromosome is tucked back into the duplex. The loop hides the end from the DNA damage response, which would otherwise treat the free end as a double-strand break and attempt to join it to other chromosomes.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the telomere structure and the proteins that protect it. Telomeres are measured by fluorescence in situ hybridisation, by quantitative PCR and by sequencing of the terminal regions, and their function is tested by mutating telomerase or the shelterin subunits.
