The nucleosome core consists of an octamer of histones, two each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4, around which about 147 base pairs of DNA are wrapped in just under two turns. The DNA follows a path that is bent by the histone surface, and the wrapping is stabilised by many contacts between the basic histone surface and the phosphate backbone. The structure is highly conserved across eukaryotes, which indicates its fundamental importance.

Watson and Crick (1953) described the DNA helix as a regular structure with about ten base pairs per turn. Wrapping the helix around the histone octamer requires it to bend sharply, and the sequence of the DNA influences how easily it bends. Some sequences favour the nucleosome and others resist it, so the positions of nucleosomes along the genome are partly encoded in the sequence.

Linker histone H1 binds the DNA where it enters and leaves the nucleosome, and it contributes to the compaction of the fibre. The linker histone is not part of the core, and its presence changes the spacing of nucleosomes in the fibre. Removal of linker histone makes chromatin more accessible, which is one of the ways a cell can open a region for transcription.

Nucleosome positioning determines what is accessible. Promoters are often kept free of nucleosomes, with a precisely positioned array of nucleosomes beside them, and this organisation allows the transcription factors and polymerase to bind. Jenuwein and Allis (2001) described how the modifications on the histone tails influence the position and stability of the nucleosome, which links the positioning to the marks described in the epigenetics module.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the histone proteins and the chromatin structures they form. Nucleosome positions are mapped by digestion with nuclease followed by sequencing, and the effect of individual histones and marks is tested by mutation and by reconstitution with defined sequences.
