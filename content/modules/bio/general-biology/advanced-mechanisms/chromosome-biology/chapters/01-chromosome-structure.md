A human chromosome contains a single DNA double helix whose total length is several centimetres. Watson and Crick (1953) described the helix, and the compaction of such a molecule into a chromosome that is a few micrometres long requires about four orders of magnitude of folding. The cell achieves this in stages, each stage reducing the length by a further factor and each relying on different proteins.

The first level is the nucleosome, in which about 147 base pairs wrap around a histone octamer. Nucleosomes repeat along the DNA with a spacing of about 200 base pairs on average, forming a beads-on-a-string arrangement that reduces the length about sevenfold. The second level is thought to be a fibre of nucleosomes that coils into a more compact structure, although the precise arrangement of the fibre in cells is still debated.

Higher-order folding forms loops and domains. The DNA is organised into loops anchored at protein structures, and these loops are grouped into domains, as discussed in the chapter on enhancers. The loops reduce the length further while keeping regions of DNA accessible for the processes that act on them.

At mitosis the chromosome condenses dramatically, and the structure becomes a compact rod. Condensation depends on the condensin complex, which folds the chromatin into a series of loops around a central axis. Jenuwein and Allis (2001) described the histone modifications that accompany this condensation, and phosphorylation of histone H3 is one of the marks associated with mitotic chromosomes.

Replication adds a further requirement. Bell and Dutta (2002) described how replication passes through the chromatin, and the chromosome must be decondensed sufficiently for replication to proceed, then recondensed for segregation.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the proteins that drive compaction and the stages of the cell cycle at which they act. Chromosome structure is studied by electron microscopy of chromosome spreads, by chromosome conformation capture, which maps the contacts along the DNA, and by live imaging of chromosomes in dividing cells.
