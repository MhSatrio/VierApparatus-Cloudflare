Segregation is the final step of division, in which the two copies of each chromosome are separated and distributed to daughter cells. It begins at the metaphase-to-anaphase transition, when the anaphase-promoting complex triggers the degradation of securin. Securin inhibits separase, a protease that cleaves cohesin, and its destruction releases the enzyme to remove cohesion from the sister chromatids. Each pair is then pulled apart by the shortening of the spindle microtubules attached to their kinetochores.

The timing of anaphase is tightly controlled. The spindle assembly checkpoint holds the anaphase-promoting complex inactive until every kinetochore is attached and under tension, as described in the chapter on kinetochores. Once the checkpoint is satisfied, the transition is switched rapidly and irreversibly, which ensures that all chromosomes separate together and that no chromosome is left behind.

The separation of sisters depends on the forces generated by the spindle. Kinetochores move toward the poles as microtubules depolymerise at their plus ends and at their minus ends, and motor proteins help to push the poles apart. The combination of these movements produces a coordinated segregation.

Errors in segregation produce aneuploidy, a condition in which a cell has an abnormal number of chromosomes. Jenuwein and Allis (2001) noted that chromosome structure and chromatin marks are part of the segregation process, and the most common source of error is a failure of kinetochore attachment. Aneuploidy is a major cause of miscarriage in humans, and it is common in cancer cells, where it contributes to genomic instability.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the anaphase machinery and the regulatory proteins that control it. Segregation is studied by live imaging of chromosome movement, by measuring the frequency of lagging chromosomes and by karyotype analysis of cells and tissues.
