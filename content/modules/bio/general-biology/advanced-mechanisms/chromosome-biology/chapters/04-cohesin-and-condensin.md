Two families of protein complexes organise chromosomes at the level of loops. Both belong to the structural maintenance of chromosomes family, and both have a characteristic architecture of two long coiled-coil arms joined at a hinge, with a head at the end of each arm that binds ATP. Cohesin holds sister chromatids together from the time they are made in S phase until they separate at anaphase. Condensin folds the chromosomes during mitosis, giving them the rod-shaped form seen under the microscope.

Cohesin entraps the two sister DNA molecules in a ring formed by its subunits. The ring is loaded onto the chromatin by a loader complex, and it is established during replication, when the fork passes through the DNA. Bell and Dutta (2002) described replication in eukaryotes, and the establishment of cohesion is linked to the progression of the fork. The cohesion is removed at anaphase by a protease that cleaves one of the subunits, which opens the ring and allows the sisters to separate.

Condensin acts on chromosomes during mitosis to shorten and thicken them. It is thought to fold the chromatin into loops along a central axis, and the loops are anchored to the axis by the complex. The activity of condensin is controlled by phosphorylation at mitosis, which activates the complex at the appropriate stage.

Loop extrusion is the model that explains how these complexes fold DNA. A complex binds the chromatin and extrudes a loop by moving along the DNA and drawing the flanking regions together. The model predicts that loops grow until the complex is stopped by a barrier, and the barriers are often bound proteins such as CTCF. The idea has been supported by imaging and by simulations, although the details of the mechanism remain under study.

Watson and Crick (1953) described the double helix whose strands must be separated for replication and whose sister molecules must be held together. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the structure of the SMC complexes and their regulation. The complexes are studied by biochemistry of their ATPase activity, by imaging of loops and by mutations that disrupt the rings.
