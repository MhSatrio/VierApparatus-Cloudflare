# Advanced Cell Signaling

Signalling is the means by which a cell turns information from its environment into a change in behaviour. At the introductory level, a signal is a molecule that binds a receptor and triggers a cascade. At the advanced level, the questions change. Why does a pathway respond to some stimuli and not others? How does a network that uses the same kinases for many different signals keep them separate? How do cells decide between a graded and an all-or-none response, and how quickly can they switch?

The module treats these questions in the order in which a signal travels through the cell. It begins with the major receptor families, then turns to the scaffolds that organise components into complexes, the feedback loops that shape responses, and the dynamics of signals in time. The chapters on crosstalk and termination show how pathways interact and how responses end, and the final chapter introduces the quantitative models that connect these features.

Advanced signalling is the study of networks, not of single chains. Each chapter therefore refers back to the mechanisms described in Cell Biology and asks what the network does that a single pathway cannot.

Readers should be comfortable with enzyme kinetics and with the regulation of enzymes described in the Biochemistry module, because much of the quantitative reasoning depends on them.
