A cell contains many protein kinases, and many of them phosphorylate similar sites. If they acted freely in the cytoplasm, a signal from one receptor would reach every target, and the cell could not respond to different stimuli in different ways. The problem of specificity is solved in part by scaffolds, proteins that bind several components of a pathway at once and hold them in a complex.

A scaffold works by increasing the local concentration of the components. Two kinases that bind the same scaffold are held close together, so a substrate passed from one to the other does not need to diffuse far, and the rate of the cascade rises. Scaffolds also ensure that components are present in the correct order and in the correct place, so that a kinase that acts on one pathway is not reached by activators of another. Koshland (2002) described the organisation of chemistry into compartments as one of the principles of living systems, and scaffolds achieve a similar organisation within the cytoplasm.

Scaffolds can also insulate pathways. When a single kinase is shared between two pathways, a scaffold that binds it to one set of partners prevents it from acting on the other. The result is that the same enzyme can have different consequences in different cells, depending on which scaffold is present. This insulation is one of the mechanisms that allows a network with a small number of enzymes to produce many distinct outputs.

The kinetic consequences are quantitative. In a cascade without a scaffold, the signal decays as it passes from one step to the next, because each step is diluted by diffusion. A scaffold reduces the dilution and can make the response sharper. Models of scaffolded cascades predict that the effective rate increases as the components are brought closer, and that the response becomes more sensitive to the input.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the protein domains that mediate binding to scaffolds and the mechanisms that assemble complexes. Scaffold function is studied by mutations that break the binding interfaces, by measuring the kinetics of the cascade in cells and by reconstituting the complex in vitro.

Scaffolds illustrate a general principle: the specificity of a network depends on its spatial organisation as well as on the chemistry of its enzymes.
