Receptor tyrosine kinases are transmembrane proteins with an extracellular ligand-binding domain and an intracellular kinase domain. Growth factors such as epidermal growth factor and insulin bind them and drive a sequence of events that is one of the most studied in cell biology. In the inactive state, the receptors are monomers or are held in a conformation that keeps the kinase inhibited. Ligand binding brings two receptors together into a dimer, which allows each kinase domain to phosphorylate tyrosine residues on the other, a process called trans-autophosphorylation.

Autophosphorylation does two things. It activates the kinase, which is partly inhibited in the monomer, and it creates binding sites for adaptor proteins. Phosphotyrosine residues in a particular sequence context are recognised by SH2 domains, which are modular protein regions that bind phosphotyrosine. Adaptor proteins such as Grb2 contain SH2 domains and additional domains that bind other proteins. Recruitment of the adaptor brings with it a guanine nucleotide exchange factor, which activates the small GTPase Ras at the membrane.

The specificity of the response is set by the pattern of phosphorylated sites. A receptor carries many tyrosines, and each recruits a different set of adaptors. The combination determines which downstream pathways are activated, so the same receptor can drive proliferation, survival or differentiation in different cells, depending on the adaptors that cell expresses.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein's function depends on its folded structure, and the ligand-induced dimerisation of the receptors is a structural change of this kind. The affinity of each domain for its partner is itself a property that can be measured and modelled.

Receptor tyrosine kinases are central to cancer biology, because many tumours carry mutations that make the receptor active without ligand. Such mutations are a standard target of drugs, and the study of the receptor mechanism is therefore both basic and clinical.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the Ras pathway and the adaptors that link receptors to it. The mechanisms are studied by biochemical reconstitution with purified receptor domains, by measuring phosphorylation sites with mass spectrometry and by introducing mutations that change the adaptor-binding sites.
