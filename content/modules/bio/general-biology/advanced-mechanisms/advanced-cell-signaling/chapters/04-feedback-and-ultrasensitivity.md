Feedback is the return of part of a pathway's output to its input. Negative feedback reduces the response, and positive feedback amplifies it. Both change the behaviour of the pathway in ways that the simple cascade does not show. Negative feedback can stabilise a steady output against variation in the input, can speed the return to baseline after a stimulus and can produce oscillations when the delay in the loop is long. Positive feedback can create bistability, in which a pathway has two stable states, and can lock a cell into a new fate.

The steady-state relation between input and output is often described by a sigmoidal curve. The steepness of the curve near the midpoint is measured by the Hill coefficient, which appears in the equation for the fraction of a responding component as a function of the input: the response equals the input raised to the power n, divided by the sum of the input raised to n and a constant. When n is one, the response is hyperbolic and graded. When n is greater than one, the response is steeper, and the pathway behaves more like a switch.

Ultrasensitivity is the condition in which the output changes much more steeply than the input. It can arise from cooperative binding, from a cascade of steps in which each is saturated, or from a double-phosphorylation cycle in which a substrate must be modified twice. A mechanism of this kind is a substrate converted between two states by enzymes that are already saturated, a situation called zero-order ultrasensitivity, which produces steep responses. Koshland (2002) listed adaptability among the organising principles of living systems, and ultrasensitivity provides one way that a cell can respond decisively to a change.

Bistability arises when positive feedback is strong enough to produce two stable states for the same input. The cell then retains a memory of past stimuli, and the switch can be reset only by a stronger signal. Such switches are thought to underlie decisions such as the commitment of a cell to division.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the examples of feedback in cell biology, including the cell cycle and the regulation of metabolic enzymes. The behaviour of feedback systems is studied by perturbing the loop, measuring the steady state and transient responses and fitting models to the data.
