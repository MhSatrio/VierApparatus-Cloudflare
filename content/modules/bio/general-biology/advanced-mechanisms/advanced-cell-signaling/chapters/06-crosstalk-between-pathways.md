No signalling pathway operates in isolation. Components are shared, outputs converge on the same targets, and one pathway can change the activity of another. This crosstalk allows a cell to integrate information from many sources, and it also makes the behaviour of the network hard to predict from the study of single pathways. A drug that inhibits one kinase may alter the output of a second pathway through a route that was not expected.

Crosstalk takes several forms. Shared components are proteins that participate in two pathways, and their activity in one changes their availability for the other. Convergent outputs occur when two pathways act on the same target, such as a transcription factor, so that the response depends on the combined input. Feedback between pathways occurs when the output of one pathway regulates a component of the other, which can make the network respond in ways that neither pathway would show alone.

The consequences can be counterintuitive. Blocking a pathway can strengthen another that normally competes with it, because the inhibited pathway no longer draws on the shared resource. Such effects have been observed in cancer, where inhibition of one signalling route can activate a compensatory route, and the tumour adapts.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the function of a protein depends on its folded structure, and crosstalk often depends on the specific structural features that allow a protein to bind several partners. Changes in the structure, through mutation or modification, can alter which partners it binds and so change the network.

Understanding crosstalk requires models that include the network as a whole. Experiments that measure only one pathway at a time can miss the compensatory responses, and the design of an experiment must include the measurements of the relevant alternatives.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the major signalling networks and the points at which they connect. Crosstalk is studied by measuring several pathways at once in the same cells, by perturbing one pathway and observing the others, and by computational models that represent the connections.
