G protein-coupled receptors form the largest family of cell-surface receptors in humans. Each has seven transmembrane helices, and they respond to hormones, neurotransmitters, odorants, light and many other signals. The helices form a binding pocket on the extracellular side, and ligand binding alters the arrangement of the helices on the intracellular side, where the receptor contacts a heterotrimeric G protein. The G protein consists of an alpha subunit that binds guanosine nucleotides and a beta-gamma dimer.

In the inactive state, the alpha subunit carries bound GDP. Receptor activation causes it to exchange GDP for GTP, and the GTP-bound alpha subunit dissociates from the beta-gamma dimer. Both parts can then regulate effectors. Alpha subunits of different families stimulate or inhibit enzymes such as adenylyl cyclase and phospholipase C, while beta-gamma dimers can open ion channels or activate other enzymes. The same receptor can therefore signal through several effectors.

The signal is amplified because one activated receptor can activate many G proteins in turn, and each G protein can generate many molecules of a second messenger such as cyclic AMP. The amplification is limited by the rate at which the signal is terminated. The alpha subunit hydrolyses its GTP slowly by itself, and regulators of G protein signalling accelerate this hydrolysis, which returns the subunit to its inactive state and allows it to reassemble with the beta-gamma dimer.

Desensitisation reduces the response to a sustained stimulus. Receptor kinases phosphorylate the activated receptor, and arrestin proteins bind the phosphorylated form, blocking G protein coupling and targeting the receptor for internalisation. The receptor may later return to the surface, so the response can recover after the stimulus is removed.

Anfinsen (1973) established the principle that the structure of a protein determines its function, and the seven-helix bundle of these receptors is a structure whose conformational changes carry the information of the ligand. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the second messengers and the kinases they activate. G protein signalling is studied by reconstitution of receptors and G proteins in membranes, by fluorescence sensors of conformational change and by genetic deletion of individual subunits.
