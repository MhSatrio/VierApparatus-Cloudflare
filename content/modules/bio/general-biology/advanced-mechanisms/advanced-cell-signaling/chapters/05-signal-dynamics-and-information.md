Early models of signalling treated the level of a signal as its message. A higher concentration of a hormone or growth factor was taken to produce a stronger response. Later work showed that the dynamics of a signal can also carry information. A transcription factor that enters and leaves the nucleus in pulses, rather than remaining there steadily, can activate different genes depending on the frequency of the pulses. The same pathway can thus encode several messages in its temporal pattern.

Pulse frequency and duration can change the outcome. A short pulse may be insufficient to switch on a gene that requires sustained activity, while a series of pulses may accumulate enough activity to do so. The cell's decoding machinery, which includes the promoters and the kinetics of the transcription factors, determines which pattern produces which response. A given signal therefore does not have a fixed meaning; its meaning depends on the cell that reads it.

Oscillations are a common form of signal dynamics. Negative feedback with a delay, as discussed in the chapter on feedback, can generate sustained cycles in the concentration of a component. The period of the oscillation is set by the delay and the strength of the loop. Oscillations can synchronise cells, can limit the time that a component is active, and can allow a single pathway to drive several processes at different phases of the cycle.

Koshland (2002) described responsiveness to changing conditions as a feature of life, and the dynamics of signals are one means of achieving it. A cell that tracks changes in its environment over time needs to respond to the rate of change as well as to the level, and temporal coding gives it that capacity.

Information theory provides a way to measure how much a cell learns about its environment from a signal. The mutual information between the input and the output quantifies the number of distinguishable states that the response can convey. Measuring this quantity requires the response of many cells to a range of inputs, and the results show how well a pathway transmits information.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the nuclear transport of transcription factors and the feedback loops that generate pulses. Signal dynamics are studied with fluorescent reporters that report activity in individual living cells over time, which reveal the variation between cells.
