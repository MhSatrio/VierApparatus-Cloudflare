Eukaryotic DNA is wrapped around histone octamers to form nucleosomes, about 147 base pairs per nucleosome. The nucleosome is a barrier to transcription factors and to the polymerase, because the DNA is bound tightly to the histone surface. Access to a gene therefore depends on whether its nucleosomes are displaced, repositioned or modified. Chromatin is not uniformly inaccessible: active genes tend to lie in open regions with few nucleosomes, and inactive genes are often packed into compact structures.

Watson and Crick (1953) described the double helix, and the nucleosome adds a further level of structure in which the helix is wound around a protein core. The wrapping is strong enough to limit access, yet it can be overcome by factors that bind the DNA, by the polymerase and by remodelling enzymes. The balance between compaction and access is a central feature of gene regulation.

Chromatin remodelling complexes use the energy of ATP hydrolysis to slide, eject or restructure nucleosomes. They are recruited by transcription factors and by modified histones, and their action can open a promoter so that the transcription machinery can bind. Remodelling is reversible, and the nucleosomes can be restored after transcription.

Histone modifications provide another level of control. Acetylation of lysine residues neutralises their positive charge and weakens the interaction between histone and DNA, while methylation can either activate or repress transcription depending on the residue modified. Jenuwein and Allis (2001) proposed that these modifications form a code that is read by other proteins, which then recruit additional regulators. The code is not a simple set of rules, but the correlation between particular modifications and gene activity is well established.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the structure of the nucleosome and the enzymes that modify histones. Chromatin accessibility is measured by nuclease digestion, which reveals open regions, by sequencing of the DNA in those regions and by mapping the histone modifications genome-wide.
