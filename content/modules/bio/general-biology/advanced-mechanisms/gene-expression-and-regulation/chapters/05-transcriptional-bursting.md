Measurements of gene expression in individual cells show that transcription is not continuous. A promoter switches between an active state, in which RNA polymerase initiates transcription, and an inactive state, in which it does not. During an active period, several RNA molecules are produced in a burst. The bursts are separated by quiet periods, and the pattern of bursts is the main source of the variation between cells that carry the same gene.

The two-state model describes the promoter as switching on at a rate k_on and off at a rate k_off. The fraction of time the promoter is active is k_on divided by the sum of k_on and k_off. During each active period, the rate of initiation is k_m, and the number of RNA molecules made per burst is the initiation rate multiplied by the mean duration of the active period. The mean expression level is therefore the burst frequency multiplied by the burst size, and the variance depends on both quantities in a way that distinguishes the two mechanisms.

Jacob and Monod (1961) described gene regulation as a switch between states, and the bursting model applies this idea at the level of individual promoters. The distinction between frequency and size is important, because a regulator may change one without changing the other. Changing the frequency alters how often a cell has a burst, while changing the size alters how much RNA each burst produces. The two effects can be distinguished by measuring the distribution of RNA numbers across cells.

Bursting is common in eukaryotes and is thought to be a feature of promoter structure and of the factors that bind it. Promoters with a TATA box and strong enhancers often show larger bursts, while housekeeping genes may burst less. The pattern depends on the gene and on the cell type.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the steps of transcription initiation, which set the timing of bursts. The bursting behaviour is measured by single-molecule RNA fluorescence in situ hybridisation, which counts the molecules in each cell, and by live imaging of reporter genes.
