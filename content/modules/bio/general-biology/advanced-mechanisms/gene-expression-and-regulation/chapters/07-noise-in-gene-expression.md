Genetically identical cells in the same environment do not produce identical amounts of a given protein. The variation, called noise, arises from several sources. Intrinsic noise comes from the random timing of the reactions that make and destroy RNA and protein within a single gene, such as the bursts described in the previous chapter. Extrinsic noise comes from differences between cells in the factors that regulate the gene, such as the concentration of a transcription factor, the size of the cell or the stage of the cell cycle.

Noise is quantified by the coefficient of variation, the standard deviation of expression divided by its mean. A coefficient of variation near one means the spread across cells is as large as the mean, which is typical of genes with bursty expression. The coefficient falls as the mean rises, and this relation is one of the signatures of the sources of noise. Intrinsic noise tends to be large when the number of molecules is small, and it decreases with the square root of the number.

Noise can be separated into intrinsic and extrinsic components by using two reporter genes in the same cell. Intrinsic fluctuations are uncorrelated between the two reporters, because each responds to its own random events. Extrinsic fluctuations are shared, because both respond to the same cellular factors. The comparison of their correlation gives a way to estimate the contribution of each source.

Whether cells benefit from noise or must suppress it is debated. Noise can allow a population to hedge its bets, so that some cells respond to a sudden change that others do not anticipate. It can also produce differences between cells that determine their fate. On the other hand, noise can make a response unreliable, and networks with negative feedback are often shown to reduce it.

Ioannidis (2005) described how biological variability can produce results that do not replicate, and the same variability means that single-cell measurements require many cells to estimate a reliable mean. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular events that generate fluctuations. Noise is studied with single-cell measurements, with dual reporter systems and with models that predict the variance from the rates of reactions.
