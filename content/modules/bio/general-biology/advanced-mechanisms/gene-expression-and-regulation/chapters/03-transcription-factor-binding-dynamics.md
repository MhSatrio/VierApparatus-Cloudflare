A transcription factor bound to its site is not fixed in place. It binds and dissociates continually, and the proportion of time that a site is occupied depends on the concentration of the factor and on the strength of the interaction. The relation is a binding equilibrium. If the factor concentration is C and the dissociation constant of the site is Kd, the fraction of sites occupied at equilibrium is C divided by the sum of C and Kd. This simple expression governs how the occupancy of each site responds to changes in the factor.

Jacob and Monod (1961) described repressors that bind operators and release them when an inducer is present. The occupancy of the operator determines whether transcription is blocked, and the dynamics of binding and release set how quickly the system responds to a change in the inducer. The same principle applies to eukaryotic factors, though the equilibria are more complex.

The residence time, the average duration of a single binding event, is a separate quantity from occupancy. Two factors may occupy a site for the same fraction of time, yet one may bind and release quickly while the other stays for long periods. Residence time influences whether a factor can recruit the machinery that starts transcription, since the machinery may need the factor to remain for a minimum time. Measurements in living cells show that many factors have residence times of seconds, which is short compared with the time that transcription takes to build up.

Concentration sets the response. A factor whose concentration changes in a cell modifies the occupancy of all its sites, and sites with different affinities respond at different concentrations. The sites therefore act as thresholds, and the set of genes activated depends on the level of the factor. This is one way that a single regulator can control many genes with different sensitivities.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the principles of protein-DNA recognition and the factors that determine binding. Binding dynamics are measured by single-molecule imaging in living cells, by fluorescence recovery after photobleaching and by biochemical measurements of dissociation constants.
