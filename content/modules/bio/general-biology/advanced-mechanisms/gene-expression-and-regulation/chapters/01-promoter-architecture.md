A promoter is the region of DNA that directs the start of transcription. It contains a core promoter, a stretch of about a hundred base pairs around the transcription start site, and proximal elements upstream of it. The core promoter recruits general transcription factors and RNA polymerase II, and it includes motifs such as the TATA box in some genes, the initiator and the downstream promoter element in others. Many human genes lack a TATA box, and their core promoters are defined by other motifs and by the local chromatin.

Jacob and Monod (1961) described the operator and promoter of the lac system as sequences that a repressor could recognise, and their model established the principle that the sequence of a regulatory region determines which proteins bind it. The principle applies to eukaryotic promoters with far greater complexity, since each promoter can bind many transcription factors at once.

Transcription factors bind short sequence motifs, typically six to twelve base pairs long. The affinity of a factor for a site depends on how closely the site matches the factor's preferred sequence, and most motifs are imperfect matches. A promoter therefore contains a set of sites with a range of affinities, and the set of factors bound at a given time depends on their concentrations and on how many sites each can occupy.

The arrangement of sites matters. Two factors bound next to each other may interact and activate transcription synergistically, while the same factors bound far apart may not. The spacing and orientation of sites can be important, and the grammar of a promoter is partly a consequence of the physical interactions between the factors bound to it. Watson and Crick (1953) showed that the DNA double helix has a defined geometry, and the spacing of protein binding sites relative to the helix turns is one of the constraints on how factors interact.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the general transcription factors and the assembly of the preinitiation complex. Promoter architecture is studied by mutating individual sites and measuring expression, by sequencing the regions bound by factors and by computational analysis of sequences across the genome.
