The first crystal structures of proteins, including that of myoglobin by Kendrew and colleagues (1960), showed a single static arrangement of atoms. Crystallography averages over many molecules and over time, and it encouraged the view that a folded protein is rigid. Later work showed that folded proteins move continually, and the motions span a wide range of timescales that are essential to their function.

The fastest motions are bond vibrations and side-chain rotations, which occur on femtosecond to picosecond timescales. Loops and side chains move on nanosecond to microsecond timescales, and they can open and close pockets that bind ligands. Whole domains can move relative to one another on microsecond to millisecond or longer timescales, and some of the largest motions are involved in catalysis, transport and the change between active and inactive states.

Dill and MacCallum (2012) emphasised that the conformations of a protein are best described as an ensemble, a population of structures weighted by their free energies. The native state is the most populated, but other conformations are present at lower frequency, and some of them may be functionally important. The function of a protein can depend on the rate at which it moves between the states of the ensemble.

Measurement uses several techniques. Nuclear magnetic resonance relaxation reports the motion of individual atoms over many timescales. Hydrogen exchange reports the opening of structure. Fluorescence and single-molecule methods track the distances between parts of a protein, and molecular dynamics simulations give atomic detail of the motions, although the results depend on the force field and the length of the simulation.

Dynamics link structure to function. A protein that binds a ligand often changes shape in the process, and a ligand that binds to one population of conformations may shift the equilibrium toward them. The concept of conformational selection describes this shift, and it provides a framework for understanding how a protein can respond to a signal without changing its fold.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the classes of motion and their roles in enzymes and in the cytoskeleton. Protein dynamics are studied by combining the methods above with the structural models of earlier chapters.
