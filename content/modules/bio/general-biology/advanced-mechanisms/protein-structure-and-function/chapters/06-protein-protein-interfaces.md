Most proteins act through interactions with other proteins, and the interface between them determines the specificity and strength of the interaction. Protein-protein interfaces are typically larger and flatter than the pockets that bind small molecules, often burying between eight hundred and two thousand square ångströms of surface. They are built from hydrophobic patches, charged pairs and hydrogen bonds, and their composition differs from the rest of the protein surface.

Hot spots are residues that contribute disproportionately to the binding energy. Alanine scanning mutagenesis, in which residues are changed one at a time to alanine, shows that a small number of side chains often account for most of the affinity. The hot spots are typically large hydrophobic residues such as tryptophan, tyrosine and arginine, and they are surrounded by less important residues that help to shield them from water.

The forces that hold complexes together include the hydrophobic effect described in the earlier modules, electrostatic attraction between charged residues and van der Waals packing. Anfinsen (1973) established that the folded structure determines function, and the interface must be shaped so that the two surfaces complement each other, as the active site of an enzyme is shaped to its substrate.

Interfaces can be targeted. Small molecules and peptides that bind a hot spot can block an interaction, and the approach has been used in drug design. The difficulty is that interfaces are large and shallow, which makes it hard for a small molecule to bind with high affinity and specificity. Success has come from identifying the pockets within the interface that can accommodate a small molecule.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the reliability of findings depends on the rigour of their analysis, and interaction studies are subject to false positives, since many interactions detected in screens do not hold up when tested directly. Interactions must be confirmed with independent methods and with mutants that disrupt the interface.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the types of protein complex and the way they assemble. Interfaces are studied by crystal and cryo-electron microscopy structures of complexes, by alanine scanning, by surface plasmon resonance and other binding measurements and by computational analysis of surface properties.
