The idea that a protein must fold to function was challenged when it became clear that many proteins, or regions of them, have no stable structure under physiological conditions. Such intrinsically disordered regions exist as ensembles of rapidly interconverting conformations. Anfinsen (1973) showed that a sequence determines a unique native structure for ordered proteins, and disordered proteins are the exception: their sequences do not favour a single fold, and their conformational ensembles are broad.

The sequence features are distinctive. Disordered regions are enriched in charged residues, in small polar residues such as serine, glycine and proline, and depleted in the large hydrophobic residues that drive the formation of a core. The balance of charge and the low hydrophobicity prevent the chain from collapsing into a compact structure. Proline and glycine further restrict the formation of regular secondary structure.

Dill and MacCallum (2012) framed folding as a landscape with a funnel leading to a native minimum. Disordered proteins lie in a landscape with no deep minimum, so the population is spread over many conformations, and the free energy differences between them are small. This flatness is the basis of their flexibility and of their ability to respond to many partners.

Disordered regions often fold upon binding. A region that is disordered alone may adopt a helix or a strand when it contacts a partner, and the folded form is stabilised by the interaction. This coupled folding and binding allows a single disordered segment to interact with several partners in different conformations, which is useful for signalling and regulation.

Disordered proteins are important in cell signalling and in the organisation of the nucleus, and they are often involved in disease when they aggregate. The chapters on signalling described many interactions in which a flexible segment mediates a response, and these are often disordered regions.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the functions of regulatory proteins whose activity depends on flexibility. Disordered proteins are studied by nuclear magnetic resonance, which reveals the lack of structure, by small-angle scattering and by single-molecule fluorescence methods. Their predicted disorder is assessed computationally from sequence, and the predictions are checked against experiment.
