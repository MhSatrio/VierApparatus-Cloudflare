Proteins are built from a limited repertoire of secondary structure elements: alpha helices, beta strands and loops. These elements combine into recurring arrangements called motifs, and motifs combine into folds, which are the overall architectures of domains. Kendrew and colleagues (1960) determined the first complete protein structure, myoglobin, which is dominated by a bundle of alpha helices, and the helical arrangement of myoglobin is one of the simplest examples of a fold.

A motif is a compact combination of elements with a defined geometry. The helix-turn-helix motif, for example, consists of two helices joined by a short turn, and it is found in many DNA-binding proteins, where one helix fits into the major groove of DNA. The motif recognises DNA through the side chains on the exposed face of the recognition helix, which is an example of how a structural motif is adapted to a function.

The Rossmann fold is a classic alpha-beta arrangement in which a central sheet of parallel strands is flanked by helices on both sides. It binds nucleotide cofactors, such as the dinucleotide used in many oxidation-reduction reactions, and it occurs in a large number of enzymes with unrelated sequences. The recurrence of the same fold for the same function is a striking feature of protein evolution.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the sequence determines the structure, but the relationship is not one-to-one in practice. Proteins with very different sequences can adopt similar folds, because the fold is determined by the pattern of hydrophobic and polar residues and by the constraints of packing rather than by the identity of individual residues. Motifs are therefore recognised more reliably by their geometry than by their sequence.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the common folds and their functions. Motifs and folds are classified in structural databases, and their distribution across proteins is analysed to reconstruct the history of families.
