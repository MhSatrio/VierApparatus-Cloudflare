An enzyme accelerates a reaction by providing a path with a lower activation energy than the uncatalysed reaction. The central idea is transition state stabilisation: the enzyme binds the transition state more tightly than the substrate or the product, which lowers the energy barrier. Kendrew and colleagues (1960) showed that the pocket of myoglobin is shaped to hold a specific ligand, and enzyme active sites are shaped to hold the transition state of their reaction.

Enzymes use several chemical strategies. Acid-base catalysis uses the side chains of residues such as histidine, aspartate and glutamate to donate or accept protons, which can make a substrate more reactive. Covalent catalysis forms a transient bond between the enzyme and the substrate, creating an intermediate that reacts more easily. Metal ion catalysis uses a metal ion to stabilise a negative charge or to activate a substrate, and some enzymes use cofactors such as flavins or pyridoxal phosphate to carry out chemistry that the amino acids cannot.

Electrostatic stabilisation is important. Charged residues in the active site stabilise a charged transition state, and the pocket is often arranged to exclude water, which would otherwise screen these interactions. Proximity and orientation also contribute, because binding brings the reactants together in the correct geometry, which lowers the entropic cost of the reaction.

The rate enhancements of enzymes are very large, often many orders of magnitude over the uncatalysed reaction. The enhancement is achieved not by making the enzyme react with unusual force, but by reorganising the energetics of the reaction so that the transition state is more accessible.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the reaction mechanisms of enzymes in metabolism, and the structures that carry them out. Enzyme mechanisms are studied by measuring the kinetics of mutant enzymes, by solving structures of enzymes bound to substrate analogues and by isotope effects, which reveal which bonds break in the rate-limiting step.
