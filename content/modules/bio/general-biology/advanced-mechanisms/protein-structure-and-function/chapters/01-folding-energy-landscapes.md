Anfinsen (1973) showed that a denatured protein can refold to its native structure when the conditions are restored. The result implies that the native state is the most stable state under those conditions, and that the process of folding is a movement toward a minimum of free energy. Dill and MacCallum (2012) reviewed the progress of the protein-folding problem since then, and their analysis frames folding as a search over a landscape of conformations, each with a free energy.

The landscape is rugged. Many local minima correspond to partly folded or misfolded states, and the chain must move between them to reach the native minimum. The landscape of a typical protein is often described as a funnel. The conformations at the top of the funnel are broad and unstructured, and as the chain folds it descends toward narrower regions of lower free energy and fewer conformations, ending in the native state.

The Levinthal paradox points out that a chain trying each conformation at random would take longer than the age of the universe to find the native state. Real proteins fold in microseconds to seconds, which shows that folding does not proceed by a random search. The funnel resolves the paradox, because the landscape guides the chain toward the native state, so that the number of conformations explored is far smaller than the total.

Intermediates are a feature of the funnel. Partly folded states form early, and they can be detected by the change in fluorescence or by the protection of backbone amides from exchange with the solvent. The formation of such intermediates shows that folding proceeds through a sequence of structural events rather than one step.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the forces that stabilise the native structure. Folding is studied by measuring the rates of folding and unfolding, by hydrogen exchange and by single-molecule force spectroscopy, which pulls a protein apart and measures the forces needed to unfold each part.
