Allostery is the regulation of a protein's activity by the binding of a molecule at a site distant from its active site. The effector and the active site are connected through the structure, so that binding at one site changes the shape or dynamics of the other. Allosteric regulation is one of the most important mechanisms in biochemistry, because it allows the activity of an enzyme to respond to the concentration of its products and of other metabolites, as described in the Biochemistry module.

Two classical models describe allostery. In the concerted model, the protein exists in two states, relaxed and tense, and the effector binds preferentially to one of them. The binding shifts the equilibrium between the states for all subunits at once, so that the protein switches as a whole. In the sequential model, each binding event changes the conformation of the subunit it occurs on, and the change is transmitted to neighbouring subunits, so that the switch proceeds in steps. Both models explain cooperative binding, but they make different predictions about intermediate states.

Anfinsen (1973) established that the structure of a protein follows from its sequence, and allostery shows that the same structure can exist in more than one functional state. The states differ in the arrangement of parts of the chain, and the effector stabilises one arrangement. Dill and MacCallum (2012) described the protein as an ensemble of conformations, and allostery is a shift in the population of that ensemble.

The mechanism of allosteric transmission is an active topic. Changes can propagate through networks of contacts, through changes in the flexibility of loops or through shifts in the packing of helices. The pathways are often revealed by mutations that alter the coupling between the sites.

Allosteric drugs are an important class of therapeutics, because they can modulate a protein without competing with its natural substrate. The modulators may be more selective than active-site inhibitors, since allosteric sites are often less conserved across protein families.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the regulatory enzymes of metabolism and the principles of their control. Allostery is studied by measuring the binding of effectors and the activity together, by structural comparisons of the states and by mutagenesis of the connecting elements.
