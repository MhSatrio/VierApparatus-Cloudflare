Bacteria communicate with one another by releasing small molecules, called autoinducers, into their environment. When the population is dense, the concentration of the autoinducer rises above a threshold, and the cells respond by changing their gene expression. The process, called quorum sensing, allows a population to act collectively, so that behaviours that are only effective in numbers are switched on together. The response is coordinated because each cell both produces and detects the signal.

The autoinducers differ among species. Gram-negative bacteria often use acyl-homoserine lactones, which diffuse across the membrane and bind an intracellular regulator, and Gram-positive bacteria often use peptides that are detected at the cell surface by a two-component system. The receptors and the response circuits are specific, so that a signal from one species is usually not recognised by another, although some species can eavesdrop on others.

Koshland (2002) described responsiveness as a principle of life, and quorum sensing is an example of a population responding to its own density. The response can be seen as a form of cooperation, since the cells invest in the collective behaviour only when it can succeed.

Quorum sensing controls many traits. In the bacterium that causes bioluminescence in marine organisms, the light is produced only when the population is dense enough to make it visible. In pathogens, quorum sensing controls the production of toxins and the formation of biofilms, which protect the population from the host immune system and from antibiotics. The control of these traits has become a target for drugs that interfere with the signals rather than killing the bacteria.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling molecules and the gene circuits they control. Quorum sensing is studied by measuring the autoinducer concentration and the response of reporter genes, by constructing mutants that cannot produce or detect the signal and by testing the effects of signal analogues.
