Cells communicate through four classical modes, distinguished by the distance over which the signal acts. In endocrine signalling, a hormone is released into the blood and acts on target cells far from its source. In paracrine signalling, a signal diffuses to nearby cells and acts on them. In autocrine signalling, a cell responds to a signal it has released itself. In juxtacrine signalling, a signal is presented on the surface of one cell and acts on the cell in contact with it. The four modes are not separate systems, and many signals act through more than one.

The range of a signal depends on how it moves and how it is removed. A hormone that is secreted into the blood can reach distant organs because the circulation carries it, and its action ends when it is cleared by the liver or kidney. A paracrine signal diffuses through the extracellular matrix and is taken up or degraded locally, so its effect falls off with distance. The concentration at a given distance depends on the rate of secretion and the rate of removal, which is a simple balance that determines the size of the responding region.

Koshland (2002) listed compartmentalisation and responsiveness among the principles of life, and the different modes of signalling are the means by which a multicellular body achieves both. The signal can be restricted to a small region or broadcast widely, depending on the need.

Juxtacrine signalling is the most local. Notch receptors, for example, are activated when they bind ligands on an adjacent cell, and the activation releases a fragment that enters the nucleus and changes gene expression. The interaction requires contact between cells and therefore sets a pattern in which neighbours of different types adopt different fates.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling molecules and receptors of each mode. The modes are identified by the distance over which the response occurs, by the sources and targets of the signal and by experiments that block the signal in one location.
