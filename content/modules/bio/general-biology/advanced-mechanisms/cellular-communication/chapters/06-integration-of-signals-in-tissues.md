A cell in a tissue is bathed in signals. It receives secreted factors from distant sources, contact signals from its neighbours, mechanical cues from its matrix and feedback from its own products. The cell must integrate these inputs into a single decision about whether to divide, differentiate, move or die. The integration is not a simple sum, because the same signal can have different effects in different contexts, and the outcome depends on the combination.

Lateral inhibition is a clear example. In a group of equivalent cells, a cell that begins to adopt a particular fate sends a signal to its neighbours that prevents them from doing the same. The Notch signalling described in the chapter on modes of communication produces this effect: the cell that expresses more ligand inhibits the receptor on its neighbours. The result is an alternating pattern, in which every other cell adopts one fate, and the pattern is robust because it is maintained by feedback.

Medzhitov and Janeway (2002) described the integration of signals in the innate immune system, where a cell detecting microbial patterns combines that information with signals from its neighbours before deciding to produce inflammatory mediators. The decision is made on the basis of several inputs together, which reduces the risk of responding to harmless stimuli.

Koshland (2002) described the adaptability of living systems, and the integration of signals in tissues is the means by which a tissue adapts its pattern to its environment. The cells respond to the immediate signal and also to the state of the surrounding population.

Robustness is a central feature of tissue patterns. The patterns persist despite variation in the cells and in the signals, because feedback corrects deviations. The mechanisms that achieve this are a subject of current study, and they combine the signalling pathways of earlier modules with the mechanical and population-level properties of the tissue.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling pathways and the feedback loops that generate patterns. Tissue integration is studied by imaging the signals and fates of individual cells, by perturbing the signals in defined regions and by computational models of cell populations.
