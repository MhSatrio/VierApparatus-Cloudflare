Cells release small membrane-enclosed particles into the extracellular space, and these vesicles carry proteins, lipids and nucleic acids that can reach other cells. The classes are defined by their origin. Exosomes are small vesicles, roughly forty to a hundred and twenty nanometres across, that form inside endosomes and are released when the endosome fuses with the plasma membrane. Microvesicles bud directly from the plasma membrane and are larger. Apoptotic bodies are released by cells that are dying and can be engulfed by neighbours.

The cargo of vesicles is selected. Proteins are sorted into exosomes by specific mechanisms, and the vesicles carry a particular set of molecules that depends on the cell that produced them. Some vesicles carry microRNAs, which may be delivered to target cells and silence their messenger RNAs, as described in the RNA Biology module. The transfer of functional cargo has been demonstrated in cultured cells, but its importance in the body is still being established.

Koshland (2002) listed the organisation of compartments among the principles of living systems, and vesicles are a means by which compartments exchange material with the outside. The vesicle is a small compartment that moves between cells.

The field has been affected by the difficulty of identifying and separating vesicles. Different preparation methods yield populations of different composition, and it is hard to be sure that a particular molecule is inside a vesicle rather than associated with it. Ioannidis (2005) described how findings in young fields with flexible methods often fail to replicate, and the claims about extracellular vesicles require standardised isolation, characterisation and controls.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the endosomal and membrane trafficking pathways that generate vesicles. Extracellular vesicles are studied by electron microscopy, by measuring the size and markers of particles and by transferring labelled vesicles into recipient cells.
