A gap junction is a channel that connects the cytoplasm of two adjacent cells, allowing small molecules and ions to pass directly between them. Each channel is formed by two hemichannels, one from each cell, and each hemichannel is a ring of six connexin subunits in vertebrates. When the hemichannels dock, they form a continuous pore about one and a half nanometres across, which excludes large molecules while permitting ions, metabolites and second messengers to move freely.

Koshland (2002) described compartmentalisation as a principle of life, and gap junctions create a controlled breach in the compartments of individual cells. The breach allows neighbouring cells to share their internal state, which is useful for coordinating a tissue as a single unit.

The clearest function is electrical coupling. In the heart, gap junctions carry the ions of the action potential from one cell to the next, so that the ventricle contracts as a unit. Mutations in connexins cause conduction defects and are associated with heart disease. In the nervous system, electrical synapses use gap junctions to transmit signals with almost no delay, which is useful for rapid escape responses.

Gap junctions also pass metabolites and second messengers, such as calcium and cyclic nucleotides, which can coordinate responses across a tissue. A calcium wave that spreads through a sheet of cells is one example, in which the signal moves by diffusion through the junctions and by release from stores in each cell.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the structure of gap junctions and the connexin families. Gap junction function is studied by dye transfer experiments, in which a fluorescent tracer is injected into one cell and its spread into neighbours is measured, and by electrophysiological recording of coupling.
