Neurons communicate at synapses. At a chemical synapse, the arrival of an action potential at the presynaptic terminal opens voltage-gated calcium channels. The calcium entry triggers the fusion of neurotransmitter-filled vesicles with the presynaptic membrane, and the transmitter is released into the cleft. It binds receptors on the postsynaptic cell, which open ion channels or activate signalling pathways. Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) described the ionic currents that generate the impulse, and the same channel types underlie the events of the terminal.

Release is fast and is controlled precisely. The vesicles are docked at specialised release sites, and a small number of them fuse in response to each impulse. The fusion is driven by the SNARE proteins, which bring the vesicle and membrane together, and by synaptotagmin, a calcium sensor that triggers the fusion within a fraction of a millisecond. The vesicles are then retrieved and refilled, so that the synapse can release again.

Electrical synapses use gap junctions, as described in the chapter on gap junctions, and they transmit signals with little delay. Chemical synapses are more common in the mammalian brain, and they allow the strength of the signal to be modified.

Synaptic strength changes with activity. Long-term potentiation increases the response of a synapse after it has been stimulated at high frequency, and long-term depression decreases it after low-frequency stimulation. The changes depend on the receptors present, on the number of vesicles released and on structural changes in the postsynaptic spine. These plastic changes are thought to underlie learning and memory, although the link is still being studied.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular machinery of release and the receptors that respond. Synaptic function is studied by recording the currents at single synapses, by imaging the release of labelled vesicles and by genetic manipulation of the release machinery.
