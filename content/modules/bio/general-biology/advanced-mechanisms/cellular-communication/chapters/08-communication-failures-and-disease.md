Communication is essential for the health of a tissue, and its failures are a common cause of disease. In cancer, cells lose the signals that normally restrain their division, such as contact inhibition and the requirement for growth factors. A mutation that keeps a signalling pathway permanently active can drive proliferation without the signal, and the loss of the signals that maintain tissue architecture allows cells to invade. The communication defect is therefore a central feature of tumour biology.

Autoimmune disease arises when the signals that control immune responses fail to prevent attack on the body's own tissues. The failure may involve the signals that remove self-reactive lymphocytes during development, the signals that suppress responses once an infection is cleared or the cytokine balance that determines whether inflammation is controlled. Medzhitov and Janeway (2002) described the innate recognition of danger, and the inappropriate activation of innate cells by self-molecules can initiate such diseases.

Neurological disorders involve failures of synaptic communication. Changes in neurotransmitter release, receptor function or the structure of synapses are associated with many conditions, and the links between specific defects and symptoms are still being worked out.

The spread of infection also depends on communication. Pathogens use signals to coordinate their behaviour, and they interfere with the signals of the host, for example by blocking cytokine receptors. The microbes that have evolved to disrupt communication are a rich source of insight into the normal signalling system.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the reliability of the claims about communication defects depends on the rigour of the studies that establish them. Treatments that target communication, such as inhibitors of signalling pathways or antibodies against cytokines, must be tested in controlled trials.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the mechanisms by which communication is normally controlled. Failures are studied in patient samples, in cell and animal models and by genetic analysis of the affected pathways.
