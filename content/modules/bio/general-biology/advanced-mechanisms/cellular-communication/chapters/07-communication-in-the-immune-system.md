The immune system is a communication network. Its cells exchange signals through cytokines, which are small secreted proteins, chemokines, which guide cell movement, and surface molecules that provide contact signals. Medzhitov and Janeway (2002) described how innate cells detect microbes and produce these signals, and the adaptive response depends on the signals that follow. The pattern of cytokines produced determines which kind of effector response develops.

Cytokines are grouped into families with overlapping and opposing functions. Some promote inflammation and the activation of macrophages, others promote the differentiation of helper T cells into subsets with specific roles, and others suppress the response. The balance between these signals sets the strength and quality of the response. A combination that favours one subset of T cells will mobilise a different set of effectors from a combination that favours another.

Co-stimulatory molecules are needed for lymphocyte activation. A T cell that recognises its antigen requires a second signal from the antigen-presenting cell, which is delivered through surface molecules, to be fully activated. Without it, the T cell may become unresponsive, a state called anergy. The requirement is an important safeguard against responses to harmless material.

Chemokines guide cells to the sites where they are needed. A gradient of chemokine concentration directs migrating leukocytes, and the receptors on the cells determine which chemokines they follow. The pattern of chemokine production in a tissue therefore determines which cells are recruited.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling receptors of immune cells and the pathways they activate. Immune communication is studied by measuring cytokine profiles in tissues and blood, by blocking individual signals and by following the migration of labelled cells in vivo.
