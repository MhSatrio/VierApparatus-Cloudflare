Most hormones are controlled by axes. The hypothalamus releases a releasing hormone into the blood vessels of the pituitary, which releases a trophic hormone into the general circulation, which stimulates a target gland to release its own hormone. The product of the target gland then feeds back on the hypothalamus and the pituitary to adjust the first steps of the cascade. The arrangement is a control system with several stages, and the stages allow the body to amplify and regulate the signal at each level.

Koshland (2002) listed adaptability among the principles of living systems, and the axis is a means of adapting the output of a gland to the needs of the body. Each feedback level can respond to a different aspect of the state of the organism, so that the system can respond to both rapid and slow changes.

The release of many hormones is pulsatile. Gonadotropin-releasing hormone, for example, is released in brief pulses, and the frequency of the pulses determines which pituitary hormones are produced. Fast pulses favour the release of one hormone and slow pulses favour another. The temporal pattern therefore carries information, similar to the pulse coding described for signalling in the Advanced Cell Signaling module.

Feedback can be negative, which is the usual case, or positive, as occurs at the midcycle surge of gonadotropins that triggers ovulation. The sensitivity of each axis depends on the number of receptors and on the rate at which the hormone is cleared, and changes in these parameters alter the set point.

Ioannidis (2005) described how measurements of hormones are subject to large variation, and the diagnosis of endocrine disease requires repeated measurements under standard conditions, since a single value can mislead.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling of the hypothalamus and pituitary. Endocrine axes are studied by measuring hormone levels over time, by stimulation and suppression tests and by genetic models of each component.
