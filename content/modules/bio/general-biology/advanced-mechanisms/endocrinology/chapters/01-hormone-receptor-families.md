Hormones act through receptors, and the receptors fall into two broad families. Nuclear receptors are intracellular proteins that bind lipophilic ligands, including steroid hormones, thyroid hormones and vitamin D. Cell-surface receptors bind hydrophilic ligands, including peptide hormones, catecholamines and many growth factors, and they transmit the signal across the membrane. The distinction reflects the chemistry of the ligand: lipophilic hormones can cross the membrane, while hydrophilic ones cannot.

Nuclear receptors share a modular structure. A DNA-binding domain recognises specific sequences called hormone response elements, and a ligand-binding domain changes shape when the hormone binds. The binding alters the recruitment of coactivators or corepressors, which changes the transcription of target genes. Anfinsen (1973) established that a protein's conformation determines its function, and the ligand-induced change of shape in these receptors is an example of the principle.

The transcriptional response is slow, taking hours, because it requires the synthesis of new proteins. The response is also long lasting, because the new proteins persist. Steroid hormones therefore produce sustained changes in cell behaviour, while peptide hormones act through faster signals.

Cell-surface receptors include receptor tyrosine kinases, which are described in the Advanced Cell Signaling module, and G protein-coupled receptors, which are also described there. A peptide hormone binds its receptor on the surface, and the receptor activates an intracellular cascade that can produce a response within seconds.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the receptor families and the mechanisms of their action. Receptors are studied by binding assays, by structural determination of ligand-binding domains and by genetic deletion in animal models.
