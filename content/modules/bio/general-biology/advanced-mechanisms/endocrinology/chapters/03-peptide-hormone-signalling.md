Peptide hormones are water-soluble and bind receptors on the cell surface. The insulin receptor is a receptor tyrosine kinase, as described in the Advanced Cell Signaling module, and its autophosphorylation recruits the adaptors that activate the Ras and PI3K pathways. The PI3K pathway, through AKT, promotes the uptake of glucose by moving glucose transporters to the membrane and stimulates the synthesis of glycogen and fat. Insulin therefore lowers blood glucose by acting on several tissues at once.

Glucagon acts through a G protein-coupled receptor, which activates adenylyl cyclase and raises the concentration of cyclic AMP in the liver. Cyclic AMP activates protein kinase A, which phosphorylates enzymes that break down glycogen and inhibit those that make it, releasing glucose into the blood. The effects of glucagon and insulin oppose each other, and the balance between them determines whether the body stores or releases fuel.

Koshland (2002) described the regulation of living systems through responsiveness, and the opposition of insulin and glucagon is a clear case of a system regulated by two signals acting in opposite directions. The balance of the signals keeps glucose within narrow limits.

The responses are rapid. Changes in glucose transport and in enzyme phosphorylation occur within minutes, while changes in gene expression take longer. Hormones therefore produce a two-stage response, an immediate change in metabolism followed by a slower adjustment in the amount of the enzymes involved.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the second messenger systems and the kinases that transduce peptide hormone signals. Peptide hormone action is studied by receptor binding assays, by measuring second messengers and phosphorylation in target cells and by mice lacking receptors or signalling components.
