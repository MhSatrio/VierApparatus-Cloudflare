Endocrine disruptors are chemicals that interfere with the synthesis, transport, binding or action of hormones. Some mimic hormones and bind their receptors, others block the receptors, and others alter the enzymes that make or break down hormones. Because receptors bind with a specific shape, as Anfinsen (1973) showed for protein structure in general, a chemical whose shape resembles that of a steroid can bind a steroid receptor, and this provides a plausible mechanism for many effects.

A plausible mechanism is not evidence of an effect in people. The evidence must show that the exposure occurs at a level high enough to act, that the chemical produces the effect in a controlled setting and that the association with disease is not explained by other factors. Ioannidis (2005) described how many associations in environmental epidemiology are weak and inconsistent, and the claims about endocrine disruption have been debated for this reason.

Low-dose effects are especially contested. Some studies have reported that very low doses produce larger effects than higher doses, which would violate the usual assumption of a monotonic relation. Such non-monotonic responses are possible in principle, but they are hard to establish, because the variation between animals and the effects of small sample sizes can produce apparent patterns. Strong evidence requires replication with adequate numbers and controls.

The regulatory assessment of such chemicals relies on a combination of mechanistic data, animal studies and human observations. The strength of the conclusion depends on how the evidence agrees across these sources, and on whether the effect is seen in more than one species and more than one laboratory.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the receptors and enzymes of hormone action that such chemicals could affect. The evaluation of exposures is an area where careful experimental design and transparent reporting matter as much as the biology.
