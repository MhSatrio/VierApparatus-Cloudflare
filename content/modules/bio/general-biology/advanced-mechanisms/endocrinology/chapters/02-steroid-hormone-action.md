Steroid hormones are lipophilic molecules derived from cholesterol. They diffuse across the plasma membrane and bind receptors in the cytoplasm, which move to the nucleus when they are occupied. The receptor binds a hormone response element in the DNA and recruits coactivator complexes that acetylate histones and remodel chromatin, as described in the Advanced Epigenetics module. Jenuwein and Allis (2001) described the histone modifications that the coactivators deposit, and the steroid response is one of the clearest examples of a hormone acting through the histone code.

The response is selective because the receptor binds different response elements in different cells, and the coregulators present in each cell determine which genes are activated. The same steroid can therefore produce different responses in different tissues. This tissue specificity is important for drugs, since a steroid analogue may be designed to act in one tissue and not in another.

The response also includes repression. Some steroid receptors recruit corepressors that remove activating marks and silence genes, so the receptor can turn genes off as well as on. The balance of coactivators and corepressors determines the net effect.

Non-genomic actions of steroids have been reported, in which effects occur within seconds or minutes and do not require transcription. These effects are mediated by membrane-associated receptors or by modulation of ion channels and kinases. The evidence for some of these actions is strong, while the identity of the receptors involved is still being established in others.

Ioannidis (2005) described the caution needed when interpreting effects that occur through multiple routes, and steroid signalling provides an example where the separation of genomic and non-genomic actions requires careful experiments.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the transcriptional mechanisms and the chromatin changes involved. Steroid action is studied by measuring receptor binding and target gene expression, by genome-wide mapping of receptor sites and by pharmacological separation of the two types of response.
