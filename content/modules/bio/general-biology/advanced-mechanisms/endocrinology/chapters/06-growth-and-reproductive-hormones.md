Growth in childhood depends on growth hormone released from the pituitary. Growth hormone acts partly directly and partly through insulin-like growth factor 1, which is produced mainly in the liver and which stimulates the proliferation and differentiation of cells in the growth plates of bone and in other tissues. The axis is regulated by feedback from insulin-like growth factor 1 and by stimulatory and inhibitory hypothalamic signals, and its disturbance produces abnormal stature.

Reproduction is controlled by the gonadotropins, follicle-stimulating hormone and luteinising hormone, released from the pituitary under the influence of pulsatile gonadotropin-releasing hormone. The gonadotropins act on the gonads to produce sex steroids and gametes, and the steroids feed back on the hypothalamus and pituitary. The feedback is negative for most of the cycle, and at the midcycle it becomes positive, which produces the surge that triggers ovulation in women.

Puberty begins when the hypothalamus increases the pulsatile release of gonadotropin-releasing hormone, a change that is controlled by mechanisms that are still being defined. The increase in the gonadotropins raises the production of sex steroids, which produce the secondary sexual characteristics. The timing of puberty is influenced by nutrition and body composition, which links the reproductive axis to metabolism.

Steroid hormones act through the mechanisms of the steroid action chapter, and their effects on chromatin described by Jenuwein and Allis (2001) explain how they change the expression of many genes. The actions of oestrogen and testosterone in target tissues depend on the receptors present and on the coregulators that cooperate with them.

Ioannidis (2005) described how claims about hormonal effects can be inflated by small studies, and hormone therapies and supplements in reproduction and growth have sometimes been promoted before adequate evidence was available.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling of the growth and reproductive axes. The axes are studied by hormone measurements, by pulsatile sampling in animals and by genetic models of the receptors and hormones.
