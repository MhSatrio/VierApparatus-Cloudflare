Hormones coordinate the metabolic network of the body by changing the activity of the enzymes described in the Metabolism module. Insulin, released in the fed state, promotes the storage of fuel: it stimulates glycolysis and the synthesis of glycogen and fat, and it suppresses the release of glucose from the liver. Glucagon, released in the fasted state, has the opposite effect, promoting the breakdown of glycogen and the synthesis of glucose.

Cortisol, released from the adrenal cortex under stress, raises blood glucose by promoting gluconeogenesis and the breakdown of protein in muscle, and it suppresses the uptake of glucose by some tissues. Adrenaline, released from the adrenal medulla during acute stress, promotes the breakdown of glycogen in muscle and liver and the release of fatty acids from fat, preparing the body for activity. The two stress hormones act through different receptors but produce a coordinated mobilisation of fuel.

Thyroid hormone raises the basal metabolic rate, as described in the Human Physiology module, by increasing the activity of the mitochondria and the expression of enzymes of respiration. The effect on energy use is one of the largest of any hormone, and the mechanism connects the endocrine system to the bioenergetics described in the Cellular Bioenergetics module.

Lane and Martin (2010) argued that the energy available to a cell constrains its complexity, and the hormonal regulation of metabolism is the means by which the body allocates its limited fuel among tissues and functions. The integration of these signals keeps the supply of fuel matched to demand.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the enzymes and transporters whose activity is changed by these hormones. The effects are measured by tracing labelled fuels, by measuring the concentrations of metabolites in blood and tissue and by hormone infusion experiments.
