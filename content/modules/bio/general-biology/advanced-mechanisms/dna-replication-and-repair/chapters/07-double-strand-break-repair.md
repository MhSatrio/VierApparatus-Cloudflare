A break in both strands of the double helix is the most dangerous form of DNA damage, because the chromosome can be separated into pieces. Ionising radiation, replication across a nick and the activity of some enzymes all produce double-strand breaks, and the cell must repair them quickly and accurately. Lindahl (1993) described the chemical sources of DNA damage, and the double-strand break is the most severe of these.

Non-homologous end joining rejoins the two ends directly. The ends are bound by the Ku protein, which holds them together, and a ligase complex joins them. The process is fast and works throughout the cell cycle, but it can introduce small deletions or insertions at the junction, because the ends may need to be trimmed before they can be joined. The method is therefore accurate in the sense that it rejoins the chromosome, but it is not accurate at the level of the sequence.

Homologous recombination uses an intact copy of the sequence as a template. The ends are resected to produce single-stranded overhangs, which invade a homologous duplex, usually the sister chromatid in S and G2 phase. The invaded strand is copied, and the repair restores the original sequence. The method is accurate, but it requires a homologous template and is largely restricted to the late part of the cell cycle.

The choice between the pathways depends on resection. Resection of the ends commits the break to homologous recombination, because the Ku protein cannot bind extended single-stranded ends. The activity of kinases and of the proteins that control resection therefore determines which pathway is used. Cells in G1 favour end joining, while cells in S and G2 favour recombination.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the proteins that recognise and process the break. Double-strand break repair is studied by inducing breaks at defined sites, by sequencing the junctions that result and by measuring the efficiency of each pathway in cells with specific defects.
