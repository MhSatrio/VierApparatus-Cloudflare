A replication fork can stall when it encounters a lesion in the template, a tightly bound protein, a region of unusual DNA structure or a shortage of nucleotides. Lindahl (1993) showed that the DNA is chemically unstable and that damaged bases are common, so forks meet obstacles regularly. A stalled fork exposes single-stranded DNA, which is vulnerable to breakage, and the cell must act to protect it and to restart replication.

The stalled fork is stabilised by proteins that bind the exposed DNA and by a checkpoint response that halts the cell cycle. The checkpoint kinases signal that replication is incomplete, which delays mitosis and allows time for repair. The checkpoint also prevents the firing of additional origins that would otherwise compete for the limited supply of replication factors.

Restarting a fork can happen in several ways. The lesion can be bypassed by a specialised polymerase that copies damaged bases with lower accuracy, which allows the fork to continue at the cost of some error risk. The fork can also be reversed, so that the new strands anneal and form a structure that can be repaired before replication resumes. Recombination with a homologous sequence can provide a template for restart, and it is one of the sources of genome rearrangement when it is used inappropriately.

The choice of pathway is important. Bypass is fast but error-prone, while recombination is accurate but can cause large changes if the homologous template is not the correct one. The cell regulates the choice through the modifications and the proteins that act at the fork.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the pathways of damage tolerance and recombination. Stalled forks are studied by treating cells with agents that block replication and by imaging the fork structures with electron microscopy and single-molecule methods.
