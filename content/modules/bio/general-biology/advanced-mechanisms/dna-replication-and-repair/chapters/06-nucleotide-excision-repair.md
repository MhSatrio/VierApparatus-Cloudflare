Some DNA lesions are too large to be removed by a single glycosylase. Ultraviolet light induces cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers and other adducts that bend and distort the double helix. Watson and Crick (1953) described the regular helical structure of DNA, and bulky lesions disrupt that regularity. Nucleotide excision repair recognises the distortion rather than the chemistry of the damage, which allows it to remove a wide variety of lesions. Lindahl (1993) noted that some damage cannot be fixed by the simpler pathways, which makes this one necessary.

The pathway has two branches that differ in how they detect damage. The global genome branch scans the whole genome for helix distortions, and it recognises damage that lies anywhere. The transcription-coupled branch detects lesions that block a transcribing polymerase, which stalls and recruits the repair machinery to the gene being transcribed. The transcription-coupled branch is faster for the active genes, because the damage in them is found immediately.

Once a lesion is recognised, the helix is unwound around it, and two endonucleases cut the strand on either side of the damage. A short oligonucleotide of about twenty-four to thirty bases containing the lesion is released. The resulting gap is filled by DNA polymerase, using the intact strand as template, and the nick is sealed by ligase.

Defects in the pathway have clear consequences. Inherited deficiencies in some of its components cause the disease xeroderma pigmentosum, in which the skin is extremely sensitive to sunlight and develops cancers. The disease shows how a repair pathway can be essential for protecting an organism against environmental damage.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the proteins of the pathway and the signals that coordinate them. Nucleotide excision repair is studied by measuring the removal of lesions from specific genes, by reconstituting the reaction and by examining the sensitivity of cells and organisms to ultraviolet light.
