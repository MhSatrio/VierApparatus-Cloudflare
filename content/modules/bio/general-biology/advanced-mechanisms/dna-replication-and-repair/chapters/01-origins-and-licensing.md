Eukaryotic chromosomes contain many replication origins, and the cell must fire each one exactly once per cell cycle. Bell and Dutta (2002) reviewed the mechanism that achieves this. The process has two stages that are separated in time. In G1, before DNA synthesis, the origin recognition complex binds the origin, and the proteins Cdc6 and Cdt1 help load the MCM helicase onto the DNA. This assembly is called licensing, and it forms the pre-replicative complex. The origin is now licensed but inactive.

Origin firing happens in S phase, when kinases activate the helicase and recruit the other factors needed to start replication. The MCM complex is activated, the DNA is unwound and the replisome assembles on each side of the origin. Firing consumes the licence, and the origin cannot be licensed again until the cell passes through mitosis.

Re-replication is prevented by several mechanisms. The licensing factors are degraded or exported from the nucleus after firing, and they are inhibited by cyclin-dependent kinase activity during S, G2 and M. Because the licence can be assembled only when these inhibitors are low, which occurs in G1, each origin is licensed and fired once. Re-replication is dangerous because it amplifies regions of the genome and produces instability.

The timing of firing is also regulated. Some origins fire early in S phase and others late, and the difference is linked to chromatin and to the organisation of the genome into domains. Late origins are often in regions that replicate later because they are less accessible.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the proteins of the replication machinery and the cell-cycle control that governs them. Origin licensing is studied by reconstituting the pre-replicative complex with purified proteins, by mapping origins genome-wide and by measuring the timing of replication in cells.
