The two strands of the double helix are antiparallel, and every DNA polymerase synthesises in the five-prime to three-prime direction. At a replication fork, one strand is therefore copied continuously in the direction of fork movement, while the other is copied away from the fork. The second strand, called the lagging strand, must be synthesised in short segments, each produced in the opposite direction to the fork and called an Okazaki fragment after the scientist who discovered them.

Watson and Crick (1953) described the antiparallel arrangement of the strands, and the discontinuous synthesis of the lagging strand is a direct consequence of it. Each fragment begins with a short RNA primer synthesised by primase. The polymerase extends the primer by adding deoxyribonucleotides, and when it reaches the previous fragment it stops. The fragments are then processed to form a continuous strand.

Processing involves three steps. The RNA primer must be removed, and it is degraded by an enzyme with flap endonuclease activity, which cleaves the displaced strand. The resulting gap is filled by DNA polymerase, which uses the adjacent fragment as template. The final nick between the fragments is sealed by DNA ligase, which forms a phosphodiester bond and completes the strand.

Errors in this process can produce gaps or nicks that threaten the genome. Mutations that impair the removal of primers cause the accumulation of unprocessed fragments, which can lead to the instability of repeated sequences. The efficiency of the process is therefore an important determinant of genome stability.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the enzymes of the lagging strand and the timing of fragment synthesis. The process is studied by labelling nascent DNA to visualise the fragments, by reconstituting the reaction with purified enzymes and by genetic defects that alter fragment length.
