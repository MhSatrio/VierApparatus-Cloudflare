The replisome is the machine that copies the DNA. It contains the helicase that unwinds the helix, the polymerases that synthesise the new strands, the primase that makes RNA primers, the single-strand binding proteins that protect the exposed DNA, and a set of accessory factors that connect the components. Watson and Crick (1953) showed that each strand can serve as a template, and the replisome is organised so that both strands can be copied at once, despite their opposite polarity.

The helicase in eukaryotes is the CMG complex, formed from the MCM proteins and additional factors that are recruited when the origin fires, as described in the previous chapter. The helicase moves along the leading strand template in a defined direction, unwinding the helix ahead of the replication fork. The leading strand is then copied continuously by DNA polymerase epsilon. The lagging strand is copied by polymerase delta in short fragments, each initiated by a primer.

Two forks are established at each origin, moving in opposite directions. Bell and Dutta (2002) described how the two replisomes are coordinated so that they proceed at similar rates and meet at a termination site. The forks are assembled as a pair, and their progress depends on the supply of nucleotides and on the availability of factors at the fork.

The replisome must also coordinate with the chromatin. As the fork passes, the nucleosomes ahead of it are disassembled and reassembled behind it, and the new histones carry marks that help restore the chromatin state. The process of copying chromatin along with the DNA is an essential part of the replication of the epigenome.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the structure of the replisome and the proteins that connect its components. Replisome composition is studied by purification of the complex, by electron microscopy of replicating molecules and by single-molecule imaging of fork movement.
