The fidelity of DNA replication depends on three layers of control. The polymerase selects the correct base with a high but imperfect accuracy. Its proofreading activity removes many of the mismatches it makes. Mismatch repair then corrects most of the errors that escape proofreading. Watson and Crick (1953) showed that base pairing provides the basis for accurate copying, and these three layers add to the accuracy of the pairing to produce the very low error rate observed in cells.

Mismatch repair must distinguish the new strand from the template, because both carry a mismatched base after replication, and only the new strand should be corrected. In bacteria, the distinction is made by methylation of adenine in the template, which is present before the new strand is methylated. In eukaryotes, the discrimination is thought to depend on the presence of nicks in the newly synthesised strand, which are sites where the replication machinery has joined fragments.

Mismatch repair proteins recognise the mismatch, make a strand-specific cut and remove a stretch of the new strand that includes the error. The gap is then filled by polymerase and sealed by ligase. The repair works on both base-base mismatches and small insertion and deletion loops, and it corrects errors that the proofreading activity cannot reach.

The balance between replication and repair also depends on the rate of replication, because faster replication leaves less time for proofreading. The overall error rate is the product of the error rates of the three layers, which gives a very small number when they act together.

Defects in mismatch repair increase the mutation rate dramatically and cause a predisposition to some cancers, most notably the inherited form of colorectal cancer. Tumours with defective mismatch repair accumulate mutations at many sites, and they respond differently to some treatments, which is one reason their identification is clinically important.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the proteins of the mismatch repair system and the signals that direct their action. Fidelity is measured by mutation accumulation experiments, in which lines of cells or organisms are grown for many generations and their genomes are sequenced, and by reporter assays that detect specific mutations.
