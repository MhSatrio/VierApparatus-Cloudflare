Base excision repair removes small chemical modifications from single bases. Lindahl (1993) showed that the most common spontaneous reactions in DNA include the hydrolysis of the bond between a base and the sugar, which creates an abasic site, and the deamination of cytosine to uracil. These changes occur continually in every cell, at rates that would produce many mutations if they were not repaired, and base excision repair is the main pathway that corrects them.

The pathway begins when a DNA glycosylase recognises the damaged base. Each glycosylase is specific for a class of damage. Uracil-DNA glycosylase removes uracil, for example, and other glycosylases remove oxidised purines. The enzyme cleaves the bond between the base and the sugar, leaving an abasic site in the strand. Structural studies of the glycosylases showed that many of them act by flipping the damaged base out of the helix and into their active site.

The abasic site is then cut by an AP endonuclease, which removes the sugar phosphate, leaving a gap of one nucleotide. DNA polymerase beta fills the gap with the correct base, using the opposite strand as template. Finally, DNA ligase seals the nick, completing the repair. The steps are performed by a small number of proteins, and their coordination ensures that the intermediate forms are short-lived.

The pathway can produce strand breaks if the intermediates are not processed, so the cell maintains the balance of its enzymes carefully. A deficiency in one of the enzymes can lead to the accumulation of breaks, which then become a source of genome instability.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the chemistry of the glycosylases and the structure of the repair complexes. Base excision repair is studied by measuring the accumulation of damage in cells lacking specific enzymes, by reconstituting the pathway in vitro and by sequencing the mutations that arise when it fails.
