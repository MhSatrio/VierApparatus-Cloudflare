Synaptic plasticity is the change in the strength of a synapse with use. The best-studied forms are long-term potentiation, in which a synapse is strengthened after high-frequency stimulation, and long-term depression, in which it is weakened after low-frequency stimulation. Both are thought to underlie learning, and their molecular mechanisms have been studied intensively in the hippocampus.

The NMDA receptor is the key coincidence detector. It is a glutamate-gated channel that is blocked by magnesium at resting potential and opens only when glutamate is released and the postsynaptic membrane is depolarised. Its opening allows calcium to enter the postsynaptic spine. The calcium signal is the trigger for potentiation, and its amplitude and duration determine whether the synapse is strengthened or weakened.

Calcium activates kinases, including calcium-calmodulin-dependent kinase II, which phosphorylates target proteins and increases the number and conductance of AMPA receptors at the synapse. The receptors are inserted from intracellular pools, which increases the response to the next release of glutamate. The effect is long lasting because the kinase can maintain its own activity by autophosphorylation, a molecular memory mechanism.

Potentiation also involves structural change. The dendritic spine enlarges, and the actin cytoskeleton within it is reorganised, which stabilises the new receptor complement. The structural change is linked to the cytoskeletal dynamics described in the Cytoskeleton module, and its maintenance is important for the persistence of the memory.

Ioannidis (2005) pointed out that many findings in this field have been based on small studies, and the robustness of individual mechanisms has been questioned in some cases. The links between a specific molecule and a behaviour require causal tests, such as the genetic deletion of the molecule in a defined circuit.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling and cytoskeletal mechanisms involved. Plasticity is studied by electrophysiological recording of synaptic responses before and after stimulation, by imaging of spines and by genetic manipulation of individual components.
