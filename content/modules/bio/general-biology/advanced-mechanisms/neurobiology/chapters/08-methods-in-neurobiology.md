The methods of neurobiology have advanced rapidly, and each one answers a different question. Electrophysiology records the electrical activity of neurons with microelectrodes. The patch-clamp technique, which seals a glass pipette onto the membrane, allows the currents through individual channels to be recorded, and it is the direct descendant of the voltage-clamp work of Hodgkin and Huxley (1952). The method reveals the properties of channels and synapses at high resolution, but it samples only a few cells at a time.

Imaging of neural activity uses fluorescent indicators that report calcium or voltage. Calcium indicators give a signal that rises when neurons fire, and they allow the activity of hundreds of cells to be followed in living animals. Super-resolution methods, of the kind described by Betzig and colleagues (2006), reveal the organisation of synapses at the nanometre scale.

Optogenetics uses light-sensitive channels and pumps, introduced genetically into defined cells, to activate or silence them. The method allows the causal role of a population to be tested, because the cells can be stimulated or silenced at known times while the animal behaves. Chemogenetics achieves similar control with engineered receptors activated by drugs, which can act over longer periods.

Connectomics aims to map the connections among neurons. Electron microscopy of thin serial sections can trace individual synapses, and the reconstruction of complete circuits in small organisms has shown the wiring of networks in detail. The approach is laborious, but it provides the anatomical basis for models of circuit function.

Ioannidis (2005) described how results from new methods can be overinterpreted before they are validated, and the best use of these tools combines several of them with appropriate controls, such as cells that are not expressing the manipulated protein.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular and molecular principles that these methods exploit. The tools are developed in specialised laboratories and shared, and their use requires understanding of their limitations.
