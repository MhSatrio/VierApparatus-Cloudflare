The Hodgkin-Huxley model, described in the Human Physiology module, identified sodium and potassium conductances that change with voltage and time. Subsequent work showed that these conductances are carried by specific proteins, the voltage-gated channels, whose structure and gating explain the model's parameters. Voltage-gated sodium channels are large proteins with four homologous domains, each containing a voltage-sensing helix. Depolarisation moves the sensors, which opens the pore, and a separate inactivation gate closes it within milliseconds.

Voltage-gated potassium channels have four identical subunits, each contributing a voltage sensor and a part of the pore. The sensor moves outward on depolarisation, and the pore opens after a delay, which produces the characteristic delayed rectifier current. The difference in kinetics between sodium and potassium channels gives the action potential its shape: the rapid opening of sodium channels produces the upstroke, and the slower opening of potassium channels brings the membrane back to rest.

Neurons contain many channel types, and the mixture determines how each cell fires. Some channels are open at rest and help to set the resting potential, others open only after sustained depolarisation, and others are sensitive to calcium or to neurotransmitters. Differences in the density and distribution of channels produce the wide range of firing patterns seen in different neurons, from regular firing to bursting.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the field has produced many claims about channel function that depend on heterologous expression and on specific drugs, and the interpretation of such experiments requires controls for the non-specific actions of the compounds used.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the structure and gating of ion channels and their role in cell signalling. Excitability is studied by patch-clamp recording of channel currents, by structural studies of channel proteins and by genetic deletion of channel subunits.
