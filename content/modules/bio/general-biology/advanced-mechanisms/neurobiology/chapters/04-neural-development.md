The nervous system develops from a sheet of ectoderm that folds to form the neural tube. Neural progenitor cells divide in its walls, and their daughters migrate to form the layers and nuclei of the brain. The patterning of the tube along its axes uses the morphogen and regulatory gene logic described in the Developmental Biology module, and the same principles that Nüsslein-Volhard and Wieschaus (1980) revealed in the fly embryo specify the regions of the developing brain.

Neurons extend axons over long distances to reach their targets. The growth cone at the tip of the axon reads guidance cues, which are attractive or repulsive, and steers the axon along a defined path. The cues include secreted molecules, membrane-bound ligands and gradients of signals, and the receptors on the growth cone translate them into changes in the cytoskeleton.

Connections are initially overproduced and then refined. Neurons that are active together strengthen their connections, while those that are not active together lose them, a process that depends on the same plasticity mechanisms described for the adult synapse. The refinement sculpts the circuit so that each neuron receives appropriate input.

Critical periods are windows in which the circuit is especially sensitive to experience. The visual system of many mammals requires patterned input during a defined period for its normal organisation, and the loss of input during that period can produce lasting deficits. The timing reflects changes in inhibition and in the extracellular matrix that stabilise connections.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling and cytoskeletal mechanisms that control neuronal migration and axon guidance. Development is studied by tracing the lineage of neurons, by imaging growth cones in embryos and by manipulating activity during the critical period.
