Neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer, Parkinson and Huntington diseases, involve the progressive loss of specific populations of neurons. Each disease has characteristic lesions. Alzheimer disease is marked by extracellular plaques of amyloid beta and intracellular tangles of tau protein. Parkinson disease is marked by the aggregation of alpha-synuclein into Lewy bodies and by the loss of dopaminergic neurons. Huntington disease is caused by an expanded polyglutamine tract in the huntingtin protein. The common theme is the aggregation of proteins that have failed to fold correctly.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that a sequence determines a native fold, and misfolding is the failure to reach that state. Aggregates of misfolded proteins can be toxic, and the cell has chaperones and degradation systems that normally prevent them from accumulating. When these systems are overwhelmed or fail with age, the aggregates build up, and the neurons that are most dependent on them are the first to die.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is another common feature. Neurons have high energy demands and long axons that depend on the transport of mitochondria, and defects in mitochondrial function, dynamics or quality control are associated with several neurodegenerative conditions. The mitochondrial processes described by Nunnari and Suomalainen (2012) are relevant to these diseases.

The sequence of events is difficult to establish. The aggregates are often found late in the disease, and it is not clear whether they cause neuron death or are a consequence of the process or even a protective response. Ioannidis (2005) described the risk of inferring causation from correlation in such situations, and the most convincing evidence comes from genetic cases in which a mutation causes both the aggregation and the disease, and from experiments that prevent the aggregation and test for protection.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the protein quality control systems and the mechanisms of degradation. Neurodegeneration is studied in post-mortem tissue, in cell and animal models and in clinical trials of agents that target the proposed mechanisms.
