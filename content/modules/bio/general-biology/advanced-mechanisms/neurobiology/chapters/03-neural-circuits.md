Neurons act together in circuits, and the function of the nervous system depends on how they are connected. A circuit is a set of neurons linked by synapses that process information in a defined way. Some circuits relay signals, others amplify or suppress them, and others compare inputs or generate rhythms. Many circuits recur across regions and species, which suggests that a small set of motifs underlies much of neural computation.

The feedforward motif passes a signal from one population to another, and it can amplify, filter or delay the signal depending on the synapses involved. The feedback motif returns the output to the input, and it can stabilise activity or produce oscillations. Lateral inhibition, in which neighbours suppress one another, sharpens representations and is common in sensory systems. Each motif can be analysed in terms of its effect on the signals it carries.

Circuit activity arises from the properties of the neurons together with their connectivity. A network of neurons that each fire in response to their inputs can produce synchronous oscillations, travelling waves or stable patterns, depending on the strength and pattern of connections. Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) described the properties of the individual neuron, and the properties of the network emerge from the interaction of many such units with one another.

Central pattern generators are circuits that produce rhythmic output without rhythmic input. They control locomotion in many animals, and they can be studied in isolated preparations, where the neurons and their connections can be identified and manipulated individually.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the organisation of neurons and synapses. Circuits are mapped by anatomical tracing, by electron microscopy of connections and by recording the activity of many neurons at once. Manipulation of identified neurons by genetic and optical methods tests the role of each element.
