Cells crawl across surfaces through a cycle of four steps. The leading edge extends forward by protrusion, driven by the polymerisation of branched actin networks described in the earlier chapter. New adhesions form between the protrusion and the substrate, through integrin receptors that bind matrix proteins and link to the actin network. The cell body is then pulled forward by the contraction of actomyosin filaments, and the rear adhesions are released so that the cell can advance. The cycle repeats, and the rate depends on how quickly each step can be carried out.

Adhesions are dynamic structures whose assembly and disassembly are regulated. The growing edge forms small nascent adhesions, some of which mature into focal adhesions that anchor the actin network and transmit force. The maturation depends on the tension generated by myosin, and the disassembly of adhesions at the rear is needed for the cell to move forward. Betzig and colleagues (2006) demonstrated the use of fluorescent proteins to resolve the molecular structure of such assemblies, which has allowed the adhesion components to be mapped.

Cells use different modes of migration. Some move with broad, flat protrusions and strong adhesions, while others move by amoeboid crawling with weak adhesions and rapid squeezing through spaces. The mode depends on the cell type, the geometry of the environment and the proteolytic activity that clears a path through the matrix.

Migration is essential in development, in the immune response and in wound healing, and it is exploited by cancer cells that invade surrounding tissue. The control of migration is therefore an important target for understanding both normal and abnormal tissue behaviour.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the adhesion molecules and the signalling that controls the actin network during migration. Migration is studied by tracking cells on surfaces, by imaging adhesions with fluorescent markers and by perturbing the proteins of the cycle.
