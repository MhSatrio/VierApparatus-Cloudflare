Cells are mechanical structures. They are stretched, compressed and sheared by their neighbours and their environment, and they generate forces through the contraction of their cytoskeleton. The cytoskeleton behaves as a viscoelastic material, meaning that it resists deformation in a way that depends on the rate of loading: at short timescales it behaves like an elastic solid, and at long timescales it flows like a viscous liquid. The balance depends on the density of the network, the crosslinks between filaments and the activity of the motors.

Mechanical properties are measured by applying forces with micropipettes, magnetic beads or atomic force microscopes, and recording the deformation. The results depend on the method and on the scale of the measurement, so comparisons between studies require attention to the conditions. Cell stiffness varies with cell type and with the state of the cytoskeleton, and changes in stiffness are associated with disease, including cancer.

Mechanotransduction is the conversion of mechanical force into a biochemical signal. Force applied to a focal adhesion can open or expose binding sites in an adhesion protein, which recruits signalling molecules. Force can also stretch the nucleus, changing the conformation of its proteins and the access of transcription factors to chromatin. The pathways allow a cell to adjust its behaviour to the stiffness and tension of its environment.

Ioannidis (2005) described how claims based on small or flexibly analysed studies can fail to hold, and mechanical measurements are sensitive to technical choices. The reproducibility of the results depends on calibrated instruments and on controls for the effects of the measurement itself.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cytoskeleton and its regulatory proteins. Cellular mechanics is an area where physical and biological approaches meet, and the best-supported conclusions combine both.
