Microtubules are hollow polymers built from alpha and beta tubulin dimers, each of which binds GTP. The dimers add to the growing end, and the GTP bound to the beta subunit is hydrolysed some time after incorporation. A growing microtubule has a cap of GTP-tubulin at its end, and the cap keeps the filament in a stable, straight conformation. When the cap is lost, the lattice of GDP-tubulin curls outward, and the filament depolymerises rapidly. This switch from growth to shrinkage is called catastrophe.

Anfinsen (1973) established that a protein's conformation is determined by its sequence and its environment, and the GTP-bound and GDP-bound conformations of tubulin are an example of a nucleotide-dependent change of shape. The difference between the straight and curved forms of the lattice produces the stability differences that drive dynamic instability.

Microtubules also undergo rescue, in which shrinking filaments return to growth. The transition is stochastic, so that the lifetime of each filament varies, and the population of microtubules shows continual turnover. The behaviour is not a simple oscillation but a random switching between states whose rates depend on the concentrations of tubulin and of regulatory proteins.

Dynamic instability allows microtubules to explore the cell. A growing microtubule searches the space until it encounters a target, such as a kinetochore or a cortical site, where it is stabilised. The search is efficient because the filament can grow and shrink rapidly. The same behaviour is important in cell division, where microtubules must find and capture chromosomes.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the tubulin structure and the regulators of microtubule dynamics. Dynamic instability is measured by time-lapse imaging of labelled microtubules in cells and by in vitro assays with purified tubulin under controlled conditions.
