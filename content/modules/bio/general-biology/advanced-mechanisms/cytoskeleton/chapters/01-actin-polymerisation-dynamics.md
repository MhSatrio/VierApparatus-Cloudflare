An actin filament is a helical polymer of subunits, and each subunit binds ATP. The polymer grows at its two ends at different rates, the barbed end growing faster than the pointed end. Growth is possible only when the concentration of free actin subunits exceeds the critical concentration for that end. At the critical concentration the rate of addition balances the rate of loss, and above it the filament grows. Because the two ends have different critical concentrations, a filament can grow at one end and shrink at the other when the subunit concentration lies between them, a behaviour called treadmilling.

Subunits bound to ATP add to the filament more readily and dissociate more slowly than subunits bound to ADP. After incorporation, the ATP is hydrolysed to ADP and phosphate, and the phosphate is released more slowly. The result is a cap of ATP-bound subunits at the growing end and a region of ADP-bound subunits behind it. The ATP cap model describes this structure, and it explains why filaments that lose the cap depolymerise rapidly.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the sequence and environment of a protein determine its conformation, and the nucleotide-dependent change of actin subunit conformation is an example of the same principle. The binding and hydrolysis of ATP alter the shape of each subunit and therefore the packing of the filament, which changes its stability.

The treadmilling and the ATP cap are central to the movements that cells make. Growing barbed ends push against membranes, and the continual turnover of subunits allows the network to be remodelled rapidly. The cell controls the dynamics through proteins that bind filament ends, sever filaments or sequester subunits.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the actin cytoskeleton and its regulatory proteins. The dynamics are measured by fluorescence recovery after photobleaching, by total internal reflection microscopy of single filaments and by biochemical measurements of polymerisation.
