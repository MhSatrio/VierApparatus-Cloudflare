Intermediate filaments are the most mechanically robust elements of the cytoskeleton. Their subunits are rod-shaped proteins with a central alpha-helical coiled-coil domain flanked by variable head and tail regions. Two subunits pair to form a dimer, dimers associate into tetramers, and the tetramers assemble into filaments about ten nanometres in diameter. The coiled coil is the main structural element, and its geometry gives the filament its resistance to stretching.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the folding of a protein is determined by its sequence, and the coiled coil is a structure that forms readily from a repeated heptad pattern of hydrophobic residues. The pattern drives the pairing of two helices and their wrapping around each other, which is the basic unit of intermediate filament assembly.

The family of intermediate filament proteins includes keratins in epithelial cells, vimentin in mesenchymal cells, neurofilament proteins in neurons, lamins in the nucleus and glial fibrillary acidic protein in astrocytes. The tissue-specific expression of these proteins identifies the cell type, which makes them useful as markers in pathology, as discussed in the Histology module.

The filaments are highly resistant to stretching, and they can extend to several times their length before breaking. This property protects cells and tissues from mechanical damage. Mutations in keratins cause skin fragility, and mutations in lamins cause a variety of diseases affecting the heart, muscle and fat, which shows the importance of the mechanical role.

In the nucleus, lamins form a meshwork underlying the inner nuclear membrane, which gives the nucleus its shape and anchors the chromatin, as described in the chapter on chromosome territories. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the organisation of the filaments in cells and their interactions with other structures. Intermediate filaments are studied by electron microscopy, by immunolabelling with antibodies specific to each family and by mechanical tests of cells and tissues.
