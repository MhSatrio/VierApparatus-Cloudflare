A filament cannot start from a single subunit, because a dimer and a trimer are unstable and dissociate faster than they grow. The formation of a stable nucleus, a short stretch of several subunits, is therefore slow. The barrier is important, because it prevents the spontaneous assembly of filaments throughout the cytoplasm. Cells control where filaments start by using nucleators that bypass the slow step.

The formin family nucleates unbranched filaments. Formins dimerise and bind the barbed end of a growing filament, and they accelerate the addition of subunits while protecting the end from capping. They are important in the formation of cables and in the contractile ring that divides the cell. The mechanism of elongation is processive, which means the formin remains attached as the filament grows.

The Arp2/3 complex nucleates branched networks. It binds the side of an existing filament, and once activated by a nucleation-promoting factor, it creates a new filament that grows at an angle of about seventy degrees from the mother filament. The branched network pushes against the plasma membrane at the leading edge of a moving cell, and the network is the engine of lamellipodia.

Betzig and colleagues (2006) showed that fluorescent proteins can be localised with nanometre precision, and the architecture of branched networks has been revealed with such methods. The images show a dense mesh of short filaments with branch points, which matches the predictions of the nucleation mechanism.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the Arp2/3 complex and the signalling that activates it. Nucleation is studied by biochemical assays with purified proteins, by imaging of single nucleation events and by genetic inactivation of the nucleators in cells.
