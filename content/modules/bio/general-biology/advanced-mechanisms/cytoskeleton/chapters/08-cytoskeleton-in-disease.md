Many inherited diseases are caused by mutations in cytoskeletal proteins. Mutations in the actin-binding and motor proteins of muscle cause myopathies, in which the contraction of muscle is impaired or the fibres are fragile. Mutations in the neurofilament and microtubule-associated proteins of neurons cause neuropathies, in which the long axons degenerate because their transport and structure are disrupted. Mutations in the spectrin network of red blood cells make them fragile and cause haemolytic anaemia. Anfinsen (1973) showed that a single change in sequence can alter the structure of a protein, and such changes account for many of these disorders.

Cancer cells exploit cytoskeletal dynamics to move and invade. Tumour cells often show increased actin polymerisation at the leading edge, enhanced adhesion turnover and changes in the expression of intermediate filaments that alter their shape and their mechanical behaviour. The changes allow the cells to leave the primary tumour and enter the surrounding tissue, which is a critical step in metastasis.

Drugs that target the cytoskeleton have been used in cancer treatment. Taxanes stabilise microtubules and block the dynamics required for mitosis, and vinca alkaloids destabilise them. The effect on dividing cells is strong, and the side effects reflect the role of microtubules in other cells, including neurons. The development of these drugs illustrates both the value and the risk of targeting a fundamental system.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the claims about new cytoskeletal drugs can be inflated when the studies are small or selectively reported, and the evaluation of such drugs requires controlled trials with defined endpoints.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cytoskeleton and the processes that are disrupted in these diseases. The defects are studied by sequencing of patients, by the reconstitution of mutant proteins and by animal models that recapitulate the disorders.
