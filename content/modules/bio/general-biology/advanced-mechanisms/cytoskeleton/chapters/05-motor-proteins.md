Motor proteins convert the chemical energy of ATP into directed movement along cytoskeletal filaments. Kinesins and dyneins walk along microtubules, and myosins walk along actin. Each motor has a head that binds the filament and a catalytic site that hydrolyses ATP, and the head changes its binding affinity during each cycle. The coordinated changes in binding and conformation produce a step along the track, and the energy of ATP hydrolysis is used to make the step go in one direction.

Okada and colleagues (2003) studied the single-headed kinesin KIF1A and showed that it moves processively along microtubules by binding tubulin in a biased way, so that the head that is free to move is more likely to bind the next site ahead. The mechanism shows how a motor can take many steps without falling off, and it illustrates that the direction of movement depends on the structure of the motor and its interaction with the track.

Kinesin and dynein move in opposite directions along microtubules. Kinesin generally moves toward the plus end, which is typically at the cell periphery, while dynein moves toward the minus end, near the centre of the cell. The directional difference allows bidirectional transport, in which cargo is carried outward and inward as needed, and each cargo carries specific adaptors that recruit the appropriate motor.

Myosins walk along actin filaments, and the family includes motors that move cargo and motors that generate tension in muscle. The myosin of muscle is the one whose mechanism is described in the Muscle Physiology chapter of the Core module, and its stepping is coupled to the release of phosphate.

Anfinsen (1973) established that the structure of a protein determines its function, and the conformational changes of motor heads that drive movement are a clear example of this principle at work.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the motor families and the cargo they carry. Motor movement is measured by single-molecule assays in which individual motors are tracked along filaments, by structural studies of the motor in different states and by genetic perturbation in cells.
