The cancer stem cell hypothesis proposes that a tumour is organised like a normal tissue, with a small population of stem-like cells that can renew the tumour and produce its bulk of non-stem cells. If correct, these cells would be responsible for relapse, since treatments that kill the bulk of the tumour but spare the stem-like population would allow regrowth. The idea has been influential and has shaped the search for new drugs.

The evidence came from experiments in which cells sorted by surface markers were transplanted into immunodeficient mice. In some tumours, only a small fraction of cells formed new tumours, and these cells could be shown to have stem-like properties, such as the capacity to self-renew in serial transplantation. The results were striking and led to wide acceptance of the idea in some cancers.

The hypothesis is contested. The results depend on the transplantation assay, which uses an artificial environment that may select for particular cells, and on the markers used to sort cells, which may not define a stable population. In several tumours, cells that lack the markers can regenerate the tumour when the stem-like cells are removed, and the cells can switch between states. Ioannidis (2005) described how findings from fast-moving areas with selective markers can fail to hold, and this debate illustrates the point.

The question is whether the hierarchy is fixed or whether it is a state that cells can enter and leave. Lineage tracing in genetically engineered models, which follows cells in intact tumours, is the most direct way to answer the question, and its results have shown that the organisation varies between tumours.

The implications for therapy are important. If stem-like cells drive relapse, treatments must target them. If the state is plastic, then therapies that target the state rather than the cell identity may be needed. The answer determines the design of trials and the interpretation of their results.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the stem cell niche and the signals that maintain stemness. Cancer stem cells are studied by transplantation, lineage tracing and single-cell analysis of tumour composition.
