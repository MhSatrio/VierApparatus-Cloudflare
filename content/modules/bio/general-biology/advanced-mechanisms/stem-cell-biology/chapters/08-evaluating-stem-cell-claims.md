Claims about stem cells attract attention, and some are made without adequate evidence. A reader who understands the standards of the field can judge them. The first question is identity: what is the cell, and how has its identity been established? A claim that a cell is pluripotent should rest on the expression of the network genes, on the ability to form all three germ layers and on the ability to contribute to a developing embryo. Takahashi and Yamanaka (2006) set a standard of this kind, with defined markers and functional tests.

The second question is function. A cell that is claimed to repair tissue must be shown to engraft, to differentiate into the required cell type and to restore function in an animal model before it is tested in people. Claims that rely only on the appearance of cells, or on their behaviour in culture, do not establish function in the body.

The third question is safety. The risk of tumour formation, immune reaction and unintended differentiation must be assessed, and the evidence must cover the long term. Ioannidis (2005) described how early results can be inflated by small studies and selective reporting, and safety claims require large, controlled and independently verified observations.

Commercial clinics that offer unproven stem cell treatments have been documented in many countries. Their claims often lack controls, use poorly characterised cells and report outcomes without denominators. Patients may face cost and risk without benefit, and the fact that a treatment is offered does not mean it has been shown to work.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biology that such claims must be consistent with. A claim that contradicts well-established biology, such as the ability of a cell to change identity without the epigenetic steps described in the earlier chapter, deserves particular scrutiny.

The standards are straightforward in principle. A claim should specify the cell, show how its identity was established, report its function in a relevant model, provide controlled evidence of benefit and describe the risks, and it should be open to independent replication.
