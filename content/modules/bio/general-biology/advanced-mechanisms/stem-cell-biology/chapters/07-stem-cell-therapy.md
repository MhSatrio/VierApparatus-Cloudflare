The most established stem cell therapy is haematopoietic stem cell transplantation, which has been used for decades to restore the blood-forming system after chemotherapy or in certain blood diseases. The transplanted cells engraft in the marrow, where they regenerate the blood lineages. The method works because the niche of the marrow can accept new stem cells, and it is one of the clearest demonstrations that stem cells can repair tissue in patients.

Other therapies are in development. Stem cells have been proposed to replace damaged heart muscle, retinal cells, insulin-producing cells and neurons. In most cases the evidence comes from animal models and early clinical studies, and the benefits are often modest or uncertain. The cells must survive transplantation, integrate into the tissue, function appropriately and avoid causing tumours or immune rejection, and each requirement is a separate hurdle.

Ioannidis (2005) described how early and small studies in a field can overstate the benefits of an intervention, and stem cell therapy has repeatedly produced early enthusiasm followed by more modest results when larger trials were conducted. Controlled trials with clear endpoints, independent replication and long-term follow-up are needed before a therapy can be considered effective.

Induced pluripotent cells, described by Takahashi and Yamanaka (2006), have been proposed as a source of patient-specific cells. The approach avoids immune rejection in principle, but the cells must be checked for genetic and epigenetic abnormalities, and the cost and time of producing them are substantial.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biology of the tissues that stem cells are intended to repair. The evaluation of therapies requires measuring the cells that engraft, their function in the tissue and the safety of the outcome.
