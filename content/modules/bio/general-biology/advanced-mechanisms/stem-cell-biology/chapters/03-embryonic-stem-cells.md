Embryonic stem cells are derived from the inner cell mass of the blastocyst, the group of cells that gives rise to the embryo itself. The cells are removed and cultured in conditions that keep them pluripotent, which usually include signals that suppress differentiation. Once established, the cells can be expanded indefinitely while retaining the capacity to form all the lineages of the body, which makes them a valuable model for studying early development.

Culture conditions are carefully controlled. The cells require specific growth factors and a surface on which they can attach, and their state is maintained by the transcription factor network described in the earlier chapter. Changes in the conditions can shift the cells toward differentiation or toward a more naive state, and the choice of medium is a significant variable in experiments.

Differentiation in vitro can be directed by adding signals that mimic those of the embryo. Cells can be pushed toward neurons, muscle, heart or blood by sequential exposure to growth factors and small molecules. The resulting cells often resemble their in vivo counterparts, but they differ in maturity and in the patterns of gene expression, and they may not function identically when transplanted.

The behaviour of cultured cells differs from that of cells in the embryo. Culture imposes selection, so that the cells that grow fastest may acquire changes, including chromosomal abnormalities and epigenetic changes that are rare in the embryo. The cells also lack the spatial information that guides development in the embryo, which means that their organisation is different.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signals that maintain pluripotency and direct differentiation. Embryonic stem cells are studied by culture, by directed differentiation, by sequencing to detect abnormalities and by transplantation experiments.
