A differentiated cell carries an epigenetic record of its lineage. The genes of other lineages are silenced by DNA methylation and by repressive histone marks, and the genes of its own lineage are active and marked by activating modifications. Reprogramming to pluripotency requires that this record be erased, so that the pluripotency genes can be reactivated and the lineage genes silenced. The epigenetic state is therefore a barrier as well as a memory.

Jenuwein and Allis (2001) described the histone code, and reprogramming shows that marks are not permanent. The removal of repressive marks, the addition of activating marks and the demethylation of DNA all occur during reprogramming, and each of these changes must be coordinated for the cell to adopt a new identity.

Takahashi and Yamanaka (2006) showed that a small set of factors can induce the changes, and the factors act by binding the chromatin of the target genes and recruiting the enzymes that modify it. The reprogramming factors open the chromatin, which makes it accessible to the remodelling and modifying enzymes that complete the erasure.

The barriers are many. Some lineage genes are heavily methylated and difficult to reactivate, and some histone marks are stable through division. The process is inefficient because most cells fail to complete all the changes, and a few reach the pluripotent state by a stochastic route. The barriers explain why reprogramming is slow and why the induced cells often retain traces of their origin.

Epigenetic memory of the original lineage can affect the behaviour of induced cells, and some induced cells differ from embryonic stem cells in their methylation patterns. Understanding these differences is important for the use of induced cells in research and in therapy.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the enzymes that add and remove methylation and histone marks. The epigenetic state of reprogrammed cells is measured by genome-wide maps of methylation and histone modifications, which are compared with those of embryonic cells.
