Adult tissues renew themselves throughout life, and each renewing tissue has a population of stem cells that supplies new cells. The stem cells sit in a niche, a local environment made of supporting cells, matrix and signals that keep them undifferentiated. The niche determines how many stem cells are present and how they divide, and it is the main regulator of the balance between renewal and differentiation.

The intestinal epithelium is renewed every few days from stem cells at the base of the crypts. These cells express a receptor for the Wnt signal, which is produced by the niche, and the signal maintains their stemness. Cells that move out of the niche lose the signal and differentiate into the cell types of the intestine. The continual flow of cells from the crypt to the surface is maintained by this gradient of signal.

The skin is renewed by stem cells in the basal layer and in the hair follicle. The epidermis is organised in layers, and the stem cells divide asymmetrically, producing one cell that stays in the niche and one that moves upward and differentiates. Jenuwein and Allis (2001) described the histone modifications that accompany the changes in gene expression during differentiation, and the changes in chromatin accompany the commitment of epidermal cells.

The blood-forming system is maintained by haematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow, as described in the Histology module. The niche includes stromal cells and blood vessels that supply signals, and the balance between quiescence, self-renewal and differentiation is controlled by the niche and by the demands placed on the system.

Maintenance over a lifetime is a challenge. Stem cells divide, accumulate damage and may be lost, and the niche must replace them. Ageing reduces the function of many stem cell populations, and the reasons are the subject of current research, including changes in the niche and in the stem cells themselves.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling that controls adult stem cells and the cell types they produce. Niches are studied by lineage tracing that follows the progeny of labelled stem cells, by ablation of niche components and by imaging of stem cells in situ.
