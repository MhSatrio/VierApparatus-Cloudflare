A pluripotent cell can give rise to all the cell types of the body, although not to the extraembryonic tissues. The inner cell mass of the blastocyst contains pluripotent cells, and these cells can be grown in culture as embryonic stem cells. Totipotent cells, found in the very early embryo before the first lineage decisions, can give rise to the whole organism including the placenta. Multipotent cells, such as the adult stem cells described later, can produce a restricted range of cell types.

Pluripotency is maintained by a network of transcription factors. The factors Oct4, Sox2 and Nanog are central, and they regulate each other and many target genes. The network sustains its own expression through feedback, so that the pluripotent state is stable. Takahashi and Yamanaka (2006) identified four factors whose expression in differentiated fibroblasts was sufficient to induce a pluripotent state, and these factors include Oct4 and Sox2.

Commitment to a lineage requires the network to be altered. The factors that promote differentiation are switched on, the network is partially dismantled and the chromatin of pluripotency genes is closed. The transition is gradual, and cells pass through intermediate states in which both programmes are partly active.

Jenuwein and Allis (2001) described the histone modifications that accompany changes in gene activity, and the chromatin of pluripotent cells has a distinctive profile of marks, including bivalent domains that carry both activating and repressive marks at developmental genes. These domains poise the genes for activation or repression during differentiation.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signalling pathways and the transcription factors of early development. Pluripotency is studied by culturing embryonic cells, by measuring the expression of the network factors and by testing the ability of cells to contribute to chimeras.
