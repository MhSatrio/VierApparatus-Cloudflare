Takahashi and Yamanaka (2006) reported that introducing four transcription factors into mouse fibroblasts could convert them into cells that resembled embryonic stem cells. The factors were Oct4, Sox2, Klf4 and c-Myc. The reprogrammed cells, called induced pluripotent stem cells, expressed the pluripotency network, could be grown indefinitely and could contribute to the tissues of chimeric embryos. The finding showed that the differentiated state is not fixed, and that cell identity can be reset by changing a small number of regulators.

The experiment was designed to test a specific hypothesis, that the factors characteristic of embryonic stem cells could induce pluripotency. The authors screened candidate factors, removed them one at a time to find the minimal set and tested the resulting cells by several criteria. The strength of the design lay in the use of defined markers and functional tests, which made the claim checkable.

Reprogramming is inefficient. Only a small fraction of the treated cells becomes pluripotent, and the process takes weeks. The barriers include the closed chromatin of the somatic genes, the need to erase the marks of the original lineage and the stochastic nature of the events that complete the switch. The efficiency has improved with additional factors and with chemical treatments that affect chromatin, but it remains a limitation.

Safety is a concern for therapy. The factors include c-Myc, which is an oncogene, and the induced cells may carry genetic and epigenetic abnormalities acquired during culture. Ioannidis (2005) described how early results in a fast-moving field can fail to replicate, and the claims about the safety and equivalence of induced cells require systematic evaluation.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the transcription factors and the chromatin changes that accompany reprogramming. Reprogramming is measured by marker expression, by the ability to form tissues and by the genetic and epigenetic integrity of the cells.
