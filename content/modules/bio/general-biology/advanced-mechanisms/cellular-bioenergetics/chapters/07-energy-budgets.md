A cell spends its ATP on many processes, and the division of the budget reveals its priorities. Protein synthesis is among the most costly, because each peptide bond requires the equivalent of several high-energy phosphate bonds, and a large fraction of the energy of a rapidly growing bacterium is used for it. Maintaining ion gradients is also expensive, and in neurons the sodium pumps can account for a large share of the energy used by the brain. The budget changes with cell type, growth state and environment.

Lane and Martin (2010) argued that the energy available per gene is a major determinant of the complexity a cell can support. Their analysis compared bacteria and eukaryotes, and the difference in energy per gene is large, which they linked to the origin of the mitochondrion. The argument makes a quantitative prediction about the energy a cell can allocate to each gene, and the energy budget is the way to test it.

Budgets are estimated by measuring rates of oxygen consumption and then assigning the fraction of respiration to each process. The method depends on the inhibitors used to block a process, and on assumptions about the stoichiometry of the reactions. The estimates have uncertainties, and the apportionment of energy among processes is often rougher than the total.

Ioannidis (2005) described how estimates derived from many assumptions can give results that are sensitive to the choice of inputs. Budget estimates should therefore be reported with their ranges and checked against independent measurements, such as direct heat production.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the processes that consume ATP and the stoichiometry of their reactions. Energy budgets connect the biochemistry of individual processes to the economy of the whole cell, which is central to understanding why cells are the size they are.
