A membrane separating two aqueous compartments can store energy as an electrochemical gradient. Protons, which are charged, are pumped across the membrane by respiratory or photosynthetic complexes, and the gradient has two components. The chemical component is the difference in proton concentration, expressed as a difference in pH. The electrical component is the difference in voltage across the membrane. Mitchell (1961) called the sum of these two the proton motive force, and it is the currency of energy in the chemiosmotic system.

The free energy stored by the gradient can be calculated. The chemical part contributes an amount proportional to the pH difference multiplied by the gas constant and the absolute temperature, and the electrical part contributes an amount equal to the charge of the proton multiplied by the membrane potential. At physiological temperatures, a pH difference of one unit contributes about sixty millivolts of potential, so the two components can be expressed on a common scale. The proton motive force in mitochondria is typically about two hundred millivolts, which is enough to drive the synthesis of ATP when the energy of hydrolysis is taken into account.

The membrane is essential. Its low permeability to protons allows the gradient to build up and to be maintained. A leaky membrane dissipates the gradient as heat, which is the basis of the uncoupling discussed in the Biochemistry module. Mitchell's hypothesis implied that the inner mitochondrial membrane must be impermeable to protons except at specific sites, and this has been confirmed by the study of the complexes that transport them.

The gradient can also drive the transport of other molecules. Metabolite transporters in the inner mitochondrial membrane use the proton gradient as well as the ATP synthase, so that the same stored energy supports both synthesis and exchange across the membrane.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the membrane complexes that generate the gradient and the transporters it drives. The gradient is measured by pH-sensitive fluorescent probes, by voltage-sensitive dyes and by electrodes in isolated mitochondria or bacteria.
