The respiratory chain consists of four large protein complexes and two mobile carriers. Complex I accepts electrons from NADH and passes them to ubiquinone. Complex II, which is also part of the citric acid cycle, accepts electrons from succinate and passes them to ubiquinone without pumping protons. Complex III transfers electrons from ubiquinol to cytochrome c, and complex IV transfers them from cytochrome c to oxygen, reducing it to water. The electrons therefore move from a low-potential donor to a high-potential acceptor, releasing energy at each step.

Mitchell (1961) proposed that the energy of these transfers is used to pump protons, and the pumping is carried out by complexes I, III and IV. Complex I pumps protons as electrons move through its iron-sulphur clusters and quinone-binding site, and complex III uses the Q cycle, in which ubiquinol is oxidised at two sites and the electrons are routed to the two cytochromes, pumping protons on each pass. Complex IV pumps protons as it reduces oxygen, using a binuclear centre of haem and copper.

The ratio of protons pumped per pair of electrons differs among the complexes, and the total yield per NADH is typically about ten protons, and about six per FADH2 because complex II does not pump. The ratio determines how much gradient each substrate can produce, and it affects the number of ATP molecules obtained from each substrate.

The complexes can associate in supercomplexes, which may improve the efficiency of electron transfer and reduce the escape of reactive oxygen species. The organisation of the complexes within the membrane is therefore part of their function.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the components of the chain and the carriers that connect them. The complexes are studied by isolation and by spectroscopy of their cofactors, by measuring proton pumping in reconstituted vesicles and by structural studies.
