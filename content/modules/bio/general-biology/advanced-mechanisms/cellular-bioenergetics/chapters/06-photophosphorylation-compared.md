Photosynthesis generates ATP by the same chemiosmotic principle as respiration, but the electrons come from light rather than from food. In oxygenic photosynthesis, light absorbed by photosystem II drives the removal of electrons from water, which releases oxygen. The electrons pass through a chain of carriers to photosystem I, which absorbs a second photon and raises the electrons to a high-potential state, where they reduce NADP+ to NADPH. Nelson and Ben-Shem (2004) reviewed the architecture of these complexes and the carriers between them.

The electron transfer through the chain pumps protons into the thylakoid lumen, in the same way that respiratory complexes pump them across the inner mitochondrial membrane. The gradient drives ATP synthase, which is located in the thylakoid membrane with its catalytic part facing the stroma. Mitchell (1961) described the chemiosmotic principle for respiration, and the same principle explains photophosphorylation, a case that confirmed its generality.

The two systems differ in important ways. The light reactions use two photosystems in series, which allow the electrons to be raised to a potential high enough to reduce NADP+, while respiration uses a single chain from food-derived donors. The thylakoid lumen is the compartment that accumulates protons, and its orientation is opposite to the matrix of mitochondria, which means that the ATP synthase faces in the opposite direction.

The energy budget also differs. Photosynthesis must capture light and convert it to a potential, and the efficiency of this conversion depends on the absorption of light by the pigments and on losses such as the emission of fluorescence. The chemistry of the carriers is adapted to these constraints.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the photosynthetic apparatus and its relation to the chloroplast. Photophosphorylation is studied by measuring the light-driven gradient in isolated thylakoids, by fluorescence methods that report the state of photosystem II and by mutants in the photosystem components.
