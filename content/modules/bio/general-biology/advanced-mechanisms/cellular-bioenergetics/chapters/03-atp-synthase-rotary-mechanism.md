ATP synthase is a molecular machine with two parts. The F0 part is embedded in the membrane and forms a channel through which protons flow. The F1 part projects into the matrix or the cytoplasm and contains the catalytic sites that make ATP. The two parts are connected by a central stalk and a peripheral stalk. Proton flow through F0 drives the rotation of a ring of subunits and of the central stalk, and the rotation changes the conformation of the catalytic sites in F1.

Boyer (1997) proposed the binding change mechanism, in which the three catalytic sites of F1 cycle through three conformations: one that binds ADP and phosphate loosely, one that binds them tightly so that ATP is formed, and one that releases the product ATP. Each rotation of the central stalk by one hundred and twenty degrees moves each site through the cycle, so the three sites together produce three ATP molecules per full turn. The mechanism explained how the energy of the gradient could be used to form a bond without the bond being formed directly by the energy.

Boyer and colleagues later showed that the catalytic sites do not act independently. The affinity of each site changes with the rotation, and the energy of the gradient is used to change the binding energy rather than to form the phosphoanhydride bond itself. The mechanism is therefore a way of coupling a conformational change to a chemical equilibrium.

Rotation of the F1 part was demonstrated directly by attaching a fluorescent actin filament to the central stalk and observing it turn in steps of one hundred and twenty degrees. The experiment confirmed the rotary model in a direct way, and it has since been repeated with other methods.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the structure of the complex and the coupling of proton flow to ATP synthesis. The rotary mechanism is studied by structural methods, by single-molecule rotation assays and by mutations that change the coupling efficiency.
