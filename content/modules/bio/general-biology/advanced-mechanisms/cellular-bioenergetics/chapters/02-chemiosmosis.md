Before Mitchell (1961), the leading explanation of oxidative phosphorylation was the chemical coupling hypothesis, which proposed that the energy of electron transfer was passed to ATP through a covalent high-energy intermediate. No such intermediate was ever isolated, and the hypothesis could not explain why the membrane was essential. Mitchell proposed instead that electron transfer pumps protons across the membrane, and that the resulting gradient drives ATP synthesis. The idea was initially strongly contested, and its acceptance required decisive experiments.

The chemiosmotic model makes several predictions that can be tested. Membranes that are sealed and that pump protons should build a gradient when electron transfer occurs. Collapsing the gradient with an ionophore should stop ATP synthesis, even when the electron supply continues. ATP synthesis should be driven by an artificially imposed gradient in the absence of electron transfer. Experiments of this kind, done in the 1960s and 1970s, supported each of these predictions, and they established the model.

The importance of the membrane follows directly. Mitchell's model requires the inner mitochondrial membrane to be a barrier to protons and to contain the complexes that pump them. The idea that a membrane could be a site of energy conversion was a major conceptual step, and it changed the view of membranes from passive barriers to active energy transducers.

The model is an example of how a hypothesis can be tested by its predictions even when the molecular mechanism is unknown. Ioannidis (2005) emphasised that the reliability of a claim depends on the tests applied to it, and the chemiosmotic model is a case in which strong and direct tests were decisive.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the complexes that pump protons and the ATP synthase that uses the gradient. The chemiosmotic principle is studied by measuring the gradient and ATP synthesis in isolated membranes, by reconstituting the system in artificial vesicles and by genetic deletion of complex components.
