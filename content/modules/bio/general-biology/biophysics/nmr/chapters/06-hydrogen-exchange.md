The amide hydrogens of the protein backbone exchange with the hydrogens of the solvent, and the rate of exchange depends on whether the hydrogen is free or is involved in a hydrogen bond in a folded structure. A hydrogen bonded in a helix or a sheet is protected from the solvent and exchanges slowly, while one in a flexible loop exchanges rapidly. Hydrogen exchange therefore reports the folded regions of a protein, and it does so in solution under conditions that can be controlled. Dill and MacCallum (2012) described the stability of the native state as the balance that makes the protection possible, and exchange measures that balance region by region.

The exchange is measured by transferring the protein into deuterated water and following the replacement of the amide hydrogens by deuterium. NMR detects the hydrogen signals as they disappear, and mass spectrometry detects the increase in mass as deuterium is incorporated. Both methods give the rates for each residue, and the pattern of protected and exposed residues is the map of the folded structure.

The protection reflects the stability of the structure. A protected region opens, transiently, and exposes its hydrogens to the solvent, and the rate of exchange is the product of the rate of opening and the intrinsic rate of exchange of the exposed hydrogen. The free energy of the opening follows from the exchange rate, and the regions that are most stable, and the ones that open first when the protein is perturbed, are revealed.

The interpretation requires assumptions. The intrinsic exchange rate depends on the sequence and the pH, and the conversion from exchange rate to free energy assumes that opening is the only process that exposes the hydrogen. A region that is protected by a bound partner or by the packing of other regions will show different protection, and the interpretation must consider the interactions that the measurement does not isolate.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the protection pattern may be interpreted as a precise description of the folded regions when the conversion depends on assumptions, and Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the explanations, such as the comparison of the pattern with the known structure, which should show the same protected regions.

Hydrogen exchange reads the protection of the backbone by its structure, and its conversion to stability depends on the assumptions of the exchange model.
