Many proteins and regions of proteins have no stable folded structure, and they exist as ensembles of rapidly interconverting conformations. The principle of Anfinsen (1973) that the sequence determines a unique fold does not apply to them in the same way, and the methods developed for folded proteins, which assume a single structure, are inadequate. NMR is well suited to these molecules, because it reports on individual residues and does not require the molecule to adopt one state. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the functional role of many disordered regions in signalling and regulation, and their study has become a major part of biophysics.

The NMR signature of disorder is distinctive. The chemical shifts of the disordered residues lie close to the values of a random coil, the unstructured reference state, and the resonances are narrow because the residues move rapidly. The absence of the dispersion of shifts seen in folded proteins is itself the evidence of disorder, and the shifts give the population of any transient structure as deviations from the random-coil values.

The conformational ensemble is described by many conformations weighted by their populations. The ensemble is more difficult to determine than a single structure, because the distance restraints average over many states and the number of states is large. Methods that generate ensembles fit them to the averaged data, and the fit can be achieved by many different ensembles, so the description is an uncertain one.

Transient structure within a disordered region is detected by small but systematic deviations from the random-coil shifts, by long-range nuclear Overhauser effects and by paramagnetic probes that report on compaction. The structure is present only for a fraction of the time, and the measurements give its average. Ioannidis (2005) described how a transient structure inferred from small deviations may be presented as a defined structure, and the uncertainty must be stated.

Disordered regions become ordered when they bind a partner, and the NMR of this coupled folding reports the change in conformation and the residues that take part in the interaction. Platt (1964) recommended comparisons of the free and bound states that could show which residues change and which remain disordered.

The NMR of disordered proteins describes an ensemble rather than a structure, and the reliability of the description depends on the number of measurements that constrain the ensemble.
