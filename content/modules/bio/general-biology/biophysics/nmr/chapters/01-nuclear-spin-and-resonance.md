Atomic nuclei with a nonzero spin behave as tiny magnets, and in a strong external magnetic field they align either with or against the field, with a small energy difference between the two orientations. The difference corresponds to a radio frequency, and the population difference between the orientations is very small, which is why NMR is intrinsically insensitive. The hydrogen nucleus, present in nearly every biological molecule, is the most sensitive, and carbon and nitrogen, which are less abundant in their NMR-active forms, usually require isotopic labelling in biological work.

A radio-frequency pulse tips the net magnetisation of the nuclei away from the field direction, and the precessing magnetisation induces a signal in a coil. The signal decays as the nuclei relax back to equilibrium, and this decay, the free induction decay, contains all the frequencies of the nuclei in the sample. A Fourier transform converts it into the spectrum, in which each resonance appears at a frequency, the chemical shift, that depends on the electronic environment of the nucleus.

The spectrum of a protein is crowded, because a large molecule contains many nuclei of the same element in similar environments. Multidimensional experiments spread the signals into two or three frequency dimensions, correlating nuclei that are coupled through bonds or through space. The correlations are the basis of the assignment, and the complexity of the experiment grows with the size of the molecule, which limits the method.

The sensitivity and the magnetic field strength set the practical limits. Higher fields give larger separations of the resonances and greater sensitivity, and the instrument's cost and the need for isotope labelling constrain the work. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the folded state of a protein is determined by its chain, and NMR observes the same state in solution, so the limit of the method is set by the size and complexity of the molecule rather than by its state.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a spectrum that is interpreted with many assumptions may give an assignment that is not unique, and Platt (1964) recommended the cross-checking of assignments by independent experiments, which reveals errors that a single spectrum would hide.

NMR detects the magnetic environment of individual nuclei, and its sensitivity and resolution set what can be learned from a molecule in solution.
