The chemical shift of a nucleus is its resonance frequency relative to a standard, and it depends on the electrons around the nucleus, which shield it from the field. A carbon in a carbonyl group, an aromatic carbon and an aliphatic carbon resonate at very different frequencies, and the shift therefore identifies the type of atom. The shifts of backbone atoms also depend on the local conformation, since the electron distribution changes with the dihedral angles of the chain, which allows the secondary structure to be read from the shifts.

Empirical and quantum calculations predict shifts from structure, and the predictions can be compared with the measured shifts to test a model or to guide assignment. The predictions are approximate, and their accuracy limits how well a shift can be interpreted as a structural measure. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the chain adopts a defined fold, and the shifts that report the fold are the consequence of its local conformations.

Assignment links each resonance to a specific atom of a specific residue. In a protein, the backbone atoms of each residue are correlated with those of its neighbours through experiments that transfer magnetisation along the chain, so that the sequence of resonances can be traced in the order of the amino acids. The sequential walk is interrupted by proline residues, which lack a backbone amide hydrogen, and by gaps where the signal is weak or missing.

Side-chain assignment follows the backbone assignment, using correlations through the chemical bonds of each residue. The complete set of assignments is the basis of the structure, since the distances between the assigned atoms are measured in the next stage. The assignment is checked for consistency, since an error in one residue propagates to the walk along the chain.

Ioannidis (2005) described how an assignment that is accepted without checking may propagate errors into the structure, and Platt (1964) recommended cross-checks, such as the consistency of the assignment across several experiments, which reveal the places where the walk has gone wrong.

Chemical shifts identify the atoms and report their local conformation, and the assignment converts the spectrum into a list of atoms whose distances can then be measured.
