# NMR

Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy measures the magnetic properties of atomic nuclei in a molecule, and the frequencies at which they resonate depend on their chemical environment. Unlike crystallography, NMR studies molecules in solution, at room temperature and in conditions close to those of the cell, and it can detect motion as well as structure. The method is limited to molecules of moderate size, but within that range it gives a structure and a picture of the dynamics that no other method provides in solution.

The chapters begin with the physics of nuclear spin and resonance, which underlies the signals. The assignment of each signal to a specific atom in the molecule follows, since the structure is built from the distances between assigned atoms. The nuclear Overhauser effect, which provides the distances, is then described, together with the calculation of structures from the distances and the restraints that accompany them. Relaxation and hydrogen exchange, which report on dynamics and stability, follow, and the final chapters address the NMR of disordered proteins and the reporting of NMR structures.

The NMR structure is an ensemble of models consistent with the restraints, and the spread among the models reflects the uncertainty of the data. The ensemble must be interpreted as such, and the regions where the models agree are better determined than those where they diverge. Ioannidis (2005) described how a model built from restraints may be presented with more precision than the restraints support, and Platt (1964) recommended checks that could show the restraints to be inconsistent with the structure.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular environment in which molecules act, and the solution state of NMR is closer to that environment than the crystal in many cases. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the folded state is determined by the chain in solution, which is the state that NMR observes.
