# Statistical Mechanics

The Thermodynamics of Living Systems topic described the energy that governs biological processes in macroscopic terms. Statistical mechanics explains where those macroscopic quantities come from. A cell contains billions of molecules, each in one of many possible states, and the behaviour of the cell is the average over all of them. The discipline shows how the probabilities of molecular states, weighted by their energies and the temperature, produce the free energies and equilibrium constants that the earlier topic used.

The chapters begin with the Boltzmann distribution, which gives the probability that a molecule occupies a state of a given energy, and the partition function that normalises it. Free energies of binding and folding follow from these, and the treatment of equilibrium is extended to the fluctuations that the average conceals. The final chapters address the application to the crowded and non-equilibrium environment of the cell and the limits of the equilibrium picture when it is applied to living systems. Zwanzig (1954) showed how perturbation methods could approximate the statistics of interacting molecules, and the same logic extends to the approximations used in biology.

The statistical description is a probability model, and its predictions must be checked against measurements of the populations of states, which are often indirect. Ioannidis (2005) described how an approximation that works in simple systems may be applied to complex ones without the check, and Platt (1964) recommended measurements that could discriminate between the statistical models of the same system.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular populations whose statistics this topic describes, and each cell is an ensemble of such populations.
