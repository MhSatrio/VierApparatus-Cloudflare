The properties of a molecule or a small compartment in a cell are not constant. The number of receptors bound at a given moment, the number of molecules in a vesicle or the concentration near a membrane all fluctuate as molecules arrive and leave. The average value describes the system, but the fluctuation describes how reliably the system holds that value. In a large system the fluctuations are small compared with the average, and in a small one they can be large, so that the average is a poor guide to what any single cell experiences.

The relative size of the fluctuation in a count of molecules falls roughly as the inverse square root of the mean number, so that a system with a million molecules fluctuates by about a thousandth of its average, while one with a hundred fluctuates by a tenth. This scaling explains why cells with few copies of a regulator behave differently from those with many, and why noise limits the precision of signalling in small compartments. Berg and Purcell (1977) analysed the limits that such fluctuations place on the ability of a cell to detect a chemical concentration, and the statistics of counting molecules set a floor on what can be sensed.

The ensemble is the set of states over which the average is taken, and the choice depends on what is held fixed. The canonical ensemble holds the temperature and the number of particles fixed, and it is appropriate for a system in contact with a heat bath. The grand canonical ensemble allows the number of particles to fluctuate, which suits a compartment exchanging molecules with a reservoir. The choice of ensemble determines which fluctuations are included in the calculation.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a result reported as an average may conceal a distribution in which a few members behave very differently, and the reporting of single-cell variation is necessary when the population is heterogeneous. Platt (1964) recommended measurements of individual systems, which distinguish between a population in which every cell is intermediate and one in which cells are a mixture of extremes.

The average describes the population, and the fluctuation describes the individual, and both must be known to understand a biological system.
