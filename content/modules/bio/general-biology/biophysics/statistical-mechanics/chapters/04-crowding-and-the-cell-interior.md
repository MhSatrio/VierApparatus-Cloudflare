The cytoplasm of a cell contains a large amount of protein, nucleic acid and other macromolecules, which together occupy a substantial fraction of its volume. Most measurements of biochemistry are made in dilute solution, where a molecule can move freely through the solvent. In the crowded interior, the space available to each molecule is restricted by its neighbours, and the statistics of its states change. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the organisation of the cytoplasm, and the crowding is one of its defining features.

Excluded volume is the principal effect. A compact state of a macromolecule occupies less space than an extended one, and in a crowded environment the compact state is favoured because it leaves more room for the surrounding molecules. The effect shifts equilibria towards compact forms, including the folded state of a protein and the associated state of a complex, and it can stabilise a fold that would be marginal in dilute solution. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the folded state is the stable state under dilute conditions, and crowding strengthens that stability in the cell.

The Boltzmann and partition function treatment of the earlier chapters applies to the crowded state, but the energies and the number of accessible states differ from those in dilute solution. The excluded volume enters as a term in the free energy, and the magnitude of the shift depends on the size and the shape of the crowding molecules. Zwanzig (1954) developed the perturbation methods that allow such interaction effects to be estimated.

Measuring crowding requires comparison. The same reaction is studied in dilute solution and in solutions containing inert crowding agents, such as polymers of defined size, and the shift in equilibrium or rate is attributed to the crowding. The crowding agents must be inert, since a chemically active agent may produce the effect for a different reason.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a model of crowding that fits one system may not transfer to another with different molecular shapes, and Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the crowding effect from other explanations, such as specific binding to the crowding agent.

The interior of the cell is a crowded medium whose effects must be measured in place of being assumed from dilute-solution results.
