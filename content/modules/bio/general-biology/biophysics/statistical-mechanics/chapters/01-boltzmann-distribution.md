A molecule at a finite temperature is not fixed in a single state. Its thermal energy causes it to move among the states available to it, and the Boltzmann distribution gives the probability of each state. The probability falls exponentially with the energy of the state, scaled by the thermal energy, so that low-energy states are more populated than high-energy ones but higher states are never empty. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the conformational states of proteins and the energy landscapes that these states occupy, and the distribution determines which states a protein spends its time in.

For two states, the ratio of their populations is the exponential of minus the energy difference divided by the thermal energy. A difference of a few kilojoules per mole changes the ratio by a large factor at room temperature, which is why small energy differences produce large changes in population. The calculation is simple, and it explains the sensitivity of biological equilibria to temperature and to small changes in binding energy.

Temperature changes the populations because it changes the thermal energy relative to the energy differences. At higher temperature, the distribution flattens and the high-energy states become more populated, which is the basis of the thermal unfolding of proteins. Zwanzig (1954) developed perturbation methods for the statistics of interacting molecules, and the Boltzmann weighting of states underlies these methods.

The energy alone does not determine the populations in a living cell. The number of states at each energy, which is the entropy, also contributes, and a state with many configurations may be more populated than one with lower energy but few configurations. The free energy combines both terms, and it is the quantity that determines the populations at equilibrium.

Ioannidis (2005) described how an assumption that the populations are determined by a single energy can lead to predictions that fail when the entropy is ignored, and Platt (1964) recommended measurements of populations at several temperatures that could discriminate between the models that include and exclude the entropic term.

The Boltzmann distribution connects the energy of a molecule to the frequency with which it is found in each state, and its use requires attention to both energy and the number of states.
