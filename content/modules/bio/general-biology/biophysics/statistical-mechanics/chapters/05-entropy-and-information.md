Entropy, in statistical mechanics, is a count of the microstates consistent with a system's observed macroscopic state. A system with many accessible microstates has high entropy and a large uncertainty about which microstate it occupies. The connection to information is direct: learning the state of a system reduces the uncertainty, and the reduction is measured by the change in entropy. Biological systems can be described in these terms, since a cell that senses its environment or copies its genome is gaining information about one state at the expense of another.

Berg and Purcell (1977) analysed the sensing of a chemical concentration by a cell with receptors, and the accuracy of the measurement is limited by the number of molecules counted over the time available. Their analysis showed that the precision of sensing is set by the statistics of arrival and binding, which is an information limit on what a cell can know about its environment. The limit is a physical one, and no biochemical mechanism can exceed it.

The energy cost of accuracy follows from thermodynamics. A process that discriminates between two states with high accuracy must make irreversible transitions that dissipate energy, because reversible processes cannot distinguish the states without an energy input. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the proofreading of DNA replication, in which the enzyme spends energy to reject incorrect bases, and the accuracy achieved is paid for by the dissipation.

The relation between information and energy has been a source of confusion, and the claim that information can be created without cost is not supported by the thermodynamic argument. The cost of erasing or measuring information is bounded below by the energy that the process dissipates, and the bound is reached only by idealised processes.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a claim that a biological system is optimal in information or accuracy may be made without measuring its cost or its limit, and Platt (1964) recommended a comparison of the measured accuracy with the thermodynamic bound, which tests whether the system is near the limit or far from it.

Entropy counts the possibilities, and information reduces them, and each reduction costs energy that the biological system must supply.
