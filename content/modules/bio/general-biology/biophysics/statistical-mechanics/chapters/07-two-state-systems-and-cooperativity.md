Many biological units have two accessible states, bound or unbound, folded or unfolded, open or closed. For a single unit the fraction in the active state follows directly from the Boltzmann distribution: it rises from zero to one as the ligand concentration or the energy difference changes, along a curve whose midpoint is set by the affinity. The curve is hyperbolic for a single binding site, and its steepness is fixed by the form of the equation. Anfinsen (1973) described the folding of a protein as such a two-state transition in simple cases, where the fraction folded changes sharply over a narrow range of conditions.

Cooperativity arises when several units interact. If the binding of one ligand changes the affinity of the next, the response of the whole becomes steeper than that of an independent site, and the curve takes a sigmoidal shape whose steepness is characterised by the Hill coefficient. The steep response is what allows a biological switch to turn on sharply over a narrow range of stimulus, rather than gradually. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cooperative binding of oxygen by haemoglobin as the classical example of this behaviour.

The statistical mechanics of cooperativity is the sum over the states of the whole assembly, with the interaction energies entering the Boltzmann weights. For a small number of units the sum can be written in closed form, and the Hill coefficient is then a consequence of the interaction energy. The model must be tested against the measured curve, and its parameters must be constrained by more than a single curve, since several interaction models can fit one curve equally well.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a steep curve may be interpreted as cooperativity when it arises from a different cause, such as a change in the number of units or an experimental artefact, and Platt (1964) recommended measurements that could separate the explanations, such as the dependence of the curve on the number of binding partners.

Two-state units combined by interactions give the switching behaviour that biology relies on, and the steepness of the switch is a property of the interactions between units.
