The equilibrium statistics of the earlier chapters describe an isolated system that has reached its most probable arrangement. The living cell is neither isolated nor at equilibrium. It exchanges matter and energy with its environment, it maintains steady fluxes that dissipate energy, as the thermodynamics topic described, and many of its processes are driven by the hydrolysis of ATP. Equilibrium statistics is therefore an approximation for the cell, and it is useful where the process of interest is close to equilibrium or where its driven parts can be separated from it.

The question of when the approximation holds has a practical answer. A binding reaction between two molecules in the cytoplasm may be close to equilibrium if the reaction is fast relative to the changes in the concentrations of its partners, and its populations can then be described by the equilibrium constant. A cycle that is driven by ATP and that has a directional flux is far from equilibrium, and its populations are not those of the Boltzmann distribution. Berg and Purcell (1977) analysed the sensing of a concentration by a cell, where the statistics of binding and the time of measurement determine the outcome, and the approach to equilibrium is part of the answer.

Crowding and compartmentation further complicate the picture. The local concentrations and the energies of molecules differ between regions of the cell, and the same molecule may have different equilibria in different compartments. The methods of Zwanzig (1954) were developed for simple fluids, and the cell's heterogeneity requires their extension.

The measurements that test whether an equilibrium description applies include the dependence of the populations on temperature and concentration, the reversibility of the process and the time scale over which equilibrium is reached. A process whose populations change with an energy-driven input, and that reverses when the input is removed, is well described by equilibrium. A process whose populations depend on the direction of approach or on a continuous supply of energy is not.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a model of an equilibrium that fits a measurement in vitro may be applied to the cell without verifying that the cell is at equilibrium, and Platt (1964) recommended the tests of reversibility and of approach that discriminate between the two situations.

Equilibrium statistics describes the living cell only where the cell is close to equilibrium, and the cell's driven processes require the statistics of steady states.
