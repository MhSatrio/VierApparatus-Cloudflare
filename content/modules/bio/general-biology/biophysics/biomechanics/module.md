# Biomechanics

Living things move, bear loads and change shape, and these mechanical functions depend on the physics of their materials. Bones resist compression, tendons transmit force, muscles generate it and cells sense and respond to the forces that act on them. Biomechanics applies the principles of mechanics to these tissues and their components, and it connects the forces that act on an organism to the structures that produce and withstand them. The Muscle and Cytoskeleton topics described the molecules involved, and this topic describes how they behave as mechanical systems.

The chapters begin with the mechanism of muscle contraction, the sliding of filaments that Huxley and Niedergerke (1954) observed directly in living fibres by interference microscopy. The mechanics of muscle as a material, its force-velocity relation and the energetics of work follow. The mechanics of the cell and its cytoskeleton, the sensing of force by cells and the measurement of mechanical properties with the methods that combine force with structure are then described, and the final chapters address the mechanics of tissues and the limits of mechanical models of living systems.

Mechanical measurements on living tissue are subject to many confounding influences, since the tissue is alive, changes with time and responds to its own forces. Ioannidis (2005) described how a mechanical model that fits a measurement may be applied to tissue whose behaviour depends on biology the model does not include, and Platt (1964) recommended measurements that could separate the mechanical from the biological contributions.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cytoskeleton and the molecular motors whose mechanics underlie the forces that tissues generate.
