Movement costs energy, and the metabolic cost of locomotion is the energy that the muscles consume in producing the work of moving the body and in holding the body against gravity. A large part of the energy that the body uses in running is not spent on the work of the muscles but is stored and returned by the elastic tendons. As the leg lands, the tendons stretch and store energy, which is released as the foot leaves the ground, and this recycling reduces the work the muscles must do. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the elastic properties of the connective tissues that make this storage possible.

The metabolic cost is set by the energy that the muscles consume, and the link between this energy and the ATP hydrolysis of the cross-bridge cycle is the link between the mechanics of the previous chapters and the energy budget of the whole animal. Mitchell (1961) described the chemiosmotic coupling by which the energy of food is converted into the ATP that drives the muscle, and the efficiency of this conversion sets the metabolic cost of the work.

The efficiency of locomotion is the ratio of the mechanical work done to the metabolic energy consumed. It is typically well below one, because part of the energy of the muscles is lost as heat, and the efficiency depends on the speed, the gait and the load. The optimum speed for the most economical movement reflects the trade-off between the cost of the cross-bridge cycle at low speed and the cost of the faster movements at high speed.

Energy use is measured by respirometry, which records the oxygen consumed and the carbon dioxide produced, and from which the metabolic power is calculated. The mechanical work is measured by the forces and motions of the body, and the two measures together give the efficiency. Each measure has errors, and the consistency of the energy budget checks the measurements, as the thermodynamics topic described.

Ioannidis (2005) described how an efficiency reported for a single gait or species may be generalised to all movement without the comparison, and Platt (1964) recommended comparisons across gaits and species that could show where the efficiency varies.

The energetics of movement combines the mechanics of muscle and tendon with the energy budget of the body, and its measurement requires the consistency of both sides of the account.
