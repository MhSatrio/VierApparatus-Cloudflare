Connective tissues such as tendon, ligament, cartilage and skin resist load through their extracellular matrices, which are dominated by collagen fibres embedded in a hydrated network. The fibres carry most of the tensile load, and their arrangement determines the mechanical response of the tissue. A tendon whose fibres are aligned along its length is stiff and strong in that direction and weak across it, and the anisotropy reflects the directions in which the tissue is loaded in life. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the extracellular matrix and the collagen network that give these tissues their structure.

Tissues are viscoelastic. Under a sustained load they creep, deforming slowly over time, and when the load is removed they recover slowly, dissipating energy in the process. The viscous component reflects the flow of the matrix and the water within it, and the elastic component the stretched fibres. Tests that apply a step load or an oscillating stress separate the two components, and the tissue's behaviour depends on the rate of loading.

Tissues remodel in response to their loads. Cells within the matrix, such as tendon fibroblasts and chondrocytes, sense the mechanical state and adjust the synthesis and degradation of the matrix, so that the tissue becomes stiffer where it is loaded and weaker where it is unloaded. The remodelling takes days to months, and it is the basis of the adaptation of bone and tendon to exercise and of the damage that prolonged unloading causes.

Tissue mechanics is measured by tensile tests that stretch a sample to failure, by compression tests that load it in the other direction and by indentation tests that probe its local stiffness. Each test samples a different aspect of the tissue, and the results depend on the size of the sample, the rate of loading and the hydration.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a tissue whose mechanical properties are measured in isolation may behave differently in the body, and Platt (1964) recommended comparisons of isolated and in situ measurements that could show the difference.

Tissues are composite materials whose anisotropy and viscoelasticity reflect their fibres and their history of loading.
