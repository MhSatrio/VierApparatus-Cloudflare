A cell is neither a rigid solid nor a simple liquid. It deforms under load and recovers slowly, which is the behaviour of a viscoelastic material, and most of its stiffness comes from the cortex, a thin meshwork of actin filaments and their crosslinkers beneath the plasma membrane. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cytoskeleton and its organisation, and the cortex is a dynamic network whose stiffness reflects the density of its filaments and the tension generated by myosin motors acting on them.

The tension is generated by the same motors that drive muscle contraction. Myosin pulls on crosslinked actin filaments, and the network is put under stress, which stiffens it and allows the cell to resist deformation. The contractility of the cortex is therefore an active property that depends on the energy supplied by ATP, and inhibiting the motors softens the cell. Huxley and Niedergerke (1954) established the sliding of filaments in muscle, and the same principle of motor-driven sliding acts in the cortex.

Cell stiffness is measured by micromanipulation. A bead is attached to the cell surface and pulled or pushed, and the resulting deformation is recorded. Atomic force microscopy presses a tip into the cell and records the force-indentation curve, from which the elastic modulus is calculated. The measurements depend on the geometry of the probe, the rate of loading and the depth of indentation, and the results must be compared under defined conditions.

The cytoskeleton transmits forces across the cell. A force applied to the surface is carried through the cortex to the nucleus and to the adhesions that attach the cell to its environment, and the forces shape the cell and drive its movement. The transmission depends on the connectivity of the network, and its disruption changes the mechanical response.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a single stiffness value may be taken as a property of the cell when it depends on the probe, the depth and the state of the cell, and Platt (1964) recommended comparisons across probes and conditions that could show which features of the measurement are the cell's own.

The cell's mechanics come from an active network whose tension makes it stiff and whose connections carry forces across it, and their measurement depends on the probe and the conditions.
