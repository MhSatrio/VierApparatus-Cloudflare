Mechanical properties are measured by methods that match the scale of the system. Optical tweezers, described by Ashkin (1970), apply piconewton forces to single molecules and beads inside cells. Atomic force microscopy presses a fine tip into a cell or a tissue and records the force and the indentation. Micromanipulation pulls on cells and tissues with calibrated probes, and mechanical testing machines stretch or compress larger samples. Motion capture records the movements of whole organisms, from which the forces and energies are inferred. Each method covers a different range of forces and sizes, and no single method spans them all.

Calibration is the foundation of every measurement. A force transducer or a trap must be calibrated against a known standard, and the calibration must be repeated when the conditions change. The sensitivity and drift of the instrument set the smallest force that can be resolved, and a measurement reported without its calibration cannot be compared with another. Huxley and Niedergerke (1954) made their measurements of muscle structure with an optical method whose calibration was tied to known dimensions of the fibre, and the same principle of calibration applies to the modern instruments.

Sample preparation changes the mechanics. Tissues that are removed from the body, dehydrated or fixed have different properties, and cells that are plated on a substrate differ from those in the tissue. The condition of the sample must be reported, and the comparison with in situ measurements tests whether the preparation has altered the result.

The reporting of a mechanical measurement must give the probe, the loading rate, the geometry, the temperature and the sample conditions. A stiffness value without these is incomplete, since the same tissue can give different values under different loads and rates.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a mechanical value reported without its conditions is treated as a property of the material, and Platt (1964) recommended comparisons across methods, which show whether a measured property is independent of the probe.

Mechanical measurements span the scales of life, and their comparability depends on calibration, sample conditions and the complete reporting of how each was made.
