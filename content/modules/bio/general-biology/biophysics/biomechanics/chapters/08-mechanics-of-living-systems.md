The mechanics of a living system is not a fixed property of its parts. Cells generate the forces that shape the tissues they form, and the tissues in turn carry forces that direct the cells, so that form and force are coupled through feedback. In development, the folding of an epithelium and the elongation of an embryo depend on the forces that cells exert on one another, and the same forces guide the growth of organs. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular behaviours that produce these forces, and the mechanics of development is the integration of many such behaviours.

The feedback also operates in repair and in disease. A tissue that is damaged remodels in response to the mechanical state of the wound, and a tissue that is persistently overloaded adapts its structure. The adaptation can be beneficial or harmful, and the mechanical signal that drives it is a potential target of intervention. Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) described the electrical signals that carry information in the nervous system, and mechanical signals carry comparable information in tissues.

Living materials differ from passive engineering materials. They generate their own forces, they remodel in response to load, and their properties change with age and with the state of the organism. A model that treats the tissue as a fixed elastic solid will misrepresent its behaviour when the tissue is active or remodelling, and the choice of model must be justified for the question.

The open questions concern how the mechanical and the biochemical signals are integrated, how the forces are controlled at the scale of the whole organism and how the mechanical properties are maintained against the turnover of their components. The answers require the combination of the methods described in this topic with the molecular and cellular biology of the earlier modules.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a mechanical model that describes one tissue may be extended to others without evidence that the same mechanisms operate, and Platt (1964) recommended the tests that separate the mechanical from the biochemical contributions to a given behaviour.

The mechanics of living systems emerges from the coupling of force and form, and its understanding requires models that treat the living material as active and changing.
