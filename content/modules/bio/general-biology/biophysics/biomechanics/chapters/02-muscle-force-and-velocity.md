A muscle that is loaded with a heavy weight shortens slowly, and one loaded lightly shortens quickly. The relation between the force a muscle produces and its velocity of shortening is hyperbolic, with the maximum force at zero velocity and the maximum velocity at zero force. This force-velocity relation is a basic property of muscle, and it reflects the cycling of the cross-bridges. A heavy load holds the cross-bridges in their force-producing states for longer, slowing the cycle, while a light load allows the cross-bridges to release quickly.

The length-tension relation reflects the overlap between the thick and thin filaments described in the previous chapter. At optimal length, the filaments overlap so that many cross-bridges can attach, and the force is greatest. At very short lengths, the filaments interfere with one another and the force falls, and at long lengths, the overlap is reduced and fewer cross-bridges can act. The geometry of the sarcomere therefore sets the force as a function of its length.

The power of a muscle is the product of force and velocity. The power is zero at zero force and at zero velocity, and it reaches a maximum at an intermediate load, which is the load at which the muscle is most effective at doing work. Most animal movements are performed at loads near this optimum, and the matching of the muscle's properties to the task reflects an evolutionary tuning of the force-velocity relation.

The efficiency of the conversion of chemical energy into mechanical work is limited. Part of the energy of ATP hydrolysis is released as heat, and the fraction converted to work depends on the load and the velocity. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the energy released by the hydrolysis of ATP, and the efficiency of the muscle is the ratio of the work to this energy. The thermodynamic limits discussed in the thermodynamics topic set the ceiling.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the force-velocity relation fitted to a few measurements may be taken as a universal law of muscle, and Platt (1964) recommended measurements across species and conditions that could show where the relation varies and where it holds.

Muscle force and velocity are linked by the cycling of its cross-bridges, and the power that the muscle delivers is the product of the two at the load it bears.
