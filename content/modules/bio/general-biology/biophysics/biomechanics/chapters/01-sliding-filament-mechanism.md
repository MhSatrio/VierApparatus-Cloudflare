Muscle is organised into repeating units called sarcomeres, each bounded by Z-discs and containing thick filaments of myosin and thin filaments of actin. During contraction the sarcomere shortens, and the thin filaments are drawn towards the centre of the sarcomere along the thick ones. Huxley and Niedergerke (1954) showed this directly in living muscle fibres, using interference microscopy to measure the lengths of the bands, and they found that the length of the thick filaments and of the overlapping regions did not change, while the distance between the Z-discs shortened. The filaments slide rather than shorten.

The observation distinguished the sliding filament mechanism from alternatives in which the filaments themselves contract. The bands that Huxley and Niedergerke measured reported the positions of the filaments, and their constancy during shortening showed that the filaments kept their lengths. The result was a decisive test: a model in which the filaments shorten would have changed the band lengths, and the measurement excluded it.

The sliding is generated by the cross-bridges between the filaments. Myosin heads project from the thick filament and bind to sites on the thin filament, where they pivot to pull the thin filament along, release, and bind again further along, in a cycle driven by the hydrolysis of ATP. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the myosin motor and the cycle of its heads, and the sliding is the summed action of many such cycles across the overlapping filaments.

The force of contraction depends on the number of cross-bridges that are attached at any moment, and this in turn depends on the overlap between the filaments. At full overlap many heads can attach, while at the extremes of overlap few can, and the relation between length and force reflects this geometry. The calcium that switches on the cycle, as described in the cardiac muscle chapter of the cardiovascular topic, controls the number of sites available.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a mechanism inferred from a few measurements may be accepted as established, and the sliding filament mechanism was supported by many independent observations of the length relations and the structure. Platt (1964) recommended the test that distinguished it from its alternatives, which the constancy of the band lengths provided.

The sliding filament mechanism explains how muscle shortens by the sliding of filaments that keep their lengths, and the measurement that established it is a model of how a decisive observation separates competing mechanisms.
