# Neural Biophysics

The nervous system computes with electricity. Its neurons receive thousands of synaptic inputs on branching dendrites, integrate them in the cell body and axon, and send spikes along long axons to other neurons, and the physics of this process determines what the nervous system can do. The Electrophysiology topic described how the electrical activity of cells is recorded, and the Neuroscience module described the cells and their connections. Neural biophysics builds models of how the neuron and the network compute, and it tests those models against the recordings.

The chapters begin with the cable theory that describes the passive spread of voltage along dendrites and axons, and with the active properties that amplify and shape signals. The integration of synaptic inputs, which determines whether a neuron fires, follows, together with the spiking models that describe its firing, from the Hodgkin and Huxley equations to simpler integrate-and-fire forms. Networks of neurons, the computation they perform and the way plasticity such as the long-term potentiation of Bliss and Lømo (1973) and the noise of the system shape their behaviour, are then treated. The final chapters address the models of memory and the reporting of neural models.

Neural models are attractive because they can compute, and an artificial network that performs a task may be taken as a model of the brain. Ioannidis (2005) described how a model that fits a task may be accepted as a description of the biology without the test of its predictions, and Platt (1964) recommended comparisons that could show the model to differ from the nervous system in a testable way.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular and molecular components of neurons whose physics this topic describes.
