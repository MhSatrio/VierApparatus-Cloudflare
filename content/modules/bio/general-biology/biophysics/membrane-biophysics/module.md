# Membrane Biophysics

Every cell is enclosed by a membrane, and many organelles are bounded by membranes too. The membrane is a thin sheet of lipids in which proteins are embedded, and its physical properties determine how the cell separates its interior from the outside, how it exchanges material and how it generates the electrical signals of excitable cells. Singer and Nicolson (1972) proposed the fluid mosaic model, which describes the membrane as a fluid lipid bilayer in which proteins move, and the model remains the basis for the physics of membranes.

The chapters begin with the bilayer itself, its fluidity and the forces that hold its lipids together. The fluid mosaic model and the mobility of membrane proteins follow, together with the mechanical properties and the curvature that membranes adopt. The permeability of the bilayer to ions and molecules is then described, and the final chapters address the membrane proteins that create the transport and electrical properties on which the cell depends, with the Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) description of excitable membranes as an example.

Membrane biophysics is a field in which models are easily proposed and difficult to test, because the membrane is complex and its components interact. Ioannidis (2005) described how a model that fits one membrane may not describe another, and Platt (1964) recommended measurements that could distinguish between models of the same membrane property.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the lipid composition and the membrane proteins of the cell, and the physical model must be checked against these components.
