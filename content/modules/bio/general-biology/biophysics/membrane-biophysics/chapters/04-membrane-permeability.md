A small uncharged molecule crosses a membrane by dissolving in the hydrophobic core of the bilayer and diffusing across it, and its permeability depends on two properties: how readily it partitions from water into the lipid and how fast it diffuses through the core. The partition coefficient measures the first, and the diffusion coefficient in the core the second. The permeability is the product of the two divided by the membrane thickness, which makes thin membranes more permeable than thick ones. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the lipid bilayer as a barrier to the passage of water-soluble molecules, and the principle of partition and diffusion explains why nonpolar molecules pass readily while polar ones do not.

Ions cross the bilayer very poorly. An ion carries a charge that is strongly disfavoured in the hydrophobic core, so that the energy cost of moving it into the membrane is large, and the rate of crossing is many orders of magnitude slower than for uncharged molecules of similar size. Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) found that the ion currents of the nerve membrane depended on specific channels, which is the reason the membrane can be selective and excitable: the bilayer alone would not allow the currents they measured.

The permeability of a membrane is measured in model systems and in cells. In model membranes, vesicles loaded with a marker are placed in a solution, and the rate at which the marker leaves or enters is followed. In cells, the uptake of a labelled molecule and its efflux are measured under defined conditions. The measurements depend on the thickness, the composition and the temperature of the membrane, and the conditions must be controlled.

Permeability has a biological consequence. Drugs must cross membranes to reach their targets, and the permeability of a molecule determines how it is absorbed and distributed, as the pharmacology modules described. The physical principle of partition and diffusion is therefore the basis for predicting the access of molecules to the interior of the cell.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a permeability estimated in a model membrane may not describe the same molecule in a cell, where the membrane composition and the presence of transporters differ, and Platt (1964) recommended measurements that could separate passive permeation from transporter-mediated uptake.

The bilayer lets small nonpolar molecules through and blocks charged ones, and the cell's channels and transporters exist to pass what the bilayer excludes.
