Singer and Nicolson (1972) proposed that membrane proteins are dispersed in a fluid lipid bilayer and can move laterally within it, and the model gave the membrane its name. The proteins were pictured as mosaic tiles floating in a sea of lipid, free to diffuse and to associate with one another. The idea explained how membrane proteins could interact and how the membrane could change its composition, and it has been developed in the decades since to take account of the constraints that cells impose on the motion.

The mobility of proteins is measured by fluorescence recovery after photobleaching, in which a region of the membrane is illuminated to destroy the fluorescence of labelled proteins and the return of fluorescence from unbleached molecules is followed. The rate of recovery gives the diffusion coefficient, and the fraction that recovers gives the mobile fraction. Many proteins diffuse freely in artificial membranes, but in cells a substantial fraction is immobile, and the immobility is the first sign that the fluid model must be qualified.

The cytoskeleton restricts protein mobility. Proteins that are anchored to actin filaments or to the cell cortex are confined to compartments whose boundaries limit their diffusion, and the confinement produces a diffusion that slows at longer times. Other proteins are restricted by binding to one another or by the lipid domains they prefer, and the restrictions form a mosaic of different mobilities across the membrane.

The consequences for signalling are substantial. A receptor that is confined may be unable to meet its partner unless the confinement brings them together, and a confined arrangement can act as a platform that concentrates the components of a signal. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the membrane signalling complexes that depend on such arrangements, and the mobility of their components determines how quickly the complexes form.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a model that is developed in artificial membranes may fail to describe the restricted proteins of cells, and Platt (1964) recommended tests that discriminate between free diffusion and confinement, such as the response of the diffusion to disruption of the cytoskeleton.

The fluid mosaic model is the starting point of membrane biology, and its qualification by the cell's constraints is where much of the current understanding lies.
