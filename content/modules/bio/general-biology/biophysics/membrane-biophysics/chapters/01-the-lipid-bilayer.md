A phospholipid has a polar head group and two nonpolar hydrocarbon tails, and in water it arranges itself so that the tails are sequestered from the solvent. The driving force is the hydrophobic effect described in the thermodynamics topic: the release of ordered water from around the tails increases the entropy of the solvent enough to favour the assembly. The lipids form a bilayer, with the heads facing the water on each side and the tails packed together in the middle. Singer and Nicolson (1972) described the bilayer as the framework of the membrane, and its self-assembly is the reason membranes form spontaneously from lipid mixtures.

The bilayer is a fluid. The lipids move laterally within each leaflet, rotate about their axes and flex their tails, and the rate of these motions depends on the temperature and the composition. The core of the bilayer is a hydrocarbon region about four nanometres thick, and the fluidity of this region determines how readily molecules cross it. Anfinsen (1973) showed that a structure formed by many weak interactions is defined by their balance, a principle the bilayer illustrates in its packing.

Lipids exhibit phase behaviour. Lipids with saturated tails pack closely and form an ordered gel phase at low temperature, and they melt into the fluid phase above a transition temperature. Unsaturated tails, with their kinks, prevent close packing and keep the membrane fluid at lower temperatures. The mixture of lipids in a membrane can separate into domains of different order, and the domains are thought to be important for the organisation of membrane proteins, although the existence and size of such domains in living cells are debated.

The two leaflets of the bilayer differ in composition, and the asymmetry is maintained by enzymes that move lipids between them. The asymmetry affects the surface charge, the curvature and the signals that the membrane presents to the cell, and its loss is a sign of cell death in some pathways.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the domains observed in artificial membranes may not reflect the domains in living cells, and Platt (1964) recommended measurements in cells that could distinguish between the domain models.

The bilayer is a self-assembled fluid whose properties follow from the balance of the hydrophobic effect and the thermal motion of its lipids.
