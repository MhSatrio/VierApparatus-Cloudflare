Many receptors span the membrane, and a signal from outside is transmitted across the bilayer by a conformational change that alters the activity of a domain on the inside. The change is often a rotation or a tilt of a transmembrane helix, and its effect depends on the lipid environment in which the helices lie. Singer and Nicolson (1972) envisaged membrane proteins diffusing freely, but signalling depends on the arrangement of receptors, and the membrane is not a uniform fluid for the purposes of signalling. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the receptor families that transmit signals in this way.

The cytoskeleton organises the membrane from beneath. Actin filaments and their associated proteins form a meshwork close to the inner surface, and transmembrane proteins are anchored to it, which confines their diffusion. The meshwork creates compartments within which receptors can cluster, and the clustering increases the local concentration of signalling components and the probability that they meet. The organisation therefore converts a random diffusion into a spatially structured signal.

Clustering affects the strength of the response. A receptor that is clustered can respond more strongly to a low concentration of ligand, because the binding of one ligand promotes the activation of its neighbours. The cooperativity of this process is a physical consequence of the arrangement, and it can be measured by the dependence of the response on the density of receptors.

The physical organisation can also limit signalling. A receptor confined in a compartment may be unable to reach its partner, and the signal is attenuated when the compartment is disrupted or when the receptor is removed from the cluster. The specificity of signalling can therefore depend on which receptors are present in which compartments, and not only on their molecular identity.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the apparent clustering of receptors in images may reflect the labelling method rather than the biology, and Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the physical clustering from the chemical signal, such as the disruption of the cytoskeleton and the observation of whether the response changes as predicted.

The membrane is a structured surface whose organisation shapes the signals that cross it, and the physics of confinement and clustering is part of the biology of signalling.
