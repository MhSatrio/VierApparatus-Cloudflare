Membrane properties are measured by a set of methods, each suited to one property. Voltage clamp holds the membrane potential at a chosen value and records the current that flows, and it is the method by which Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) separated the ionic currents of the nerve membrane. Patch clamp records the current through a small patch of membrane, or through a single channel, and it extends the measurement to individual molecules. Fluorescence recovery reports the mobility of proteins, as described in an earlier chapter, and the mechanical properties of vesicles are measured by observing their shapes and fluctuations under defined forces.

Each method has its own sources of error. Voltage clamp depends on the adequacy of the clamp in controlling the whole cell, and the currents from distant parts of a large cell may not be controlled. Patch clamp depends on the seal between the pipette and the membrane, and a leaky seal adds a current that is not from the channels. Fluorescence recovery depends on the photobleaching being uniform and on the labelling not disturbing the protein. The controls for these errors are part of every measurement.

Imaging membranes and their domains is a further source of interpretation. A labelled probe may bind preferentially to one lipid phase, the labelling may aggregate the proteins it marks, and the resolution of the microscope may not separate structures that are close together. Ioannidis (2005) described how an image of apparent domains can be a product of the labelling and the analysis, and the controls must show that the features are not artefacts.

The comparison of measurements between laboratories requires full reporting. The composition of the membrane, the temperature, the buffer and the calibration of the instrument must be stated, and the raw data should be available. Platt (1964) recommended that the controls be designed to show a measurement to be wrong, and the inclusion of a known standard in each series is such a control.

The properties of a membrane are measured by methods that each reveal one aspect, and the reliability of the picture depends on the controls that separate the property from the artefact.
