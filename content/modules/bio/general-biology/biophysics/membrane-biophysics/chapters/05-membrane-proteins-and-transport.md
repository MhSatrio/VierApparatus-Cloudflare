Membrane proteins fold within the bilayer, and their hydrophobic surfaces are matched to the lipid core while their polar regions are placed where they can interact with water or with the head groups. Singer and Nicolson (1972) described proteins as dispersed in the membrane, and the matching of protein surface to lipid determines whether a protein inserts and how it is oriented. Anfinsen (1973) showed that proteins fold into defined structures, and the transmembrane regions of membrane proteins fold into bundles of helices or barrels whose hydrophobic surfaces face the lipids.

A transporter moves a molecule across the membrane by binding it on one side, changing its conformation so that the binding site opens on the other side and releasing it there. The alternating access mechanism explains how the transporter avoids opening a continuous pore, and the conformational change is the energy-consuming step in active transport. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the classes of transporter, and the conformational cycle is the common feature of the carriers that move sugars and amino acids.

Active transport moves a molecule against its gradient, and the energy comes from the hydrolysis of ATP or from a coupled downhill flow of another ion. A pump that hydrolyses ATP uses the energy to drive its conformational cycle, so that the ion is moved against its electrochemical gradient. A secondary transporter uses the downhill movement of one ion, such as the sodium that flows into the cell, to carry a second molecule uphill, and the stoichiometry of the coupling sets the maximum gradient that can be achieved.

The energetics of coupling can be calculated from the free energies of the gradients, and the calculation requires that the stoichiometry and the gradients be measured. The efficiency of the transporter depends on how completely its cycle is coupled, and a leak of the coupling ion reduces it.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the stoichiometry of a transporter may be assumed rather than measured, and Platt (1964) recommended measurements that could test the coupling, such as the dependence of transport on the concentration of the coupling ion.

Membrane proteins are folded to fit the lipid, and the transporters among them use conformational cycles to move molecules against their gradients at a cost of energy.
