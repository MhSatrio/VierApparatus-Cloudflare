A membrane is a thin elastic sheet, and its resistance to bending and stretching determines how it can be shaped. The bending stiffness of a bilayer is of the order of ten to twenty times the thermal energy, which is enough to resist the curvature that a cell generates by its proteins but not so large that the membrane cannot be reshaped. The tension of the membrane, which is set by the cell's surface area and its volume, opposes further stretching. Singer and Nicolson (1972) described the membrane as a fluid structure, and its mechanics extends the fluid picture to the forces that deform it.

Lipids and proteins have a preferred curvature. Some lipids, with cone-shaped geometries, favour a membrane that curves towards the side on which their head groups lie, and some membrane proteins have wedge-shaped domains or scaffolding structures that impose a curvature. When the spontaneous curvature of the components matches the shape the membrane must adopt, the energy cost of the deformation is low, and the membrane bends easily. The curvature shapes the organelles and the vesicles that the cell uses for trafficking.

Membrane budding, fusion and fission are the processes by which vesicles form, merge and separate. Each requires the membrane to pass through highly curved intermediates, which cost energy, and the cell supplies the energy by the assembly of proteins that pull the membrane into shape or by the hydrolysis of nucleotides. Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) worked with a membrane whose electrical properties were the subject of their analysis, and the mechanical properties of the same membrane are the other side of its function.

The energetics of these processes can be estimated from the bending energies and the spontaneous curvatures, and the estimate can be compared with the measured energies of the events. The comparison tests whether the mechanics captures what the cell does.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a model of membrane bending that uses assumed curvatures and stiffnesses may give a result that depends on those assumptions, and Platt (1964) recommended measurements of the bending stiffness and of the energy of a trafficking event that could test the model.

The membrane is an elastic and curved structure, and its shape and its trafficking follow from the mechanics of its lipids and proteins.
