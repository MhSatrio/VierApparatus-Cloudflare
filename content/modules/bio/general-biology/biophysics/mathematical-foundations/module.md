# Mathematical Foundations

Biology is increasingly quantitative. The processes described throughout this curriculum, from enzyme kinetics and membrane potentials to population growth and gene expression, are changes in time and variation across individuals, and they are described with mathematics. This topic supplies the tools that the rest of the biophysics and quantitative sections use: how to write a rate of change, how to solve the equations that result, how to treat variation as probability and how to decide whether an observed effect is larger than chance.

The chapters begin with rates and differential equations, which describe continuous change, and with the exponential and logistic growth they produce. Probability and the variation of biological measurements follow, then the estimation of effects and their uncertainties and the testing of hypotheses with their errors. Linear systems and the modelling of biological processes come next, and the final chapter addresses the limits of models and the discipline of testing them. Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) showed how a set of differential equations, fitted to measurements, could describe the nerve impulse, and the same approach runs through the whole topic.

Mathematics makes assumptions visible, which is one of its strengths, and it also allows a model to fit data it was built to fit. Ioannidis (2005) described how the flexibility of statistical analysis can produce apparent results, and Platt (1964) recommended mathematical predictions that could fail. The chapters emphasise the prediction tested against new data as the proof of a model.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biological processes whose rates and variation these tools describe.
