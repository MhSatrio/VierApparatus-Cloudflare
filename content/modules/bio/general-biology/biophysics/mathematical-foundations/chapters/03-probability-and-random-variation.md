Biological measurements vary, and the variation has two sources. Individuals differ, because each has its own genetic makeup, history and environment, and each measurement also contains error from the method. Probability distributions describe both. For counts of discrete events, such as the number of molecules that react in a short interval or the number of cells that divide in a culture, the binomial and Poisson distributions give the probability of each count. The Poisson distribution has the property that its variance equals its mean, which is a useful check on whether a count behaves as a random process.

Continuous measurements, such as a concentration or a length, are often described by the normal distribution, which is symmetrical about its mean and is defined by its mean and standard deviation. The central limit theorem explains why the normal distribution appears so often: the average of many independent contributions tends towards normality whatever the distribution of the individual contributions. This is why the mean of a sample is usually normally distributed even when the individual values are not.

The distinction between variation and error matters. The variation between individuals is a property of the population, and it does not diminish with more measurements of the same individual. The error of a measurement is a property of the method, and repeated measurements of the same sample average it out. A study that reports the variation as if it were the error, or the error as if it were the variation, will draw the wrong conclusion about how precisely the population mean is known.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the variability of biological measures can be underestimated in small studies, which produces results that appear more precise than they are. Platt (1964) recommended comparing the alternative explanations of a variation, such as the effect of a treatment against the chance distribution of the outcome.

Gillespie (1977) showed that the random occurrence of chemical reactions in small volumes gives rise to fluctuations that the deterministic description averages away, and the probability distributions are needed to describe a system where the number of molecules is small.

Probability describes the randomness in biology, and distinguishing its sources is the first step in judging whether a difference is real.
