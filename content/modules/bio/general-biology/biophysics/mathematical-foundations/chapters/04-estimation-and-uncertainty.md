An estimate is a value calculated from a sample that is taken as the best guess of a parameter of the population, such as its mean, its rate or its effect size. The estimate is never exact, because a different sample would give a different value. The uncertainty of the estimate is expressed by the standard error, which is the standard deviation of the estimate across repeated samples, and by the confidence interval, the range that would contain the true value in a stated proportion of such repetitions, usually ninety-five per cent.

The standard error falls as the sample size rises, roughly in proportion to the square root of the number of observations. A study with four times the data halves the standard error and halves the width of the interval. The consequence is that precision is costly: doubling the precision requires four times the data, and the sample size must be chosen in advance so that the interval will be narrow enough to answer the question.

The meaning of a confidence interval is often misunderstood. It is not a probability that the true value lies within the interval for this particular study. It describes the long-run behaviour of the procedure: if the study were repeated many times, the stated proportion of the intervals would contain the true value. For a single study, the interval is a summary of the uncertainty that the data support, given the assumptions of the model.

Ioannidis (2005) described how small studies produce wide intervals that are often reported as precise estimates, and how a reported effect from a small study tends to be larger than the true effect. The width of the interval is the honest measure of what a study shows, and it should be reported beside every estimate.

Platt (1964) recommended stating a claim so that it can be tested, and an interval gives the range of values that the data cannot exclude. A claim that a rate is positive is stronger when the interval excludes zero by a wide margin than when it barely does.

An estimate without its uncertainty is an incomplete statement, and the interval is what makes the estimate usable.
