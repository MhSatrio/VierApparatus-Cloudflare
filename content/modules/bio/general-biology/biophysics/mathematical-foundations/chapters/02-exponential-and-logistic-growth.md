If the rate at which a population grows is proportional to its size, the population grows exponentially. Each individual produces a fixed number of new individuals per unit time, so the number of new individuals increases with the population itself. The solution is an exponential function of time, and the doubling time, the interval over which the population doubles, is constant. The Microbial Cultivation topic used this model for the exponential phase of a bacterial culture, where the nutrients are abundant and each cell divides at a steady rate.

Exponential growth cannot continue indefinitely. Resources are finite, and the rate of growth falls as the population approaches the level the environment can support. The logistic equation adds this constraint by making the rate proportional to the population multiplied by the fraction of the capacity that remains. The solution is an S-shaped curve that rises exponentially at first, slows as it approaches the carrying capacity and levels off there. The carrying capacity is a parameter, and it is estimated from the data rather than assumed.

The two models describe different conditions. In the early phase of growth in a rich medium, the exponential model fits well, and the logistic model reduces to it. Near the plateau, the logistic model captures the slowing that the exponential model cannot. Fitting both to the same data and comparing the fits is a way of testing whether the constraint is needed.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a model with an extra parameter can fit data better simply because it has more freedom, and the comparison must penalise the extra complexity. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the alternatives, and the prediction of the logistic model for the plateau, tested in a new experiment, discriminates it from a model in which growth continues.

Growth models are simple descriptions of a population's response to its resources, and their parameters summarise the conditions under which the population was measured.
