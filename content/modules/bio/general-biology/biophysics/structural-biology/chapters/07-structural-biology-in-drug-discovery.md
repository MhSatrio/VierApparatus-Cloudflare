Structural biology is a central tool of drug discovery. A co-crystal structure shows a candidate ligand bound in the active site or pocket of its target, and it reveals which groups of the ligand make which contacts. Medicinal chemists use this information to modify the ligand so that the contacts are improved or new ones are formed. The approach follows the logic of the first structures: the shape of a binding pocket, seen in the structure, guides the design of a molecule that fits it.

Fragment-based discovery builds ligands from small molecules that bind weakly to a target. The fragments are identified by their binding in a screen, and their structures in complex with the target show where they sit. Fragments that bind in adjacent sites can then be linked into a larger molecule with much higher affinity. The method exploits the observation that binding energy is distributed across a ligand, and that small pieces can be combined.

Predictions of affinity from structure are less reliable than the structures that motivate them. The computed binding energy depends on the model of the water, the protonation of the groups and the flexibility of the target, none of which the static structure fully determines. A ligand that fits the pocket in a model may bind weakly in practice because of an unfavourable desolvation or an inaccessible conformation.

The evidence that a designed compound acts on its intended target requires more than its structure in complex with the target. The compound must show the predicted activity in a functional assay, its selectivity against related targets must be measured, and its effect in cells or tissues must be shown to depend on the target. Ioannidis (2005) described how a structure that suggests a mechanism may be accepted before the activity and selectivity are established, and Platt (1964) recommended tests that could show the compound to act through another route.

Each step of structure-based design must be validated by experiment, since the structure is a model whose predictions guide the choice of compound. The consistency of the predictions with the measured activities is the test of the model, and the failures are the means of improving it.

Structure-based drug design uses the three-dimensional arrangement of a target to guide the making of compounds, and its success is measured by the activity and selectivity that the compounds show.
