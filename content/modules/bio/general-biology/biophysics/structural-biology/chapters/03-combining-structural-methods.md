No single method determines the structure of every molecule. Crystallography requires crystals, which large and flexible assemblies often do not form, and it captures the molecule in the crystal lattice rather than in solution. Electron microscopy images molecules in a frozen film and reaches the resolution needed for large complexes, but it struggles with small or heterogeneous particles. Nuclear magnetic resonance gives distances and dynamics in solution, but it is limited to smaller molecules. Integrative modelling combines the data from these methods, and from chemical cross-linking and mass spectrometry, into a model of the whole assembly.

Bai et al. (2015) described the rapid rise of cryo-electron microscopy as a method for large assemblies, and its strength is complementary to that of the other methods. A low-resolution map of a complex can be combined with the high-resolution structures of its parts, obtained by crystallography, to place each part in the whole, and the cross-links identify which parts are adjacent. The combination gives a model that no single method could produce.

Data from different sources have different reliability, and the model must weight them accordingly. A distance from an NMR measurement is precise, while a cross-link is a noisy indication of proximity, and a low-resolution density constrains the overall shape but not the details. The weights determine the influence of each datum on the model, and they must be stated, since a different choice of weights can produce a different model.

The combination carries a risk. When sources disagree, the model may satisfy both by compromise, and the disagreement is hidden rather than revealed. A model that fits every datum at a moderate level may be less informative than one that fits most data well and identifies the discrepant ones. The disagreements must be reported, since they often mark the places where the model is uncertain or where the structure differs between conditions.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that combines many sources can appear more convincing because of the number of sources, although the sources may share a common assumption, and Platt (1964) recommended a test that could reveal the disagreement, such as the fit of the model to a datum withheld from its construction.

Combining methods builds models of assemblies that no single method could resolve, and the honest model reports where its sources disagree.
