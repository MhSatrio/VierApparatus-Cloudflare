Structures are deposited in public archives so that they can be shared, compared and checked. A deposited entry contains the atomic coordinates, and it should also contain the experimental data, the statistics of the refinement and the validation report. The coordinates alone are not sufficient to judge the model, because the quality of the structure depends on the data it was built from and the resolution at which it was determined. Brünger (1992) introduced the free residual that the validation reports carry, and an entry without these measures is hard to evaluate.

Standards define what an entry must contain. They specify the format of the coordinates, the annotation of the molecule and its ligands, and the method and conditions of the measurement. The standards allow entries to be read by software and compared across methods, and they make it possible to search the archive for structures with particular features. A structure whose conditions are not recorded cannot be placed in context.

Archives are used to compare structures. A new structure of a protein is compared with others of the same family to identify conserved features, and the comparison reveals variation in the fold and in the active site. The comparison is only meaningful when the entries are of comparable quality, and a comparison that includes low-resolution or poorly validated entries will give misleading differences.

Users must judge each entry. The resolution, the free residual, the geometry statistics and the number of observations all indicate the reliability of a structure, and the functional conclusions drawn from it should match the reliability. A structure at low resolution cannot support conclusions about the orientation of individual side chains, however often it is cited.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the accumulation of entries can give an impression of consensus when many structures share a common error, and Platt (1964) recommended that the comparison include independent determinations, which reveals whether a feature is real or an artefact of the method.

The archive is a shared record of models, and its value depends on depositors providing the data needed to judge them and users reading the quality of each entry.
