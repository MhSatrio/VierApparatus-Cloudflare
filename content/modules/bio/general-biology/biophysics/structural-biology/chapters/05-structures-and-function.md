A structure is valuable when it explains how a molecule works. Kendrew et al. (1958) showed that the heme of myoglobin sits in a pocket whose architecture accommodates oxygen, and the structure explained the storage function without any further experiment. Such explanations are the purpose of structural biology, and they lead to predictions about which residues are essential and how the molecule should respond to changes in its environment.

Functional sites are identified from the structure by their location, their chemistry and their accessibility. A cleft lined by residues that could bind a substrate is a candidate active site, and residues whose side chains point into it are candidates for catalysis. The candidates are then tested by mutating them and measuring the function, as the structure-function chapter of the molecular biophysics topic described. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the activity depends on the fold, and the identification of the site depends on the fold being correctly determined.

A static structure may not show the functional state. An enzyme may be captured in an inactive form in a crystal, and the active form, which closes around its substrate, may differ substantially. The structure then explains the inactive state, and the mechanism of activation is inferred rather than observed. Multiple structures in different states, and the combination with dynamic measurements, are needed to describe the cycle of function.

Structures guide the design of mutations and drugs. A mutation that is predicted to disrupt a salt bridge can be tested for its effect on stability and function, and a drug designed to fit a pocket can be tested for its binding. The predictions carry the uncertainty of the model, and their test is the check that the structure is useful for the function.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a functional mechanism proposed from a structure may be presented as established before the experiments that test it, and Platt (1964) recommended the test that distinguishes the proposed mechanism from the alternatives, such as the mutation that removes the proposed interaction and abolishes the function.

A structure explains function when the explanation is tested, and the distinction between what the structure shows and what is inferred from it is essential.
