# Structural Biology

Function in biology is almost always a consequence of structure. An enzyme catalyses a reaction because its active site has a particular shape, a receptor recognises its ligand because its binding pocket fits it, and a machine moves because its parts are arranged in a particular way. Structural biology determines these arrangements. The Molecular Biophysics and Protein Folding topics explained the forces that shape a structure. This topic describes the experimental methods that measure it and the reasoning by which a measurement becomes a model.

The chapters follow the discipline from the first structure to the modern complexes. Kendrew et al. (1958) determined the structure of myoglobin by X-ray crystallography, and the principles of that work are described first. X-ray crystallography, cryo-electron microscopy and nuclear magnetic resonance are then treated as the three main methods, each with its own sample requirements and limits. The final chapters address the combination of methods, the validation of models and the use of structures to explain function.

Structural determination is inference from indirect data, and the model is only as reliable as the data and the validation behind it. Ioannidis (2005) described how a model can be accepted without the checks that its uncertainty requires, and Platt (1964) recommended testing a structure against independent predictions. Watson and Crick (1953) showed how a structure can be inferred from diffraction data together with the chemistry of the molecule, and the same combination of data and constraint is used throughout the field.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the macromolecular machines whose structures are determined, and the structure must be read in the cellular context of the machine it describes.
