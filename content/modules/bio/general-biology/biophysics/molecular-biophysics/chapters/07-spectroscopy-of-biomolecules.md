Biomolecules absorb and emit light in ways that depend on their structure, and spectroscopy reads these dependences without the need to crystallise the molecule. Circular dichroism measures the difference in absorption of left- and right-handed polarised light, which is sensitive to the secondary structure of a protein, with characteristic signals for the helix and the sheet. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the folded state is a defined structure, and the circular dichroism spectrum changes when the protein unfolds, which allows the stability of the fold to be followed.

Fluorescence is sensitive to the environment of an aromatic residue or of an attached probe. Tryptophan emits at a wavelength that shifts when it moves from a buried, nonpolar position to an exposed, polar one, and the shift reports the change in the protein's structure. The fluorescence is also quenched by nearby groups, and the quenching gives information about distances. Watson and Crick (1953) recognised that the stacking of bases in the double helix changes the absorbance of nucleic acids, and the same principle of the environment shaping the spectrum applies to fluorophores.

Spectra report averages over all the molecules in the sample and over the time of the measurement. A signal is a property of the population, and a change in the signal may arise from a change in the fraction of molecules in one state, from a change in the structure of each molecule or from both. The interpretation of a spectrum therefore depends on assumptions about the states present, and a single spectrum rarely determines the structure uniquely.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a spectrum that is assigned to a structure by comparison with reference spectra may be assigned wrongly when the reference set is incomplete, and Platt (1964) recommended measurements at several conditions that discriminate between alternative assignments.

Spectroscopy reads the environment and the conformation of biomolecules through their interaction with light, and its interpretation is limited by the averaging over the population.
