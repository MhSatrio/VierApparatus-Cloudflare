Molecular recognition is the specific binding of one molecule to another, and it underlies enzyme catalysis, signalling, the replication of DNA and the action of drugs. Watson and Crick (1953) described recognition by complementary shape and chemistry in the base pairs, and the same principle, extended to proteins, governs most interactions. The surfaces of the two molecules fit together through contacts that are individually weak, and the specificity of the binding comes from the number and the arrangement of these contacts.

Two models describe how binding occurs. In induced fit, the first molecule binds loosely and the binding induces a change in the shape of the second that improves the contacts. In conformational selection, the second molecule exists in several shapes, and the ligand preferentially binds one of them, shifting the equilibrium. The two models predict different relations between the binding rate and the concentration and different responses to mutations, and the measurement of the kinetics can distinguish them. Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein samples a range of conformations under physiological conditions, which makes conformational selection a plausible route.

Water is part of the recognition. The interface between two molecules excludes water, and the release of the water from the surfaces contributes to the binding, as the hydrophobic effect described earlier. The water that remains in the interface can bridge contacts, and its arrangement affects the specificity. Changing the solvent changes the binding, and this sensitivity is a sign of the role of water.

The characterisation of a binding event requires three measurements. Affinity is the strength of binding at equilibrium, kinetics are the rates of association and dissociation, and specificity is the preference for the intended partner over others. An affinity alone does not reveal the kinetics, and two binders of the same affinity may differ greatly in how quickly they associate and dissociate, which affects their behaviour in the cell.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a measured affinity may be reported without the conditions that determine it, and Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the models of binding, such as the kinetic dependence on concentration predicted by each.

Molecular recognition is specific because many weak contacts act together, and its characterisation requires the equilibrium, the rates and the selectivity of the binding.
