A structure becomes informative about function when a feature of it is linked to an activity. A pocket in the structure may be proposed as the active site of an enzyme, and a residue lining the pocket may be proposed as the catalytic residue. The proposal is a hypothesis, and it is tested by changing the residue and measuring the activity. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the structure determines the activity, and the link between a feature and an activity is the means of showing which features of the structure matter.

Mutagenesis is the principal test. A residue proposed to be essential is replaced by one that cannot perform its role, and the activity is measured. A loss of activity supports the proposal, but it does not establish it, because the replacement may also disturb the fold, and a loss of activity could come from misfolding rather than from the loss of the proposed chemistry. The mutant must be shown to fold correctly, and the control of a conservative change that preserves the chemistry but not the residue is valuable.

Many functions depend on conformational change, in which the protein moves between states, and a single static structure captures only one of them. An enzyme may close around its substrate, a transporter may switch the opening between the two sides of a membrane and a receptor may change shape on binding. The structure of one state does not describe the transition, and the function must be understood from the ensemble of states and the rates of moving between them.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a structure that suggests a mechanism may be accepted before the mechanism is tested, and Platt (1964) recommended experiments that could distinguish between the proposed mechanisms, such as mutations that favour one conformation and predicted effects on the activity.

Structure is a starting point for understanding function, and its explanatory power depends on testing each proposed link and on recognising the states the structure does not show.
