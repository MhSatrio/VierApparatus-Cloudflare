A structure is not observed directly. Diffraction of X-rays by a crystal produces a pattern of spots whose positions and intensities are related to the arrangement of electrons in the molecule, and the structure is recovered by solving the phase problem and fitting an atomic model to the resulting density. Watson and Crick (1953) inferred the double helix from the diffraction patterns of DNA fibres together with the chemistry of the bases, and the same combination of pattern and chemical constraint remains central to structure determination.

Nuclear magnetic resonance measures the distances between nuclei in solution, through the nuclear Overhauser effect and the couplings between atoms, and the distances are used as constraints on a model. The method gives structures in solution and of flexible molecules, which crystallography may not, but the structures are ensembles of models consistent with the constraints rather than a single determined arrangement. Electron microscopy of frozen molecules, combined with image averaging, now reaches near-atomic resolution for large assemblies, and the reconstruction depends on the alignment and the averaging of many particle images.

The resolution of a structure limits what it can show. At low resolution, the positions of atoms and the identity of side chains are uncertain, and a model built into such a density may be placed in the wrong register. The resolution is reported with the structure, and the claims made about individual residues must be limited to the resolution at which they are determined.

Validation checks whether the model is consistent with the data it was built from, and with the chemistry of the molecule. The fit of the model to the density, the geometry of the bonds and the frequency of unusual conformations are all measured. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the folded structure follows the rules of protein chemistry, and a model that violates those rules is unlikely to be correct.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a model can be refined until it fits the data closely while the data themselves are insufficient to define it, and the validation must include a test on data not used in the refinement. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could show the structure to be wrong, such as a prediction of the effect of a mutation that the structure implies and that the experiment can confirm or refute.

A structure is a model inferred from data, and its usefulness depends on the resolution it achieves and the validation it passes.
