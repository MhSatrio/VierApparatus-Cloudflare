Macromolecules are held together mainly by non-covalent forces, which are much weaker than the covalent bonds that link their atoms into chains. A hydrogen bond, in which a hydrogen atom is shared between an electronegative donor and an acceptor, has an energy of a few kilojoules per mole in water and is directional, which is why the hydrogen bonds of a helix or a base pair are arranged in a precise geometry. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the hydrogen bonding that gives the secondary structures of proteins and the base pairs of nucleic acids, and the arrangement of these bonds determines the shape of the molecule.

Watson and Crick (1953) showed that the complementary pairing of bases depends on hydrogen bonds between specific donors and acceptors, with two bonds for adenine and thymine and three for guanine and cytosine. The difference in the number of bonds explains the greater stability of the guanine and cytosine pairs, and the geometry of the pairing explains why the double helix is specific.

Van der Waals contacts arise from fluctuating charge distributions that induce attractions between nearby atoms, and they are weak individually but add up across the many contacts in a folded protein. Electrostatic interactions between charged groups are stronger in a low-dielectric environment and weaker in water, so their contribution depends on whether the charges are buried or exposed. The hydrophobic effect, described in the thermodynamics topic, is the release of ordered water when nonpolar groups are buried, and it is often the largest contribution to folding.

The sum of these forces is marginal. A folded protein is stable by only a few tens of kilojoules per mole, which is comparable to the thermal energy of the molecule, and this is what allows proteins to unfold and refold, and to bind and release their ligands. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the stability of a fold is a balance of many small contributions, and a single mutation that removes one contact can destabilise the structure.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the contribution of an individual interaction is often inferred from a structure without measuring it, and Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the contributions, such as the effect of mutating one residue on the stability of the fold.

The stability of a biological macromolecule is the sum of many weak attractions that are individually fragile and collectively sufficient.
