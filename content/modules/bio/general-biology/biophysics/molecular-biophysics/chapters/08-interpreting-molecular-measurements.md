Every molecular measurement is an indirect report. A spectrum, a diffraction pattern, a binding curve or a melting profile is converted into a structure, an energy or a rate by a model, and the conversion carries assumptions. The assumptions are often implicit, and the interpretation can be correct only when they hold. Ioannidis (2005) described how the interpretation of a measurement can be accepted without examining the assumptions that connect it to the claimed quantity, and this chapter asks that the assumptions be stated for each measurement.

Averaging over populations is the most common assumption. A measurement made on a sample of molecules reports the average, and the average may reflect a mixture of states that is not present in any single molecule. A protein that spends half its time in each of two conformations gives an average that resembles neither, and the interpretation must consider whether the population is homogeneous. Anfinsen (1973) assumed that a folded protein has a single defined structure, and the assumption fails for proteins with flexible or disordered regions.

The model that converts the signal is a second source of assumption. A two-state model converts a melting curve into a free energy, and a different model would give a different value from the same curve. Platt (1964) recommended the comparison of alternative models with the same data, and an interpretation that does not survive the change of model is weak.

Each measurement has an uncertainty, and the report must state it. The uncertainty of a binding constant, a structure or an energy depends on the signal-to-noise ratio, the number of data points and the model, and it must be carried into any conclusion drawn from the measurement. A value reported without its uncertainty cannot be compared with another value meaningfully.

The report must give the conditions, the model, the fit and the uncertainty, so that another group can check the interpretation. The raw data should be deposited where possible, since the interpretation may change as the models improve.

The molecular measurement is only as strong as the chain of assumptions that connects it to the structure or energy claimed, and that chain must be exposed.
