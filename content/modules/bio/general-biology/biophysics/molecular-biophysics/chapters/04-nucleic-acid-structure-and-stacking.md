The double helix described by Watson and Crick (1953) is held together by two forces. The first is the hydrogen bonding between paired bases, which gives the specificity of the pairing. The second, often the larger in magnitude, is the stacking of the flat base pairs on one another, in which the aromatic rings interact through van der Waals contacts and the electron clouds of the rings. Stacking is strongest between adjacent purines and is sensitive to the sequence, and it contributes much of the stability of the helix in water, where the bases would otherwise be exposed to the solvent.

The sugar-phosphate backbone carries the negative charges that are screened as described in the earlier chapter, and its conformation determines the form of the helix. The sugar pucker and the rotation about the backbone bonds give the B form of DNA, the more compact A form of dehydrated DNA and the form that RNA adopts. Alberts et al. (2022) describe these structures and their functional roles, and each form is a different balance of the same forces.

The sequence changes the stability of a duplex. A pair of guanine and cytosine contributes three hydrogen bonds and stacks strongly, so a duplex rich in guanine and cytosine melts at a higher temperature than one rich in adenine and thymine. The melting temperature is therefore a function of the composition and of the length, and it is a basic parameter for the design of primers, as the PCR topics described.

Melting is measured by following a signal that changes when the duplex separates, such as the absorbance at 260 nanometres, which rises as the stacking is lost. The transition is sharp for a long duplex and broad for a short one, and the midpoint of the transition defines the melting temperature. The measurement depends on the salt concentration, which screens the backbone charges, and the conditions must be stated with the result.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a melting temperature calculated from a formula may differ from the measured value, because the formula assumes conditions that do not hold, and Platt (1964) recommended comparing the measured melting with the prediction, which tests whether the model of stacking captures the sequence dependence.

The stability of nucleic acid structure is the sum of a specific pairing and a non-specific stacking, and the sequence determines how much of each the duplex has.
