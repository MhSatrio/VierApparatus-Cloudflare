Nucleic acids and many proteins carry many charged groups, and the electrostatic repulsion between like charges would prevent the molecules from folding or from binding one another if it were not moderated. Water is a polar solvent with a high dielectric constant, which weakens the interaction between two charges by a large factor, and the ions dissolved in the water further screen the charges. Watson and Crick (1953) placed the negatively charged phosphate groups on the outside of the double helix, where the surrounding water and counter-ions reduce their mutual repulsion, and the stability of the helix depends on this screening.

The screening is described by the Debye length, the distance over which an electrostatic potential falls by a factor of e in a salt solution. It decreases as the ionic strength increases, so that at the salt concentrations typical of the cell the range of electrostatic interactions is only a few nanometres. This short range means that charges that are far apart along a molecule interact weakly, while charges that are close interact strongly, and the geometry of the charges determines the binding.

The binding of a positively charged protein to a negatively charged nucleic acid is driven partly by electrostatics, and the counter-ions released from both molecules when they bind contribute an entropy gain. The dependence of the binding on salt concentration is therefore a signature of the electrostatic contribution, and the measurement of binding at several salt concentrations separates the electrostatic term from the specific contacts.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the ionic environment of the cell, and the cytoplasm contains a salt concentration and a range of counter-ions that set the screening that the molecules experience. The screening in a living cell differs from that in a simple buffer, and the buffer must be chosen to match the question.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a calculated electrostatic energy can depend strongly on the dielectric constant and the charge model assumed, and Platt (1964) recommended a comparison that could test the calculation, such as the change in binding when the salt concentration is varied as the model predicts.

Electrostatic interactions in water are short-range and salt-sensitive, and their effects on binding are measured by varying the ionic conditions.
