# Molecular Biophysics

Biological function rests on the physics of molecules. A protein folds, binds a ligand, catalyses a reaction or changes shape because of the forces between its atoms and between it and the water around it, and a nucleic acid carries information because of the hydrogen bonds that pair its bases. Molecular biophysics is the discipline that describes these forces, the structures they produce and the methods by which the structures are determined. It provides the physical basis for the biochemistry of the earlier modules.

The chapters begin with the non-covalent forces, including hydrogen bonds, electrostatic interactions, van der Waals contacts and the hydrophobic effect, which together stabilise macromolecular structure. The electrostatics of charged molecules in water follow, with the nucleic acid structure that Watson and Crick (1953) established as a worked example. The experimental determination of structure by diffraction and spectroscopy comes next, followed by the dynamics that Karplus and McCammon (1983) described, and the final chapters address the link between structure and function and the uncertainty of structural claims.

The determination of a structure is an inference from a diffraction or spectroscopic pattern, and the structure is a model that fits the pattern. Ioannidis (2005) described how a model that fits the data can be accepted without checking its uncertainty, and Platt (1964) recommended testing the structure against a prediction, such as the effect of a mutation on the binding that the structure implies.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular macromolecules whose structures this topic examines, and each structure must be read in the context of the cell in which it functions.
