Much of twentieth-century biology was reductionist. Genes were identified, proteins were purified, and each part was characterised in isolation, and this work revealed the molecules from which living systems are built. Kitano (2002) noted that knowing the parts does not, by itself, explain how the system behaves. The response of a cell to a signal depends on feedback, on the timing of its reactions and on the concentrations of many molecules at once, and these properties belong to the network in which the parts are connected.

An emergent property is a behaviour of the whole that is not evident from the behaviour of any part. A cell that switches between two states, a tissue that maintains a constant temperature or a population of neurons that generates a rhythm all show emergent properties. The switch in a cell may be produced by two proteins that activate each other, and neither protein alone switches. Alberts et al. (2022) describe many such networks in the molecular biology of the cell, and their examples show why a list of components is an incomplete description.

Systems thinking therefore asks a different question. Instead of asking what a component does, it asks how the components are connected, what the connections do to signals that pass through them, and how the whole responds when one part is changed. The answers are often expressed as equations, because the verbal description of a feedback loop with several branches is difficult to reason about without them.

The approach does not reject reductionism. It needs the properties of the parts as its inputs, and the measurement of those properties is the first step of any model. The systems approach adds a second step, in which the parts are assembled into a model that is tested against the behaviour of the whole. A model that reproduces the behaviour of the whole, and predicts a behaviour that has not been seen before, gives more confidence than an accurate list of parts.

The rest of this topic develops the tools for that second step, starting with the mathematics of the networks that link the parts.
