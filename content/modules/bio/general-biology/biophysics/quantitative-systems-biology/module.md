Quantitative systems biology asks how the parts of a living system combine to produce its behaviour. A single enzyme can be characterised in a test tube, and a single cell can be studied under a microscope, yet the response of a whole cell to a hormone or the behaviour of a tissue after an injury depends on how many components interact, and in what order. Kitano (2002) argued that the properties that matter most for biological function are often properties of the network, not of its individual components, and that understanding them requires models that can be analysed and tested.

The field works in a cycle. Measurements are used to propose a network of interactions, the network is written as equations, the unknown parameters are estimated from data, and the model's predictions are compared with new experiments. A model that predicts correctly under new conditions gains support, and one that fails shows where the understanding is incomplete. The cycle is the practical content of the topics that follow.

The chapters cover the main elements of the approach. They describe the mathematical models of biochemical networks, the role of feedback in producing stability and oscillation, the structure of biological networks as graphs, the estimation of parameters, the physiological models that link cells to organs, the role of noise in small systems, and the standards for validating and reporting a model. Each chapter shows the link between a mathematical idea and a biological question.
