X-ray free-electron lasers produce pulses of X-rays that are many orders of magnitude brighter than conventional sources and last a few femtoseconds. A pulse is short enough to record a diffraction pattern before the radiation damage has time to destroy the crystal, and the approach is called diffraction before destruction. Each pulse records a single pattern from a crystal that is then vaporised, so a fresh crystal must be supplied for each pulse, usually as a stream of micro-crystals in a jet.

The data are collected serially, from many crystals that are each in a random orientation. Each crystal yields a partial data set, containing some of the reflections, and the complete data set is assembled by merging the partial sets after determining the orientation of each. The merging assumes that the crystals are isomorphous, so that their diffraction is comparable, and that the orientation of each can be found reliably. Brünger (1992) described the statistical measures of quality that apply to the merged data, and the merging must be assessed by them.

The merging of many partial data sets is a source of error. A crystal whose lattice differs slightly from the others will add noise, and the statistics of the merged set can hide the outlying crystals. The criteria for including each partial set must be stated, and the data that are excluded must be reported.

The short pulses also make time-resolved studies possible. A pump pulse, such as a flash of light, starts a reaction in the crystal, and a probe pulse records the diffraction at a chosen delay. A series of such measurements follows the structural change as it proceeds, and the approach has shown the motion of molecules that undergo a light-driven or a catalytic change.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a time-resolved sequence of structures may be interpreted as a movie of the reaction when the intermediate steps are not separately measured, and Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the reaction intermediates, such as the consistency of each intermediate with an independent measure of its population.

Free-electron lasers extend crystallography to the time domain and to crystals that were once too sensitive to measure, and their data demand careful merging and interpretation.
