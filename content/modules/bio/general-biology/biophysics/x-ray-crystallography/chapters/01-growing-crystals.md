X-ray diffraction requires an ordered crystal, in which the molecules are arranged in the same orientation at many repeating positions, and growing such a crystal from a biological macromolecule is often the hardest step. The molecule must be pure and homogeneous, since a mixture of forms will not pack into a single lattice. Kendrew et al. (1958) grew myoglobin crystals from a purified protein, and their success depended on the conditions that favour orderly packing over disordered precipitation.

The principal method is vapour diffusion, in which a drop containing the protein and a precipitant is equilibrated against a reservoir of higher precipitant concentration, so that water slowly leaves the drop and the protein concentration rises gradually. The rise must be slow enough that the molecules have time to find their lattice positions rather than aggregating. The pH, the temperature, the salt and the presence of additives all change the crystallisation, and a screen of many conditions is usually needed to find one that works.

Crystal quality depends on the same conditions. A crystal that grows quickly is often disordered, and it diffracts weakly or to low resolution. Slow growth and the choice of conditions that favour a single nucleation event produce larger and more ordered crystals, and the quality is judged by the resolution of the diffraction they give.

Flexible and heterogeneous molecules crystallise poorly. A protein with a long disordered loop may form crystals only when the loop is removed or shortened, and the removal changes the molecule. A protein that exists in several conformations may crystallise in the one that packs best, and the crystal then reports only that conformation. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the fold is defined by the chain, and the crystal's selection of a conformation is a further influence on what is seen.

Crystal contacts can alter the structure. Molecules in a lattice touch their neighbours, and the contacts can stabilise a surface or a loop in a position it would not occupy in solution. The structure then describes the molecule in the crystal, and its relevance to solution must be assessed by comparison with other methods.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the crystal structure of a protein may be treated as its structure in solution without this assessment, and Platt (1964) recommended comparison of the crystal structure with an independent measurement of the same protein in solution.

Growing a crystal is the first condition of an X-ray structure, and the crystal shows the molecule in the conditions the crystal imposes.
