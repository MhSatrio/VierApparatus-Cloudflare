The X-rays that produce the diffraction also damage the crystal. The energy absorbed breaks chemical bonds and creates reactive species, and the damage accumulates as the crystal is exposed. Some of the damage is general, reducing the order of the lattice and the intensity of the diffraction, but some is specific and alters the structure itself. Kendrew et al. (1958) recorded their data with the technology of the day, when the damage was a serious limit, and the problem has become more severe as the intensity of the sources has increased.

Specific damage affects particular chemical groups. Metal centres are reduced, which changes their coordination and the position of the metal, carboxyl groups are decarboxylated, and disulphide bonds are broken and reformed. The changes can alter the structure at the site of function, and an apparent structure of an active site may reflect the damage rather than the native state. Ioannidis (2005) described how a feature that is a product of the measurement may be reported as a property of the molecule, and the sites most susceptible to damage must be checked.

Cryocooling to low temperature slows the damage considerably, and most data are now collected from crystals held at about one hundred kelvin. The cooling reduces the rate of radical formation and the diffusion of the radicals that cause the damage. Cryocooling also changes the structure, since the molecules are held in a frozen solvent and the thermal motion is reduced, which can alter the conformations and the occupancy of flexible regions.

The cryoprotectant used to prevent ice formation is a solution that can enter the crystal and bind to the protein. Its presence may alter the structure, and the density of the protectant in the model must be assessed. A feature that is present only in the frozen crystal may reflect the protectant or the freezing rather than the molecule in solution.

Platt (1964) recommended a comparison of the structure at different doses and temperatures, which reveals whether the feature changes with the damage, and the comparison of frozen and room-temperature structures tests whether cooling has altered the molecule.

The structure obtained from a damaged or frozen crystal is a measurement of the crystal, and the checks for damage are the means of separating the molecule from the artefact of the measurement.
