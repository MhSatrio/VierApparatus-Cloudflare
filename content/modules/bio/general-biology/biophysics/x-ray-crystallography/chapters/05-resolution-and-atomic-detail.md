Resolution is the smallest spacing between planes of atoms that the diffraction data record, and it is a measure of the detail in the electron density. Crystal structures are commonly grouped by resolution. At around three ångströms, the course of the chain is visible and the large secondary structures are recognisable, but the side chains are poorly defined. Kendrew et al. (1958) built myoglobin at a resolution where the chain could be traced but the side chains were ambiguous, and the placement of many of them was a matter of interpretation.

At around two ångströms, the side chains are generally visible and their orientations can be assigned with some confidence. At one and a half ångströms or better, individual atoms are resolved, the alternative conformations of side chains can be distinguished and water molecules can be placed. Brünger (1992) showed that the statistical measures of fit depend on the resolution, and the quality of a model must be judged in that context.

The claims made from a structure must match its resolution. A structure at low resolution cannot support conclusions about the hydrogen bonding of a particular side chain or about the mechanism of a catalytic residue whose orientation is uncertain. Ioannidis (2005) described how a mechanism inferred from a low-resolution structure may be presented as established, and the resolution must limit the claims.

The resolution is reported with every structure and with every claim drawn from it. A figure that shows a side chain at the atomic level, from a structure at low resolution, presents detail that the data do not contain, and the reader should be told the resolution of the data behind it.

Platt (1964) recommended that a claim be stated so that it could be tested, and a claim about an atom's position at a stated resolution can be tested by a higher-resolution structure or by an independent method. The resolution is therefore part of what makes a structural claim testable.

The resolution of the data sets the atomic detail of the model, and a structural claim is only as fine as the resolution that supports it.
