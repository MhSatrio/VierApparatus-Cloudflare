# X-ray Crystallography

X-ray crystallography is the method that produced most of the atomic structures of biological molecules. A crystal is a regular, repeating arrangement of molecules, and when it is struck by X-rays the scattered waves interfere to produce a pattern of spots. The positions and intensities of the spots encode the arrangement of electrons in the crystal, and the structure is recovered by a sequence of steps that the chapters follow in turn. Kendrew et al. (1958) used the method to determine the first protein structure, and the steps they developed remain in use.

The chapters begin with the growth of crystals, which is often the hardest part of the process, and the collection of the diffraction data. The phase problem, which arises because the detector records intensities without phases, is then described, together with the methods used to solve it. Model building and refinement follow, along with the validation that Brünger (1992) placed on a quantitative footing, and the final chapters address the interpretation of crystal structures and the limits of what the crystal shows.

The method is a sequence of inferences, each of which can fail. Ioannidis (2005) described how the chain of decisions in a structure determination can produce a result that looks precise, and Platt (1964) recommended checks at each step that could reveal errors. Watson and Crick (1953) inferred the structure of DNA from diffraction together with chemistry, and the crystallographic method has always combined the data with constraints from chemistry, as the demonstration by Anfinsen (1973) that folding follows the chain's chemistry requires.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biological molecules that crystallography has revealed, and the crystal structure is the means by which their architecture is learned.
