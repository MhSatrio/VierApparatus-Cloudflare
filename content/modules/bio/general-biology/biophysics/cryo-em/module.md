# Cryo-EM

Cryo-electron microscopy has transformed structural biology in the past decade. Molecules are frozen in a thin film of vitreous ice, imaged with an electron microscope, and their images are combined into a three-dimensional reconstruction. The method does not require crystals, and it can determine the structures of large complexes and of molecules that resist crystallisation. Bai et al. (2015) described the rapid rise of the method and the reasons it became central to structural biology, and this topic describes the steps by which a set of images becomes a model.

The chapters follow the process. The principles of electron microscopy of frozen samples come first, followed by the preparation and vitrification of the sample, which determine whether the particles are intact and well distributed. The image processing that averages many particle images, the classification of heterogeneous populations and the reconstruction and resolution that follow are then described. The model building, the tomography of cellular samples and the reporting of cryo-EM structures complete the topic.

A cryo-EM map is an average over many particles, and each particle may be in a different state or condition. The method is powerful because it can separate these states computationally, and it is also vulnerable to errors in the alignment and classification that can produce features that are not in the molecule. Ioannidis (2005) described how the processing of noisy data offers many choices that can change the result, and Platt (1964) recommended checks that could reveal artefacts.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the large complexes of the cell whose structures cryo-EM reveals, and these structures are best understood in the context of the processes they carry out.
