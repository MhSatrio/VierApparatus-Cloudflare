Cryo-electron tomography records a series of images of the same specimen at different tilt angles, and these are reconstructed into a three-dimensional volume. The volume shows the arrangement of molecules within a cell, a vesicle or a thin section, in their native context, and it is one of the few methods that can see macromolecules in place. Bai et al. (2015) described the extension of cryo-EM to cellular samples, and the tomogram is the means by which the organisation of the cell's machinery becomes visible.

The tilt series cannot cover every direction. The specimen is tilted only to a limited angle, because at high tilt the electron path through the sample is long and the image is obscured. The directions that are not measured form a region of Fourier space called the missing wedge, and the reconstruction is blurred and elongated along the beam direction as a result. The missing wedge is an intrinsic limit of tomography, and the interpretation of features must take it into account.

Subtomogram averaging recovers the structure of molecules within the tomogram. Many copies of a molecule are located in the volume, each is extracted as a subtomogram, and the copies are aligned and averaged. The averaging overcomes the noise and the missing wedge, since the copies are in different orientations, and it can give a structure at a resolution far better than the tomogram itself. The method depends on the copies being identical, and the heterogeneity of a cellular population must be classified as in single-particle analysis.

The value of tomography is the context it provides. A molecule seen in its cellular neighbourhood reveals its associations, its distribution and its relation to membranes and other structures, which isolated particles cannot show. Ioannidis (2005) described how a context-rich image may be interpreted as a functional arrangement without evidence of the interactions, and Platt (1964) recommended tests that could confirm an association, such as the loss of the feature after the partner is removed.

Cryo-electron tomography brings the molecules of the cell into view, and its interpretation must account for the missing directions and for the averaging that recovers detail.
