A cryo-EM sample must be frozen so rapidly that water does not have time to form crystals. A thin film of the sample solution, a few tens of nanometres thick, is applied to a grid with a holey carbon support and plunged into a cryogen, such as liquid ethane, cooled by liquid nitrogen. The water is solidified into a glass, the vitreous ice, which holds the molecules in their hydrated state without the damage of crystalline ice. The method preserves the molecules in a form close to their native one, and this is the principal advantage over the other methods.

The thickness of the ice controls the contrast and the ability to image the particles. Too thick an ice layer scatters electrons strongly and obscures the particles, while too thin a layer may squeeze the particles or leave them unable to adopt their natural orientations. The blotting of the grid before freezing controls the thickness, and the process is adjusted for each sample. The distribution of particles on the grid must also be controlled, since particles that clump or lie too close together cannot be separated in the images.

The air-water interface is a serious source of artefacts. Molecules that reach the thin film surface adsorb to the interface, where they may unfold or denature, and they may adopt preferred orientations that bias the reconstruction. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the structure of a protein depends on its environment, and the air-water interface is an environment that can destabilise the fold. Surfactants, support films and rapid freezing are used to limit the number of particles that reach the interface.

Preferred orientation is a further problem. If the particles adopt a few orientations on the grid, the reconstruction has gaps in the directions of view that are missed, and the resolution and the accuracy of the map suffer. Tilting the specimen during collection, or changing the support, can reduce the problem, and the orientation distribution must be reported.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the success of a preparation is often judged by the appearance of the particles on the grid, and Platt (1964) recommended a direct test of whether the preparation preserves the molecule, such as the comparison of the structure with an independent determination of the same molecule.

Sample preparation determines what the microscope can see, and the artefacts introduced at the interface and the grid must be recognised and controlled.
