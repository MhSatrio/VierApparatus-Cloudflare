An electron microscope forms an image from electrons that pass through a thin specimen. Biological material scatters electrons weakly, and the contrast of an image depends on the phase shift that the specimen imposes on the electron wave. The low contrast of biological specimens is the central difficulty of the method, and the signal is far smaller than the noise that the detector records. Bai et al. (2015) described how the improvement of detectors and of processing made the weak signal usable, and this chapter describes the physics on which the images depend.

Electrons damage the specimen. The energy they deposit breaks bonds and moves the atoms, and the damage increases with the dose. To preserve the molecule the images must be taken at very low dose, so that each image records a small number of electrons per pixel, and the image is extremely noisy as a consequence. The noise is then averaged away by combining the images of many particles, which is why the method depends on processing.

Contrast is generated by defocus. The objective lens of the microscope is set slightly out of focus, which converts the phase shift of the specimen into an amplitude contrast in the image. The contrast transfer function describes how each spatial frequency is transmitted, and it oscillates with frequency, so that some frequencies are transmitted with the wrong sign or not at all. The correction of this function is a necessary step in the reconstruction.

The microscope's optics modify the signal. The detector's response, the coherence of the beam and the aberrations of the lenses all alter the image, and each must be measured or corrected. A reconstruction that ignores the transfer function may show features that are artefacts of the optics rather than of the molecule.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the correction of the transfer function involves parameters that may be chosen to improve the apparent result, and Platt (1964) recommended a check on the correction, such as the reconstruction of a known specimen that tests whether the optics have been handled correctly.

Electron microscopy of frozen samples records a low-dose, low-contrast image whose meaning depends on correcting the optics and on averaging many particles.
