The voltage clamp is a feedback circuit that holds the potential across a membrane at a value chosen by the experimenter. When the potential is stepped, the membrane current changes as the ionic channels respond, and the clamp supplies the current needed to keep the potential at its set value. The current the clamp supplies is therefore equal to the current that the membrane's channels carry, and recording it gives the ionic current directly. Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) developed this technique on the squid giant axon, and it transformed the study of excitability.

Their experiments used the clamp to separate the currents. By stepping the potential to different values and changing the ionic composition of the solutions bathing the axon, they isolated the sodium current, which flows inward early in the response, from the potassium current, which flows outward later. The separation depended on the selective removal of each ion, and it allowed the two conductances to be measured as functions of voltage and time.

The recorded current contains components that are not ionic. The capacitance of the membrane produces a transient current when the potential is stepped, and the leak through the membrane produces a steady current, and these must be subtracted. Methods that apply small test steps in the opposite direction, and subtract the response, remove the capacitive and leak components, and the subtraction must be checked for accuracy.

The clamp controls only the region where it measures. In a cell of complex shape, the potential is not uniform, and the parts of the cell far from the electrode are not clamped. The current recorded then comes partly from unclamped regions, distorting the kinetics. This space clamp problem limits the voltage clamp in neurons with long dendrites, and the limit must be assessed for each preparation.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a separation of currents by ionic substitution assumes that the substitution affects only the intended current, and Platt (1964) recommended controls that test the separation, such as a blocker specific to each current.

The voltage clamp holds the potential and reads the current, and its interpretation depends on the subtraction of artefacts and on the control of the potential throughout the cell.
