Many drugs act by blocking ion channels, and the block depends on the state of the channel. A drug may bind only when the channel is open, so that it is most effective during activity, or it may bind preferentially when the channel is inactivated, so that its effect grows as the cell is held depolarised. The state dependence explains why a drug can have very different potency in different tissues and under different conditions, and it is a central feature of channel pharmacology that the electrophysiological measurements reveal. Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) described the gating states of the sodium channel, and the drug's binding to these states is measured by the same kind of recording.

The potency of a channel blocker is measured by applying the drug at several concentrations and recording the reduction in current. The concentration that halves the current is the potency, and the state dependence is measured by changing the voltage protocol, which changes the proportion of channels in each state. Neher and Sakmann (1976) made the single-channel recordings that show how a drug interrupts the openings, and the individual events reveal the mechanism of block.

Screening for unwanted channel block is required in drug development. A compound that blocks a cardiac potassium channel, for example, may prolong the repolarisation and predispose to arrhythmia, as the cardiac chapter described, and the screen identifies such compounds before they reach patients. The screen compares the block of the target channel with that of the channels whose block is undesirable, and the ratio of the potencies is a measure of selectivity.

The relation between the block in the screen and the clinical risk must be established. A compound that blocks the channel in vitro at a concentration far above that achieved in patients carries a smaller risk than one that blocks at a therapeutic concentration, and the comparison requires the plasma concentrations and the binding in the tissue. Ioannidis (2005) described how a screen that detects block may be taken as a predictor of harm without the evidence that the concentration matters, and Platt (1964) recommended comparisons of the screen's predictions with the clinical outcomes.

Channel pharmacology uses electrophysiology to measure the potency and state dependence of drugs, and the screen's value depends on the link between the cellular block and the risk in patients.
