The heart's rhythm depends on electrical excitation that is generated by specialised cells and spread through the muscle. The cardiac action potential has a plateau, sustained by a slow inward calcium current balanced by outward potassium currents, and this plateau separates it from the brief spike of nerve. Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) described the currents of the nerve membrane, and the cardiac currents are a related family with different kinetics and additional components, suited to a muscle that must contract for a sustained period.

The plateau prevents the heart from responding to a second stimulus during its contraction, which would produce a sustained contraction that could not pump blood. The duration of the plateau and the repolarisation that ends it are set by the balance of the currents, and changes in any of them alter the duration of the cardiac cycle's electrical phase. The electrocardiogram is a summed record of the electrical activity of the whole heart, recorded from electrodes on the surface of the body, and its waveforms reflect the spread and the repolarisation of the excitation.

Mutations in the cardiac channels can prolong or shorten the repolarisation. A prolonged repolarisation predisposes to early afterdepolarisations and to dangerous arrhythmias, and it is visible in the electrocardiogram as a lengthened interval. Neher and Sakmann (1976) introduced the single-channel recording that allows a mutant channel to be studied in isolation, and the effect of a mutation on the kinetics of the channel can be measured directly.

Many drugs act on the cardiac channels, and some prolong the repolarisation as an unwanted effect. The screening of drugs for this effect is required before they are approved, and the measurement of the channel block in isolated cells is a central part of the screen. The relation between channel block and the clinical arrhythmia must be shown, since the block alone does not establish the risk.

Ioannidis (2005) described how an association between a channel mutation and an arrhythmia may be assumed to be causal from the mechanism, and Platt (1964) recommended the tests that could separate the mechanism from its clinical consequence, such as the correlation of the cellular effect with the electrocardiographic change in the same individuals.

The electrical behaviour of the heart is set by its channels, and its disturbance is visible in the electrocardiogram and in the arrhythmias that follow.
