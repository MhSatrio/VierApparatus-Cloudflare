A channelopathy is a disease caused by an abnormality in an ion channel, and many of the inherited disorders of the heart, the nerve and the muscle are of this kind. A mutation changes the gating, the conductance or the expression of a channel, and the change alters the electrical behaviour of the cells that express it. The electrophysiological recording of the mutant channel, in a heterologous system or in the patient's cells, characterises the change, and the characterisation is the first step in linking the mutation to the disease.

The clinical disease arises from the electrical change in the tissue. A mutation that prolongs cardiac repolarisation produces an arrhythmia, one that alters neuronal excitability produces seizures, and one that changes the excitability of muscle produces weakness or stiffness. The link between the cellular change and the tissue disease depends on the cell type, the location of the mutant channel and the contribution of other channels, and the chain of explanation must be shown rather than assumed.

The characterisation of a mutant is a comparison with the wild type. The same channel is expressed with and without the mutation, and the kinetics, the voltage dependence and the current are compared under the same conditions. The comparison identifies the defect, which may be a shift in voltage dependence, a change in inactivation or a reduction in current. Ioannidis (2005) described how a mutation that changes a recorded property may be assumed to cause the disease without the evidence linking the property to the clinical course, and Platt (1964) recommended the tests that link the two.

The electrophysiological phenotype can guide treatment. Some channelopathies respond to drugs that correct the specific defect, and the choice of drug follows from the identified mechanism. The effectiveness of such treatments must be shown in patients, since a drug that corrects the defect in a cell may not improve the clinical course.

The study of channelopathies connects the biophysics of the channel to the disease it causes, and each link requires its own evidence.
