Development is often described as a deterministic program, but the molecular events that drive it are random, and the cells of an embryo make choices under noise. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the signals and transcriptional switches that direct cell fate, and the switches are subject to the noise described in the earlier chapters. Some developmental choices are made by chance, so that a population of equivalent cells divides into fates in proportions set by the statistics of the switch rather than by a precise signal.

Such stochastic choices can produce reproducible patterns. The proportion of cells adopting each fate is fixed by the probabilities, even though the fate of any single cell is not predictable. The pattern is robust at the level of the population, while the individual cell is unpredictable, and this is a common feature of developmental systems that use randomness as a tool.

Embryos also achieve robustness against noise. The timing of developmental events and the size of organs are reproduced across individuals despite variation in their molecular components, and the mechanisms include feedback that corrects deviations and the averaging of signals across many cells. Robustness is measured by the variation of an output across individuals and by its response to a perturbation.

Distinguishing a deliberate stochastic strategy from uncontrolled variation requires evidence. A strategy predicts that the proportions of fates depend on the probabilities of the switch, and that changing the probabilities changes the proportions in a predicted way. Uncontrolled variation shows no such dependence. Ioannidis (2005) described how a biological variation can be interpreted as adaptive without evidence that it is, and Platt (1964) recommended the perturbation experiment that separates the two possibilities.

Development therefore combines chance and control, and its reproducibility at the level of the organism depends on how the randomness of the molecular events is organised.
