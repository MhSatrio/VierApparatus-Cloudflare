# Protein Folding and Dynamics

Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein folds into its native structure from its sequence alone, and the question of how it does so in the short time available in the cell has been studied for half a century. The Molecular Biophysics topic described the forces that stabilise a fold. This topic describes the process of folding and the motions of the folded protein, which together determine how a sequence becomes a functional molecule and how that molecule changes its shape to act.

The chapters begin with the energy landscape, a picture in which the conformations of a chain are arranged by free energy, and folding proceeds down a funnel towards the native state. The intermediates and the kinetic traps that slow folding follow, and the protein-folding problem as Dill and MacCallum (2012) framed it is then considered. The motions of folded proteins, which are essential to their function, are described next, with the molecular dynamics simulations of Shaw et al. (2010) as an example of how these motions are studied, and the final chapters address the failure of folding and the measurement of dynamics.

The field has produced many theoretical models, and their predictions must be tested against measurements of folding rates and pathways. Ioannidis (2005) described how a model that reproduces known results may not predict new ones, and Platt (1964) recommended experiments that could discriminate between the models of folding.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the chaperones that assist folding in the cell, and the cell is not a passive solvent in which folding occurs unaided.
