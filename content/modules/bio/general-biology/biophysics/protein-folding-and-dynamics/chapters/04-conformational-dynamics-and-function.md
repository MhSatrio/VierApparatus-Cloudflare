A folded protein is not rigid. Its atoms vibrate on the picosecond scale, its side chains rotate on the nanosecond scale, its loops move on the microsecond to millisecond scale and whole domains may change their relative positions over longer times. The motions are not random noise. They are the means by which many proteins carry out their functions, and the rates of the motions are part of what the protein does. Karplus and McCammon (1983) described how the dynamics of proteins are linked to their function, and the picture has since been refined by experiment and simulation.

Enzymes use conformational motion in catalysis. A substrate may bind to an open form and the enzyme may close around it, excluding water and bringing the catalytic groups into place. The closing is a motion on a timescale that can limit the rate of the reaction, and the chemistry follows the motion. Shaw et al. (2010) simulated the structural dynamics of proteins at atomic resolution and showed how these motions can be followed, with the aim of relating them to function.

Allosteric regulation transmits a signal from a site that binds an effector to a distant site that acts on the substrate. The transmission is a change in conformational distribution, in which the effector shifts the equilibrium among the states, and the shift propagates through the structure. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the structure is linked throughout, so that a change at one point can affect others, and allostery depends on this linkage.

The rates of conformational change are measured by relaxation methods that perturb the equilibrium and follow its return, by spectroscopy that reports the change in a signal and by single-molecule techniques that observe individual molecules switching. Each method has a time window, and the motions faster or slower than the window are invisible to it.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a motion inferred from a simulation may be presented as a measured property, and Platt (1964) recommended comparing the simulated motions with the measured rates to test the simulation.

A folded protein is a dynamic machine whose motions are part of its function, and the measurement of those motions is part of understanding what it does.
