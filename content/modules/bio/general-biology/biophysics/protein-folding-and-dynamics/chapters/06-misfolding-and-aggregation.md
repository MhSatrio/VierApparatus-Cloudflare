A misfolded protein is a chain that has adopted a conformation other than the native one, and some misfolded conformations expose surfaces that allow the molecules to stick together. Aggregation is the association of many such molecules into ordered assemblies, most often amyloid fibrils, in which the chains are arranged in cross-beta structures. Anfinsen (1973) established that the native fold is the state of lowest free energy under normal conditions, and misfolded states are higher in energy, which is why they must be actively prevented from accumulating in the cell. Dill and MacCallum (2012) described how the landscape can channel misfolded chains into traps, and aggregation is a fate of trapped chains.

Aggregation follows a nucleation and growth pattern. The formation of the first small assembly, the nucleus, is slow and rate-limiting, because it requires several molecules to come together in the correct orientation. Once a nucleus exists, further molecules add to it quickly, so the process is accelerated by seeding. The kinetics of the process show a lag phase, during which nuclei form, followed by rapid growth, and the lag phase is the time at which intervention could prevent the fibrils from forming.

Many diseases are associated with the aggregation of specific proteins, and the aggregates are found in the affected tissues. The relation between the aggregates and the disease is still debated, since the soluble oligomers that precede the fibrils may be more toxic than the mature fibrils, and some aggregates may be protective. Ioannidis (2005) described how the presence of aggregates in a disease can be taken as the cause without the test that would establish the cause, and the evidence must distinguish cause from consequence.

The cell has quality control systems that prevent aggregation. Chaperones bind the exposed surfaces of misfolded chains and assist their refolding, and the proteasome and autophagy remove chains that cannot be rescued. The failure of these systems, with age or stress, allows misfolded proteins to accumulate, and the balance between production and clearance determines whether aggregation occurs.

Platt (1964) recommended tests that could separate the explanations, such as an intervention that reduces the aggregation and the observation of whether the disease changes as the explanation predicts.

Misfolding and aggregation are the failures of the folding process and of the cellular systems that guard against it, and their study connects the physics of the landscape to the disease it can produce.
