Allostery is the regulation of a protein's activity at one site by the binding of a molecule at another. The classic models treat the protein as a set of subunits or conformations that are in equilibrium, and the binding of an effector shifts that equilibrium. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the fold is a defined arrangement, and an allosteric protein has more than one such arrangement with the balance between them set by the bound ligands. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cooperative binding of oxygen by haemoglobin as a classical example, in which the binding of one ligand changes the affinity for the next.

The concerted model proposes that all subunits switch together between two states, so that the binding of a ligand to the active state favours the switch of the whole protein. The sequential model proposes that each binding event induces a change in the subunit that binds, and the change propagates to its neighbours in a sequence. The two models predict different relations between the number of bound ligands and the affinity, and the measurement of the binding curve distinguishes them. Platt (1964) recommended such a discriminating test, in which the models make different predictions for the same measurement.

Coupling through the structure is the physical route of the signal. A change at the effector site alters the energies of the contacts around it, and the change propagates to the active site through a network of residues whose interactions are shifted. The network can be identified by measuring how mutations at one site change the energetics at another, and such measurements reveal which parts of the structure carry the signal.

The experimental tests of an allosteric pathway require mutations that disrupt the proposed route without disrupting the rest of the fold. A pathway that carries the signal should be blocked by a mutation along it, and the block should be specific to the route. Anfinsen's principle that the structure is a single linked arrangement means that a mutation can affect the fold globally, and the control of folding stability is necessary.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a proposed pathway from a structure may be accepted on the basis of proximity, and proximity alone does not establish the coupling, while Platt (1964) recommended the mutational test that separates the two.

Allostery transmits a signal through the shifts in a protein's conformational equilibrium, and a signalling pathway is shown by the effect of disrupting it.
