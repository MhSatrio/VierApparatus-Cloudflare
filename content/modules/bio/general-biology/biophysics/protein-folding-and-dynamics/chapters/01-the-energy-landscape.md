The energy landscape is a way of picturing the folding of a protein. Each conformation of the chain has a free energy, and the set of all conformations forms a surface whose height is the free energy and whose coordinates describe the shape of the chain. The unfolded chain lies high on the surface, where it has many conformations and high entropy, and the native fold lies at the bottom, where the free energy is lowest. Dill and MacCallum (2012) described the protein-folding problem in terms of this landscape, and the question is how the chain descends it.

The landscape is a funnel. The conformations near the native state are more numerous and lower in energy than those far from it, and the funnel narrows as the chain approaches the bottom. A chain at any point on the funnel tends to move towards the native state, because the downhill steps are more probable than the uphill ones. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the native state is the lowest free energy state, and the funnel is the shape of the surface that guides the chain to it.

The funnel resolves a paradox. A chain of a hundred residues has an enormous number of conformations, and a search of all of them would take far longer than the observed folding time. The funnel means that the chain does not search at random. Each step is biased towards lower energy, so the search is directed, and the time to fold depends on the roughness of the funnel rather than on the number of conformations.

The landscape is rugged. Local minima, which are conformations of low energy that are not the native state, can trap the chain, and the escape from a trap requires an unfolding of part of the structure. The ruggedness slows folding and makes some proteins fold slowly or aggregate, and the trapping is a source of misfolding in disease.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a picture that is compelling may be accepted without the tests that its predictions require, and Platt (1964) recommended experiments that could show the landscape to be different, such as measurements of the folding rate under conditions that change the funnel.

The energy landscape is a picture of how a chain finds its fold, and its shape explains both the speed and the failures of folding.
