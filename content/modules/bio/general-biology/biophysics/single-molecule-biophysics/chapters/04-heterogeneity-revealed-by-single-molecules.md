An ensemble measurement reports the average of a property across a population of molecules, and the average describes no single molecule if the molecules differ. Two enzymes with the same average activity may differ greatly: one may be uniformly moderate, while the other alternates between a very fast state and a very slow one. The ensemble cannot distinguish these, but single-molecule measurements can, because each molecule's behaviour is recorded in turn. Moerner and Orrit (1999) described the detection that makes this possible, and the heterogeneity they reveal is often the most informative result of the method.

Heterogeneity arises from several sources. Molecules may differ in their conformations, having adopted different structures during their synthesis or folding, and the conformations may interconvert slowly. They may differ in their histories, having been modified or bound to different partners, and the history determines the subsequent behaviour. They may differ in their local environment, sitting in different positions in the membrane or the cell, and the environment alters the rates of the reactions they perform.

The distribution of a property is the record of the heterogeneity. A histogram of the activity, the binding time or the conformation across many molecules shows whether the population is uniform or has several classes, and the shape of the distribution reveals the number of classes and their proportions. The interpretation requires that the distribution be measured from enough molecules, since a distribution from a few molecules can appear to have structure that is not present.

Heterogeneity has biological consequences. A cell with molecules of different activity responds to a stimulus differently from one in which all molecules are alike, and the population of a few highly active molecules may dominate a response. The heterogeneity therefore matters for function, and single-molecule measurements connect the molecular distribution to the behaviour of the cell.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a distribution inferred from a small sample may be taken as the population distribution, and the sample must be large enough to establish the classes. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could show whether the classes are real, such as the demonstration that a molecule remains in its class over time.

Single-molecule measurements show the population as a set of individuals, and the distribution of their behaviour is a property that the average hides.
