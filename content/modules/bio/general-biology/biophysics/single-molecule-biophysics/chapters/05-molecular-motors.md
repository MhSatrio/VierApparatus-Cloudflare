Molecular motors convert chemical energy into mechanical work, moving along filaments or rotating within their structures. Kinesin walks along microtubules, myosin pulls on actin filaments, and the rotary motor of the ATP synthase turns its central shaft as protons flow through it. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the motors of the cytoskeleton and their roles in transport and contraction, and single-molecule methods reveal the steps and the forces that these machines produce, which ensemble measurements can only infer.

Optical trapping, as Ashkin (1970) introduced it, can follow a motor's steps by attaching a bead to the motor and holding it in a trap. The bead's displacement shows each step, and the force needed to stall the motor reveals its maximum force. Kinesin takes steps of about eight nanometres, matching the spacing of the tubulin subunits, and the step size reveals how the motor relates to the track it follows. The stall force is a measure of the work the motor can do against a load.

The stepping cycle is coupled to the hydrolysis of ATP. Each step corresponds to the hydrolysis of one ATP molecule in many motors, and the number of steps per ATP sets the efficiency of the coupling. The chemical energy released by hydrolysis is compared with the mechanical work of the step, and the ratio gives the efficiency. Mitchell (1961) described how the energy of the proton gradient drives the rotary synthase, and the rotary motor is the reverse of the synthase's synthesis.

The efficiency of molecular motors is high by the standard of human machines, and the reasons include the close coupling of the chemistry to the mechanics and the small size of the steps. The measurement of the efficiency depends on knowing how many ATP molecules are consumed per step and how much work each step performs, and both must be measured on the same molecule.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a motor's efficiency may be estimated from averages across many molecules, and the averages can conceal slipping or futile cycles. Platt (1964) recommended single-molecule measurements of the coupling, which test whether each step is paid for by one ATP.

Molecular motors show how chemical energy becomes steps and forces, and the single-molecule record reveals the coupling that the ensemble averages.
