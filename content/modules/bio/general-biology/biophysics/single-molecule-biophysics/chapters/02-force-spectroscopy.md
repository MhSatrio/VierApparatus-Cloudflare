When a molecule is stretched by a trap, its extension rises with force in a way that reveals its mechanics. A polymer such as DNA extends in a characteristic curve whose shape is set by its stiffness and its entropy, and a structured protein resists stretching until a force is reached at which a domain unfolds, when the extension jumps. The force-extension curve is therefore a fingerprint of the molecule, and the sequence of jumps reveals the number and the order of the domains that unfold. Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein folds into a defined structure, and force spectroscopy can unfold that structure one domain at a time.

Unfolding under force is a non-equilibrium process, because the force is applied at a finite rate. The force at which a domain unfolds depends on the loading rate, and the faster the force is applied, the higher the force at which unfolding occurs. The dependence on loading rate allows the energy landscape of the unfolding to be estimated, and the analysis must account for the rate, since a comparison of forces measured at different rates is not a comparison of stabilities.

The rate at which a bond breaks also depends on the force. A bond held under a force breaks faster, because the force lowers the energy barrier to its rupture, and the dependence on force reveals the distance to the transition state along the direction of pulling. The analysis of rupture rates under force gives information about the energy landscape that the bond traverses, which is complementary to the information from unfolding.

Intermediates appear as additional steps in the force-extension curve. A molecule that unfolds through a partly folded state shows a step of intermediate length before the full extension, and the presence and the length of the intermediate are evidence for its existence. The intermediates detected under force may differ from those detected in solution, since the force alters the landscape, and the interpretation must consider this.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the identification of an intermediate from a force curve may be made from a single trace, and Platt (1964) recommended a test that could confirm the intermediate in many molecules, with the consistency of its length across traces as the criterion.

Force spectroscopy shows the mechanics of a molecule one step at a time, and the forces at which each step occurs reveal the energies that hold the molecule together.
