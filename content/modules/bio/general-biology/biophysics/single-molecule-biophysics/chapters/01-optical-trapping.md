Ashkin (1970) showed that a beam of light exerts a force on a small particle, the radiation pressure, and that a focused beam can accelerate a particle and hold it in place. The particle is attracted to the centre of a focused beam because light refracted through a bead pushes it back towards the focus, and the bead is held in a trap whose stiffness depends on the power of the laser and the size of the bead. The optical trap can hold a bead in three dimensions and move it without contact, which makes it a tool for applying force to a single molecule.

The trap must be calibrated. The stiffness of the trap, which relates the displacement of the bead to the force on it, is measured by observing the bead's thermal fluctuations, which follow the equipartition principle, or by applying a known drag from a flowing fluid. The calibration converts the measured displacement into a force, and its accuracy determines the accuracy of every force reported. The calibration depends on the bead's size and on the optical properties of the sample, and it must be redone when these change.

A biological molecule is attached to the bead at one end and to a surface or a second bead at the other. The attachment must hold under the force applied and must not disturb the molecule, and the chemistry of the linkage is chosen to be specific and strong. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular machines that such measurements examine, and the attachment must allow them to act as they would in the cell.

The position of the bead is detected with a precision of a nanometre or below, usually by interfering light from the bead with a reference beam or by imaging its position on a detector. The precision of the detection, together with the trap stiffness, sets the smallest force and displacement that can be resolved, and the measurement must be designed to resolve the features of interest.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a force measured with an uncalibrated trap may be reported as a property of the molecule, and Platt (1964) recommended controls that test the calibration, such as measuring a standard whose behaviour is known.

Optical tweezers hold a bead by the force of light, and their use on molecules depends on calibration, attachment and precise detection.
