A single-molecule experiment is a system with several parts, and each part can introduce a behaviour that is attributed to the molecule. The molecule must be attached to a surface or a bead, and the surface may bind it, alter its folding or hold it in a conformation that it would not adopt in solution. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular environment in which molecules normally act, and the artificial surface is a poor substitute unless its effects are measured and excluded.

The attachment chemistry can change the molecule. A linker that is long or flexible may allow motions that are not present in the cell, and a linker that is short may restrict them. Labels attached for fluorescence can alter the binding or the conformation, and a labelled molecule must be compared with an unlabelled one in an assay that measures the same function. Ashkin (1970) showed that a bead can be held by light, but the bead itself is a large object attached to a small molecule, and its drag and its interactions can affect the measured motion.

Fluorophores blink and bleach. A fluorophore may switch between bright and dark states in a way that resembles a conformational change, and it may bleach permanently after a time. The blinking must be distinguished from a real state change, and the bleaching limits the observation time. Moerner and Orrit (1999) described the photophysics of single fluorophores, and their analysis is the basis for telling blinking from conformation.

The controls are the means of showing that an observed behaviour belongs to the molecule. A control with a molecule known to lack the behaviour, or with the same molecule under conditions that abolish it, should show the absence of the effect. The surface should be tested with an empty attachment, and the labelling should be tested for its effect on the function. Ioannidis (2005) described how an observed behaviour that is not checked against such controls may be attributed to the molecule when it arises from the apparatus.

Platt (1964) recommended a test that could show the behaviour to be an artefact, and the controls are that test: a behaviour that survives the removal of the molecule or of its specific feature is not a property of the molecule.

The molecule is measured in a system that is not the cell, and the controls show which of the observed behaviour is the molecule's own.
