Moerner and Orrit (1999) reviewed the detection of single molecules by their fluorescence, which became possible when the background light could be reduced enough for the signal of one molecule to be seen. A single fluorophore emits a stream of photons, and the emission stops when it bleaches. The detection requires a sensitive camera or detector, a low background and a means of immobilising or tracking the molecule, and the signal is a sequence of bright and dark periods that reflects the molecule's behaviour.

Fluorescence resonance energy transfer transfers energy from an excited donor fluorophore to an acceptor fluorophore at a distance, and the efficiency falls steeply with the distance between them. Two labels attached to different parts of a single molecule report the distance between those parts, and a change in efficiency reports a change in the conformation. The method gives distances in the range of a few nanometres, which is the range of many conformational changes in proteins and nucleic acids.

Real-time observation reveals binding and switching. A molecule that binds a partner shows a change in fluorescence when the partner arrives and leaves, and the durations of the bound and unbound periods give the rates. A conformational switch shows as a change in the energy transfer efficiency, and the sequence of states can be followed as the molecule moves. The observations are direct, and they reveal the individual trajectories that an ensemble average would merge.

The precautions are important. A signal that appears to come from one molecule may come from a cluster of molecules, or from an aggregate, and the single-step bleaching of the signal is one check that the observed spot contains one fluorophore. The attachment of the labels may alter the behaviour of the molecule, and the labelling must be shown not to disturb the process.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a result from a small number of single-molecule traces may be reported as typical when it reflects the particular molecules selected, and Platt (1964) recommended analysing a large number of molecules and testing whether the observed states are consistent across them.

Single-molecule fluorescence shows the behaviour of individual molecules in real time, and its interpretation depends on confirming that each signal truly comes from one molecule.
