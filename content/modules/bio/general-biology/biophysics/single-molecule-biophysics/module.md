# Single-Molecule Biophysics

Most biophysical measurements average over vast numbers of molecules, and the average can hide the behaviour of the individual. A population of enzymes may appear to act uniformly while each molecule switches between active and inactive states, and a population of binding sites may conceal a few that bind tightly and many that bind weakly. Single-molecule methods observe one molecule at a time, and they reveal the distribution of behaviour that an ensemble measurement can only summarise. The methods are now central to molecular biophysics.

The chapters begin with the optical methods that trap and manipulate single molecules, following the radiation-pressure principle that Ashkin (1970) demonstrated for small particles. Optical tweezers and force spectroscopy follow, then single-molecule fluorescence, which Moerner and Orrit (1999) reviewed as the detection of single molecules in condensed matter. The interpretation of single-molecule data, the heterogeneity they reveal and the limits of what a single trajectory can establish are then treated, and the final chapters address the experimental design that makes these measurements reliable.

Single-molecule measurements have a particular risk. The number of molecules observed is small, and a feature seen in a few molecules may be an artefact of the method or of the surface on which the molecules are attached. Ioannidis (2005) described how a striking observation in a small sample can be accepted without the replication that the sample size requires, and Platt (1964) recommended controls that show the feature is not produced by the method.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular machines whose individual behaviour these methods reveal, and each machine's cycle is a sequence that the single molecule displays directly.
