# Thermodynamics of Living Systems

Every process in a living cell obeys the laws of thermodynamics. Cells extract energy from nutrients, store it in molecules and membranes, and spend it on work: the synthesis of molecules, the movement of ions, the contraction of muscle and the maintenance of order against the tendency of systems to become disordered. The Mitochondrial Biology and Cellular Bioenergetics modules described the pathways by which energy is captured. This topic describes the thermodynamic principles that make those pathways work and the constraints that they must satisfy.

The chapters move from the basic quantities of energy and work, through the free energy that determines whether a reaction proceeds, to the coupling of reactions that makes an unfavourable step possible. The chemiosmotic gradient that Mitchell (1961) proposed is then described as a store of energy in the membrane, and the folding of proteins is treated as a thermodynamic equilibrium. The final chapters address the non-equilibrium steady states in which living systems are maintained and the methods by which their energy use is measured.

Thermodynamics constrains what is possible, but it does not say how fast a process will proceed, and it does not identify the mechanism. The chapters therefore pair the thermodynamic argument with the measurement that tests it, and Ioannidis (2005) described how a theoretical argument that is not checked against measurement can be accepted too readily. Platt (1964) recommended tests that could distinguish between the explanations that a thermodynamic argument allows.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular processes that the thermodynamics governs, and the energy budget of the cell is the ledger in which each process is recorded.
