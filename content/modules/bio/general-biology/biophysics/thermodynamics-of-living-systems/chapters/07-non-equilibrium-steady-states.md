A system at equilibrium has no net flux of matter or energy, and its composition does not change in time. A living cell is never at equilibrium while it is alive, because the processes that maintain its organisation are continuous. Instead it occupies a non-equilibrium steady state, in which fluxes of substrates and energy pass through it, its composition remains roughly constant and it continually dissipates energy as heat and waste. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the fluxes of metabolism, and the steady state is the balance of inflows and outflows that keeps the concentrations within their working ranges.

The maintenance of such a state costs energy. The fluxes that hold the cell away from equilibrium must be driven by the hydrolysis of ATP and by the gradients that respiration establishes, and the energy consumed is dissipated. The cell does not store this energy for the organisation it maintains; the organisation exists only while the dissipation continues. When the flux stops, the cell approaches equilibrium and dies, which is the thermodynamic meaning of death.

Mitchell (1961) showed that the proton gradient of mitochondria is maintained by the continuous flow of electrons from food to oxygen, and the gradient exists only while the flow proceeds. The steady state of the gradient is therefore a dynamic one, in which the leak of protons back across the membrane is balanced by the pumping driven by respiration.

Thermodynamics constrains the rates of these fluxes. A process that is far from equilibrium proceeds in one direction with a large driving force, and its rate depends on the enzymes and the transport that carry it. Rates are not determined by thermodynamics alone, but thermodynamics sets which directions are possible and how much force is available to drive them.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a steady state that appears constant may conceal fluctuations and compensations that a single measurement misses, and Platt (1964) recommended measurements of flux, rather than of concentration alone, to test the steady-state picture.

Life is a dissipative steady state, and its organisation is the visible trace of the energy it continues to spend.
