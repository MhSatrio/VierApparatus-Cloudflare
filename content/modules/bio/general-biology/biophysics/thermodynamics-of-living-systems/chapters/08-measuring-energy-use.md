Energy use is measured by three main approaches. Calorimetry measures the heat released by a system directly, and it captures the total energy dissipated regardless of the pathway. Respirometry measures the consumption of oxygen and the production of carbon dioxide, which, combined with the known energy content of the fuels, gives the energy released by oxidation. Isotope tracing follows labelled atoms through metabolic pathways, and it reveals the fluxes that carry energy from one molecule to another. Each method answers a different question, and together they provide a check on the energy budget.

The first law provides the consistency check. The heat measured by calorimetry should equal the energy released by respiration, and the energy stored in new biomass should account for the difference between the energy consumed and the energy dissipated. A budget that does not close indicates a missing term, a measurement error or a flux that has been overlooked, and the closure is the test that the measurements have captured the system.

Attribution is more difficult. The total energy use of an organism is measured, but the share spent on a particular process, such as the pumping of ions or the synthesis of protein, must be inferred from inhibitors, from the rate of the process and from assumptions about its energy cost. Each inference carries uncertainty, and the attribution of a large fraction of energy to one process is only as reliable as the assumptions behind it.

Isotope tracing reveals flux but not energy directly. A labelled substrate shows the route by which carbon or nitrogen passes through a network, and the rate of label incorporation gives the flux, from which the energy consumed can be estimated if the energy cost of each step is known. The approach therefore combines the measurement of flux with the thermodynamic values of the reactions.

Mitchell (1961) emphasised that the energy of respiration is stored and used in forms that are not easily measured, such as the proton gradient, and the accounting must include these intermediate stores. Ioannidis (2005) described how an accounting that is forced to balance by adjusting an assumed term gives a false sense of consistency, and Platt (1964) recommended measurements that could discriminate between the alternative accounts of the same budget.

The energy budget of a living system can be measured, but each entry in it is an estimate, and the honest accounting reports the uncertainty of each.
