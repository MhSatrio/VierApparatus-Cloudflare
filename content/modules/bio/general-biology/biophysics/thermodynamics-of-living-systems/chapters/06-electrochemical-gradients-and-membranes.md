A concentration difference of an ion across a membrane stores free energy, and so does an electrical potential difference, because the ion is charged. The two combine into an electrochemical gradient, and the free energy stored by moving one ion across the membrane is the sum of the chemical term, which depends on the ratio of concentrations, and the electrical term, which depends on the membrane potential and the ion's charge. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the membrane pumps and channels that establish these gradients, and each gradient is a store of energy that the cell can draw upon.

At equilibrium, the chemical and electrical terms cancel, and the membrane potential at which no net movement occurs is the equilibrium potential for the ion. The Nernst relation gives this potential from the logarithm of the ratio of concentrations, and the equilibrium potential for potassium is close to the resting potential of many cells, which is why potassium largely determines it. Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) used this relation to describe the driving force on each ion during the action potential.

The action potential is a transient departure from the resting state, produced by the opening of sodium channels and then potassium channels. The sodium influx carries energy into the cell, which is restored by the sodium pump, and the energy for the pump comes from the hydrolysis of ATP. The cell therefore spends energy continuously to maintain the gradients that allow its electrical signals.

Mitchell (1961) applied the same logic to mitochondria, where the electrochemical gradient of protons is the proton-motive force. The force combines a pH difference, which is the chemical term, with the membrane potential, which is the electrical term, and the sum drives the ATP synthase. The equivalence of the two terms is what allows the energy of respiration to be stored in a form that can be used anywhere in the membrane.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a calculated gradient depends on assumed values of the concentrations and the potential, and each should be measured where possible. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could discriminate between explanations of a change in the potential, such as a change in permeability against a change in the pump.

The membrane stores energy as the balance of chemical and electrical forces, and the cell maintains that store at a continuous cost.
