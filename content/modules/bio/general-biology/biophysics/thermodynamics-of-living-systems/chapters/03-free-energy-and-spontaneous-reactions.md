The free energy change of a reaction, written as delta G, combines the enthalpy and the entropy change and gives the direction in which a reaction proceeds at constant temperature and pressure. A reaction with a negative free energy change releases energy and proceeds spontaneously in the forward direction, and one with a positive change requires energy input. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the free energies of the reactions of metabolism, and the sign of the change is the first thing a biochemist examines when a pathway is analysed.

The standard free energy change is defined at a standard concentration of one molar for each solute and at pH seven. It is related to the equilibrium constant by an expression that gives the concentration ratio at which the reaction has no net driving force. The relation is exact for the standard state and shows that a large equilibrium constant corresponds to a large negative free energy change, while a constant near one corresponds to a change near zero.

The actual free energy change in a cell depends on the actual concentrations of the substrates and products, not on the standard state. A reaction whose standard change is positive can proceed if the product is removed quickly enough, and a reaction whose standard change is negative can be reversed if the product accumulates. The cell adjusts these concentrations, and the free energy of the reaction in the cell is therefore a property of the cell's state as well as of the reaction.

Mitchell (1961) argued that the energy of electron transfer is conserved in a gradient of protons across a membrane, and the free energy of the proton gradient is what drives the synthesis of ATP. The calculation of this free energy requires the concentrations of protons on each side and the electrical potential across the membrane, and the sum of these terms is the proton-motive force.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a calculated free energy depends on the values assumed for the concentrations, which are often not measured in the cell, and the uncertainty in those values must be carried into the conclusion. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could discriminate between the alternatives, such as measuring the concentrations directly and comparing the calculated direction with the observed one.

The sign of the free energy change tells whether a reaction can proceed under given conditions, and the cell's concentrations determine which conditions apply.
