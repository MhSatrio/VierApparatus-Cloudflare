Energy is the capacity to do work, and it takes several forms: chemical energy stored in the bonds of molecules, electrical energy in an ion gradient, mechanical energy in a moving structure and thermal energy in the random motion of molecules. The first law of thermodynamics states that energy is conserved, so that the energy a cell takes in from food and light must equal the energy it stores, does as work and releases as heat. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the metabolic pathways by which these transfers occur, and the energy budget of the cell can be followed through them.

Work is the transfer of energy by an organised process, such as the movement of a molecule against a force or the pumping of an ion against its gradient. Heat is the transfer of energy by the random motion of molecules, which cannot be fully converted into organised work. Every biological transformation releases some of its energy as heat, and the cell must dispose of this heat, which is why metabolism generates warmth and why the rate of metabolism is limited by the capacity to lose it.

The first law is an accounting principle, and it gives a constraint that every measurement of energy use must satisfy. The energy consumed by an organism, measured as oxygen used or food burned, must equal the energy that appears as heat and as stored or external work. A measurement that does not balance indicates a missing term in the accounting, and this check is one of the tests of the energy budget.

The distribution of energy among these forms determines what a process can do. A reaction that releases chemical energy can drive a movement only if the energy is coupled to the movement by a mechanism, and the coupling is what the later chapters describe.

Ioannidis (2005) described how an energy measurement that does not balance may be explained away by an assumption rather than corrected, and the balance should be checked against independent measures. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could separate the alternative accounts of the energy budget, such as measurements of heat production and of oxygen consumption that should agree.

Energy is conserved in the living cell, and the accounting of its forms is the basis for understanding what the cell can achieve.
