# ELISA

The enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay measures a specific substance in a liquid sample by its binding to an antibody immobilised on a plate. Engvall and Perlmann (1971) described the quantitative form, in which an enzyme attached to an antibody converts a colourless substrate into a coloured product whose intensity is proportional to the amount bound. The Western Blotting module described the same antibody logic applied to separated proteins. ELISA applies it to whole samples in a microplate, which allows many samples to be measured at once.

The chapters follow the order of an assay. The principle of the reaction comes first, with its roots in the labelling of antibodies that Coons et al. (1941) demonstrated. The immobilisation of the antigen or antibody on the plate, and the blocking of the remaining surface, follow. Then the sandwich and competitive formats, the enzyme conjugates and substrates, the standard curve and quantification. The final chapters cover controls and the validation that shows the assay measures what it claims to.

The assay gives a number, and a number can look more precise than the method supports. Ioannidis (2005) described how results reported without their uncertainty mislead, and the chapters emphasise the validation parameters that set the limits of a result. Platt (1964) recommended that an assay be designed so that it can distinguish between the hypotheses it is meant to test, and the controls serve that purpose.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the immune recognition on which the assay depends, and the analytes it commonly measures.
