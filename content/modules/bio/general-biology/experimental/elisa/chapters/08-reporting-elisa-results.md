An ELISA result is a number, and the number is meaningful only with the context that produced it. The report must allow another laboratory to repeat the assay and must allow a reader to judge how far the value can be trusted. Ioannidis (2005) described how results that are reported without their conditions cannot be tested, and the ELISA is particularly prone to this because many reagents and steps affect the signal.

The format and the reagents are stated in full. The capture and detection antibodies are identified by source, clone and lot, the enzyme and substrate are named, and the coating, blocking and incubation conditions are given. The sample preparation and dilutions are described, so that the calculation can be reversed if needed. Engvall and Perlmann (1971) established the quantitative method, and its reporting should be as complete as the original description.

The standard curve is reported with its fit, its range and the residuals. The controls on each plate are reported with their values, and the limits of detection and quantification are stated. A value outside the range is marked as such, rather than reported as if it were measured. The validation parameters, including precision and recovery, accompany the results.

Each reported value carries its uncertainty. A single number without an interval gives an impression of precision that the assay may not support. Platt (1964) argued that a claim should be stated in a form that could be shown false, and an interval with a stated confidence allows a reader to see whether the difference between two groups is larger than the assay's variation.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the value measured depends on the epitopes that remain accessible, and the report should state the form of the analyte that the assay recognises. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biological variation that the uncertainty must include.
