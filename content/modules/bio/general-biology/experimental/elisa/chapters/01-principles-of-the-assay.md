An ELISA turns a molecular binding event into a colour that can be read by a spectrophotometer. The analyte in the sample binds to an antibody fixed to the bottom of a well, and the amount bound is revealed by a second antibody carrying an enzyme. Engvall and Perlmann (1971) showed that the enzyme converts a colourless substrate into a coloured product, and the absorbance of the product in each well reports the amount of analyte. The principle of attaching a label to an antibody to make its binding visible was established by Coons et al. (1941), and the ELISA applies it to a microplate.

The steps are performed in a fixed order. The capture reagent is coated onto the plate, the surface is blocked, the sample is added and allowed to bind, the detection antibody is added, the enzyme is provided and the substrate is added last. Between steps the plate is washed to remove unbound material, and an incomplete wash leaves background that is then measured as signal. The timing of each step affects the amount bound, and it must be the same for every plate.

The signal is proportional to the analyte only within a working range. At low concentration, the signal is close to background and differences are hard to resolve. At high concentration, the binding sites are saturated and the signal levels off. The standard curve therefore has a sigmoidal shape, and the useful part is its middle section. Samples outside this range must be diluted and measured again.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a result read from the flat part of a curve can appear precise while actually being unreliable, and the working range should be stated. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the antibody-antigen binding that the assay relies on, and the affinity of that binding sets how tightly the analyte is held.
