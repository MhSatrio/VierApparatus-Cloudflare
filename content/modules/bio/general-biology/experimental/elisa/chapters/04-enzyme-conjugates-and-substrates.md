The enzyme gives the ELISA its amplifying power. Engvall and Perlmann (1971) attached an enzyme to the detection antibody so that each bound antibody could convert many substrate molecules into product. The label principle originated with Coons et al. (1941), who attached a fluorescent group to an antibody, and the enzyme label replaces the fluorophore in the colourimetric assay. Horseradish peroxidase and alkaline phosphatase are the two common enzymes, and each has a different substrate and a different sensitivity.

Horseradish peroxidase is widely used because it is small, stable and fast. Its substrates, such as tetramethylbenzidine, give a coloured product that is read at a specific wavelength, and the reaction is stopped with acid, which also changes the colour and improves reading. The enzyme is inhibited by azide, which must therefore be excluded from buffers. Alkaline phosphatase gives a stable product and tolerates some conditions that inhibit peroxidase, but its reaction is slower, and it is inhibited by phosphate.

The conjugation chemistry must preserve both the enzyme and the antibody. A conjugate with too many enzymes per antibody can reduce binding, and one with too few gives weak signal. Commercial conjugates are supplied with a stated activity, and each new lot should be checked against the previous lot on a standard.

The reaction must be timed. The product accumulates over time, and reading too late moves the signal beyond the linear range. The readings are taken at a fixed time after the substrate is added, and the time is the same for every plate. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the activity of a protein depends on its structure, and the enzyme loses activity if it is heated or stored badly, so its activity should be checked by its own standard.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the timing and the lot of an enzyme conjugate can change results from one study to another, and these details should be reported. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the enzymatic reactions that the labels imitate.
