The sandwich format captures the analyte between two antibodies. The capture antibody is fixed to the plate, the sample is added, and a second antibody that binds a different epitope is then added and detected by the enzyme. The signal rises with the amount of analyte. The format requires an analyte that carries at least two epitopes that can be bound at once, which is the case for many proteins larger than about fifteen kilodaltons. Engvall and Perlmann (1971) used an antibody-based design of this kind, and the two-site arrangement gives high specificity, since both antibodies must recognise the analyte.

The competitive format suits small molecules, which have only one epitope and cannot be bound by two antibodies at once. A fixed amount of labelled analyte is mixed with the sample and allowed to compete for a limited number of antibody sites on the plate. The more analyte in the sample, the less labelled analyte binds, and the signal falls as the concentration rises. The standard curve therefore slopes in the opposite direction to the sandwich format, and this must be remembered when the values are calculated.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the folded form of a protein determines its epitopes, and the choice of format depends on whether the epitopes survive in the sample. A sandwich assay requires two epitopes on the native protein, while a denatured sample may expose different ones.

The choice of format affects sensitivity and the risk of interference. A sandwich assay is generally more sensitive and specific, but it is limited to larger analytes. A competitive assay tolerates small molecules but can be affected by any substance that binds the antibody or the labelled analyte.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the direction of the standard curve is a common source of error when the format changes, and the orientation should be checked for every new assay. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecules that are measured in each format.
