Every ELISA plate needs controls, because the signal is small and easily confused with background. A blank well contains all reagents except the sample and shows the background of the plate and the substrate. A negative control contains a sample known to lack the analyte, and a positive control contains a known amount of it. Engvall and Perlmann (1971) relied on such controls to establish that the enzyme signal reported the analyte, and the controls serve the same purpose on every plate.

Background arises from nonspecific binding of the detection antibody or the enzyme to the plate, from incomplete washing and from substrate that has begun to react. It is measured in the blank and subtracted from the other readings, but the subtraction is valid only when the background is stable across the plate. A plate with high or variable background should be repeated, since the subtraction then adds error to every value.

Cross-reactivity is the binding of the antibody to molecules other than the analyte. A related protein or a metabolite that shares the epitope will give a signal, and the assay will overestimate the analyte. The cross-reactivity is measured by testing the related molecules at the concentrations they are present in real samples. Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein's shape determines its epitopes, and a close relative with a similar fold can share them.

Platt (1964) recommended tests that can distinguish between explanations, and a spiked sample and a cross-reactant panel do this by showing whether the signal is specific to the analyte. A negative result from these tests strengthens the claim, and a positive result limits it.

Ioannidis (2005) described how controls that are not reported cannot support a conclusion, and each plate's controls should be recorded with its results. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecules that commonly cross-react in biological samples.
