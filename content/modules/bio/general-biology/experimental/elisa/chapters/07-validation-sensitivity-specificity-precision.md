Validation shows that an ELISA measures what it claims to measure, over the range it claims to cover. Engvall and Perlmann (1971) established the quantitative method, but a method is trusted only after its performance has been measured. The parameters are fixed in advance, with acceptance criteria stated before the data are collected, so that the validation cannot be shaped by its results.

The limit of detection is the lowest concentration that gives a signal distinguishable from the blank, commonly taken as the blank mean plus three standard deviations of the blank. The limit of quantification is the lowest concentration that can be measured with acceptable precision, often taken at a coefficient of variation of about twenty per cent. Values between these limits are reported as detected but not quantified, rather than as numbers that imply a precision the assay lacks.

Specificity is tested against related molecules and against the matrix of the sample. Recovery of a known amount added to a real sample shows whether the matrix changes the response, and a recovery far from one hundred per cent shows interference. The result is reported with the range over which recovery was measured.

Precision is measured within a plate, between replicate wells, and between plates on different days. The coefficient of variation is the standard measure, and the acceptance value is stated for each level. Platt (1964) argued that a validation is strongest when it is designed to fail, and the precision test is useful in that way, because it reveals a method that cannot reproduce itself.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the apparent performance of a method is often higher in the laboratory that developed it than elsewhere, and the validation should be repeated in an independent setting before the assay is widely used. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biological variation in the analytes that make such validation necessary.
