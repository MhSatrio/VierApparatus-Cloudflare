The first step of an ELISA fixes the capture reagent to the plate. A polystyrene plate binds proteins by adsorption, and the capture antibody or antigen is applied in a coating buffer, usually at a slightly alkaline pH, and left to adsorb overnight at a low temperature or for a few hours at room temperature. The amount adsorbed depends on the concentration, the buffer, the temperature and the plate surface. Engvall and Perlmann (1971) relied on this adsorption to make their assay, and the same dependence on conditions remains today.

Adsorption can change the shape of the protein. Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein's activity depends on its folded structure, and a protein that spreads or unfolds on a plastic surface may lose the epitope that the detection antibody requires. For this reason the coating is tested, and the activity or binding of the immobilised reagent is confirmed before the assay is used for samples.

Blocking covers the remaining binding sites on the plate. A protein solution such as bovine serum albumin or casein is added after coating, and it adsorbs to the free surface so that the later reagents bind only where intended. Insufficient blocking leaves background, and excessive blocking can reduce the capture of the analyte. The blocking buffer is chosen to suit the antibodies and the sample.

Consistency between plates is controlled by using the same coating batch, the same incubation and the same blocking for all plates in a study. A standard included on each plate reveals variation in coating, and the result from each plate is normalised to its own standard.

Ioannidis (2005) described how uncontrolled variables such as coating can produce differences that are then attributed to biology, and these should be recorded. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the folding of proteins that determines whether an epitope remains exposed after adsorption.
