# Microscopy Techniques

The Microscopy module explained how microscopes form images and what limits their resolution. This topic is about the work of making a microscopy experiment succeed: preparing the specimen so that it shows the biology rather than the preparation, labelling the structures of interest, choosing the imaging method, and turning the images into measurements that can be compared between samples.

The chapters follow the order of the experiment. They begin with fixation and immunofluorescence for fixed specimens, then turn to live-cell imaging, to the confocal and super-resolution methods that Hell and Wichmann and Betzig and colleagues developed, and to electron microscopy protocols. The later chapters treat correlative imaging, which combines two methods on the same specimen, and the quantification of images, where the risk of bias is greatest.

Ioannidis (2005) pointed out that a striking image can persuade more than the data behind it justify. The module therefore treats the choice of field, the processing of images and the reporting of settings as parts of the method, not as afterthoughts.

Readers should keep the instrumentation of the previous topic in mind, since the same detectors and optics are used here, and the choices made at the bench determine what the instrument can show.
