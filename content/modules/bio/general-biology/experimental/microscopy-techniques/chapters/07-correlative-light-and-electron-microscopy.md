Light microscopy shows where a structure is and how it behaves, while electron microscopy shows what it looks like at the level of membranes and filaments. Correlative methods combine the two on the same specimen. A fluorescent marker, such as a protein fused to a fluorescent tag, identifies a region of interest in a living or fixed cell, and the same region is then examined in the electron microscope. The combination answers questions that neither method can answer alone, such as the identity of an ultrastructure seen in the electron image.

The registration of the two images is the main difficulty. The specimen must be marked so that the region can be found again after processing, and the processing itself can distort the tissue. Fiducial markers, such as particles visible in both microscopes, provide reference points. The transformation between the two images is calculated from these points and applied to the fluorescence image.

The fluorescent signal may be lost or changed during electron microscopy preparation. Some fluorescent proteins survive fixation and resin embedding, while others do not, and the chosen label must be tested. Betzig and colleagues (2006) imaged fluorescent proteins in cells, and the same proteins are often the labels used in correlative work, but their survival through the electron preparation must be confirmed.

Ioannidis (2005) described the risk of assuming that the structure seen in the electron image is the structure that was labelled. The correlation must be checked, for example by showing that the fluorescent signal lies within a defined membrane compartment in the electron image.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the membrane structures that the correlation is designed to identify. The protocol should record the markers used, the registration method and the accuracy of the overlay.
