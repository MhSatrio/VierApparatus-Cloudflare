Fixation stops the processes of the living cell and holds its molecules in place, so that they can be labelled and imaged. The most common fixative is formaldehyde, which cross-links proteins and preserves most structures well. Alcohols such as methanol precipitate and extract proteins, which preserves some epitopes that aldehydes destroy but disturbs membranes and soluble proteins. The choice depends on what must be seen. A protocol that keeps the organelle of interest intact may remove the protein to be detected, and the reverse is also true.

Fixation is not a neutral step. Cross-linking can mask an epitope, so that an antibody no longer binds, and the masked antigen may appear absent when it is present. Antigen retrieval, which uses heat or enzymes to reverse cross-links, can recover some signals but can also damage the structure. Controls that lack the primary antibody, and that use a known positive specimen, show whether the labelling is specific and whether the fixation allows detection.

Permeabilisation allows antibodies to enter the cell. Detergents such as Triton X-100 dissolve membranes, and their concentration determines which compartments remain accessible. Blocking with serum or protein reduces the nonspecific binding of antibodies, and insufficient blocking produces a diffuse background that can be mistaken for specific signal.

Ioannidis (2005) described how methods with many flexible steps produce results that are sensitive to those steps. The fixation, permeabilisation and blocking conditions should be standardised, and their effect should be tested in a pilot experiment before the main study.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular structures that fixation must preserve. The protocol should record the fixative, its concentration, the temperature and the duration, since each of these affects the result.
