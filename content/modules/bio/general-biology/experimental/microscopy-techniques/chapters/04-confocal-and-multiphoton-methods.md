A confocal microscope places a pinhole in front of the detector, so that only light from the focal plane reaches it. The optical section is thin, and a stack of sections can be combined into a three-dimensional image. The size of the pinhole sets the trade-off between resolution and signal: a small pinhole gives a thinner section with less light, and a larger one gives more light but a thicker section. The choice depends on the question, and the setting must be recorded.

Multiphoton microscopy excites fluorescence by the simultaneous absorption of two or more photons of longer wavelength, which is possible only at the focus of a pulsed laser, where the intensity is high. The excitation is confined to the focal volume, so no pinhole is needed to achieve sectioning, and infrared light penetrates tissue more deeply with less scattering. The method is therefore suited to thick and living tissue, and it causes less damage to the surrounding specimen.

Hell and Wichmann (1994) showed that the diffraction limit can be exceeded by controlling where fluorescence is emitted, and confocal and multiphoton methods use related ideas to confine the excitation. The methods are not super-resolution, and their lateral resolution remains limited by the wavelength and the objective.

The practical settings matter. Laser power must be enough to give adequate signal without bleaching or damaging the specimen. The detector gain must be set within its linear range. The sampling of the stack must satisfy the sampling theorem, so that the reconstruction is faithful to the specimen. Each of these choices affects the quantitative results.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the tissue structures that are imaged in depth. Ioannidis (2005) described the consequences of unreported settings, since images acquired with different parameters cannot be compared, and the parameters must be recorded with each image.
