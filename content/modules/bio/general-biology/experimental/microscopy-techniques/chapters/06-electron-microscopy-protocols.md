Electron microscopy requires specimens that survive a high vacuum and an electron beam. Standard protocols fix the tissue chemically, usually with glutaraldehyde followed by osmium tetroxide, which cross-links and stains membranes. The specimen is dehydrated through a series of solvents, infiltrated with a resin, and polymerised into a block. Ultrathin sections, of about seventy nanometres, are cut with a diamond knife and stained with heavy metals such as uranium and lead, which scatter electrons and give contrast. Hooke (1665) first examined sections of tissue, and the principle of thin sectioning is the same, though the scale is now far smaller.

Each step changes the specimen. Chemical fixation takes time to penetrate and can produce artefacts in regions far from the surface, and dehydration can shrink and distort soft structures. The protocol must be chosen with the structure in mind, and the artefacts must be recognised by comparison with better-preserved material.

High-pressure freezing and rapid plunge freezing preserve specimens by forming vitreous ice, in which water does not crystallise. The method keeps the structure close to its living state, and it avoids many of the artefacts of chemical fixation. Cryo-electron microscopy images frozen specimens directly, and the reconstruction of structures from many images depends on the uniformity of the frozen sample.

Staining enhances contrast but can also introduce artefacts. The heavy metals bind to specific structures, and the uniformity of staining must be checked across the section. Controls that compare stained and unstained sections, and that repeat the preparation on independent samples, guard against artefacts.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a single well-preserved image can be unrepresentative of the sample. Quantitative electron microscopy requires systematic sampling of sections and blinded counting, so that the images chosen do not determine the result.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the membrane and organelle structures that these protocols reveal. The protocol should record the fixative, the freezing method, the sectioning thickness and the staining, since each affects what can be seen.
