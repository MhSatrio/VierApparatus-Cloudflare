Every step of a purification needs a measure of the total protein, because the yield and the purification factor are calculated from it. The Bradford assay, described by Bradford (1976), is one of the most used methods. A dye, Coomassie Brilliant Blue, binds to protein and shifts its absorbance maximum from about 465 to about 595 nanometres. The increase in absorbance at 595 nanometres is proportional to the amount of protein within a useful range, and it is read against a standard curve made with a protein of known concentration, usually bovine serum albumin.

The standard curve must be prepared with the same buffer as the samples, since the buffer affects the response. A curve is built from several concentrations spanning the expected range, and the unknowns must fall within the linear part of the curve. Samples outside the range are diluted and measured again, and the dilution is included in the calculation.

Interfering substances must be controlled. Detergents, reducing agents and some buffers change the dye response, and a sample containing them can give a false reading. The effect is checked by adding the same substances to the standards. Where the interference cannot be avoided, a different assay or a precipitation step to remove the interfering material is used.

The response also depends on the protein. Dye binding is stronger for some amino acid compositions than for others, so an assay calibrated with one protein gives an approximate result for another. This limits the accuracy, and the calibration protein should be recorded.

Ioannidis (2005) described how an approximate measure can appear precise when reported to many decimal places, and the uncertainty of the assay should be stated with each result. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the amino acid composition that determines how a protein binds dyes.
