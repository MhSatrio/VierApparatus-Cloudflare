A purification usually uses more than one chromatographic step, and the order of the steps matters. The Chromatography module described the separations in detail, and the question here is how they are combined. Each step should remove a different class of contaminant, so that the steps do not overlap in what they retain. A first step that captures the target from a large volume is called the capture step, and it is chosen for capacity and for a strong differentiating property, such as ion exchange when the target is strongly charged. An intermediate step removes the contaminants of similar charge or size that remain, and a polishing step removes the final traces and any aggregates.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the properties of a protein follow from its structure, and the separations exploit different ones: charge, size and binding. A sequence in which each step uses a different property is more likely to separate the target from the contaminants than a sequence that repeats the same property, which is the logic Platt (1964) recommended for discriminating between alternatives.

The buffers must be compatible. A protein eluted from one column with a high salt concentration is often loaded directly onto the next, and the salt must be reduced or the pH adjusted for the second step to bind. Buffer exchange by dialysis or gel filtration is therefore a routine part of the sequence, and it costs yield and time, so it is planned in advance.

The sequence is judged by the purity after each step. A step that does not improve purity may be unnecessary, and a step that loses a large fraction of the target may be placed too early in the sequence. The order is adjusted after pilot runs.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a sequence chosen without testing can give results that were not anticipated, and each proposed order should be tested on a small scale before committing the whole preparation.
