A purification is judged by numbers, and the numbers are arranged in a table with one row for each step. The total activity of a fraction is the activity measured in the assay multiplied by the volume, and the total protein is the concentration multiplied by the volume. The specific activity is the total activity divided by the total protein, and it measures how much activity each unit of protein carries. A rising specific activity shows that the target is being enriched relative to the contaminants.

The yield at each step is the total activity of that step divided by the total activity of the starting extract, expressed as a percentage. The purification factor is the specific activity of the step divided by the specific activity of the starting extract. These two quantities together describe the purification: the purification factor shows how much the target has been enriched, and the yield shows how much of it has survived. A step with a high purification factor and a low yield may be too costly, and a step with a high yield and no purification may be unnecessary.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein's activity depends on its structure, and a loss of activity during a step can reflect denaturation rather than loss of material. The total activity can therefore fall without any physical loss, and the protein assay may not reveal it. This ambiguity is reduced by measuring activity in every step under the same conditions.

Platt (1964) recommended that data should be arranged so that they discriminate between alternatives, and the purification table does this by showing exactly where the target and the contaminants behave differently.

Ioannidis (2005) warned that a result can appear to have a high purification factor because the starting extract had low activity from an assay problem, and the calculations should be checked for this. The table should be reported in full, with all steps.
