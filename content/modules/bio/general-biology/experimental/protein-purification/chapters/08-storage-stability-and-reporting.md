A purified protein is often less stable than it was in the cell, because the components that protected it have been removed. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the native fold is stabilised by its environment, and in storage the environment is chosen to maintain that fold. The buffer is kept at the pH of greatest stability, the ionic strength is set to avoid aggregation and reducing agents are added when the protein has cysteine residues that oxidise. Glycerol at about ten to fifty per cent is a common additive, because it lowers the freezing point and reduces damage from ice formation.

Storage temperature is chosen according to the protein and the period. Many proteins are kept at minus eighty degrees Celsius for long periods, and aliquots are used to avoid repeated freezing and thawing, which denatures many proteins. Some proteins are unstable when frozen and are kept at four degrees Celsius for short periods, with the risk of microbial growth controlled by a preservative or sterile filtration.

Stability must be tested rather than assumed. The activity is measured at intervals after storage under the chosen conditions, and the rate of loss determines the shelf life. Ioannidis (2005) emphasised that an assumed stability can lead to results that cannot be reproduced when the stored material has changed, and the activity of the stored sample should be checked before it is used in a key experiment.

The report of a purification is part of the method. It should state the source, the assay and its conditions, each step with its volume, total protein, total activity, specific activity, yield and purification factor, the purity by SDS-PAGE and the storage conditions. Platt (1964) argued that a claim is strengthened when it is tested against a clear alternative, and the report should give the evidence that supports the identity and purity of the target.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell biology that the purified protein is taken from, and the report should state how closely the purified form resembles the protein in the cell.
