A purification begins with the question of how the target will be recognised in a mixture of many thousands of proteins. The answer is an assay, a measurement that responds to the target and to nothing else in a useful way. An enzyme is followed by its catalytic activity, a binding protein by its ability to bind a ligand, and a protein without a known function by an antibody or a tag. The assay must be specific enough that contaminants do not contribute to it, and it must be sensitive enough to detect the target at every step.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the activity of a protein depends on its folded structure, and an assay that requires the native fold can be lost during purification if the conditions are harsh. The assay must therefore be run under conditions that preserve activity, and its stability over the time of the purification must be tested. A target that loses activity during handling cannot be followed, and the loss cannot be distinguished from a failure of the purification.

The source must be chosen so that the target is present at a useful level. Tissues or cells that overexpress the protein give more material, and a recombinant source can supply it in quantity. The choice affects what contaminants are present, and it should be recorded with the purification.

Platt (1964) argued that a method is most informative when its steps distinguish between outcomes, and the assay should be designed to show whether each step has removed the contaminants it was intended to remove. The plan should state the success criteria in advance, including the required purity, the yield and the specific activity.

Ioannidis (2005) described how criteria chosen after the results are known can make a purification appear successful, and the criteria written at the start protect against this. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the sources in which target proteins are found.
