The first physical step of a purification is to break the cells and release their contents into a buffer. The choice of method depends on the cell type. Soft cells, such as cultured mammalian cells, can be lysed with a detergent or by repeated freezing and thawing. Bacteria are usually disrupted by sonication or by a French press, which forces the cells through a narrow valve under high pressure. Plant and fungal cells, with their walls, often need grinding or enzymatic digestion first. The method should release the target fully while doing as little damage as possible, since heat and foaming denature many proteins.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein's activity depends on its folded structure, which is sensitive to temperature, pH and the presence of denaturing agents. The disruption is therefore carried out cold, usually at about four degrees Celsius, in a buffer that holds the pH near the value at which the target is stable. Reducing agents may be added to protect cysteine residues, and protease inhibitors are added to prevent the target from being digested by proteases released from the cell.

Clarification removes the debris. The crude lysate is centrifuged at high speed to pellet membranes, cell walls and insoluble material, and the supernatant is retained. Filtration through a membrane of a defined pore size removes fine particles that would block a column. The clarified extract is then loaded onto the first separation step.

The lysate contains everything the cell contains, so its volume and total protein are large. Recording the volume and the total protein at this stage gives the starting point against which all later yields are calculated.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular compartments whose disruption is required. Ioannidis (2005) pointed out that the disruption step is a common source of variation, and its conditions should be recorded in full.
