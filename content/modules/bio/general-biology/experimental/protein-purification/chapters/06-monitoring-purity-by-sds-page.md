SDS-PAGE is the most common check on purity during a purification. A sample from each step is denatured and run beside a standard of known molecular masses, as Laemmli (1970) described. The target should appear as a band of the expected size, and the bands of contaminants should decrease as the purification proceeds. The method is simple and fast, and it reveals contaminants of different sizes that the activity assay cannot see. For small peptides, the Tricine system of Schägger and von Jagow (1987) gives better resolution.

The bands are interpreted by position and by intensity. A band at the expected mass is assigned to the target only if the activity co-purifies with it, and the assignment is strengthened by a band that shifts or disappears with a specific treatment. The intensity of the target band increases as the purification proceeds, and the intensity of contaminant bands falls. A purity estimate can be made by densitometry, as described in the Electrophoresis module, although the stain may respond differently to different proteins.

A single band does not prove purity. Contaminants of the same size as the target co-migrate with it, and a band can contain several proteins. Subunits of a complex appear as separate bands, and a target that has been partly cleaved can give a band that looks like a contaminant. The preparation is therefore checked by a second method, such as mass spectrometry or a second assay, which tests a different property.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a gel that shows a clean band can be presented as proof of purity when it is only a single line of evidence. The full gel with the standards should be retained, and the claim should be limited to what the gel shows.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the subunit structures of proteins that give multiple bands. The record should include the sample at each step, the load, the stain and the purity estimate with its limits.
