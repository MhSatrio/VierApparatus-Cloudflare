# Protein Purification

Purifying a protein means separating it from the thousands of other proteins in a cell until the target is pure enough to study. The chromatography and electrophoresis modules described the separations and the checks on each. This topic treats purification as a complete strategy: how the work is planned, how the target is followed through each step and how the result is judged and reported.

The most important decision is the assay. A purification is followed by a measure of the target's activity or of a property unique to it, because total protein cannot identify the target. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the folded structure of a protein determines its activity, and the assay must be performed under conditions that preserve that structure. Platt (1964) argued that a sequence of tests should be designed to discriminate between outcomes, and a purification is a sequence in which each step is chosen to exclude the contaminants that remain.

The chapters follow the sequence of work. Planning and the choice of assay come first, followed by cell disruption and clarification, protein quantification, precipitation and concentration, the chromatographic sequence, the checks on purity and the calculation of yield and specific activity. The final chapter covers storage and the reporting of the purification so that another laboratory can repeat it.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a purification reported without its yields and assay conditions cannot be judged, and the reporting of each step is treated as part of the method rather than an afterthought.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular sources of the proteins that purifications target.
