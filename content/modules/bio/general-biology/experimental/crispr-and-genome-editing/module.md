# CRISPR and Genome Editing

The Gene Expression, DNA Replication and Repair, and Genetics modules described how the genome is copied, repaired and read. Genome editing makes deliberate changes to it. The CRISPR system, first understood as a bacterial defence against viruses, was shown by Jinek et al. (2012) to be a programmable enzyme that cuts DNA at a site defined by an RNA guide. Within a year, Cong et al. (2013) and Mali et al. (2013) had adapted the system to edit the genomes of mammalian cells, and it became the most widely used editing tool in the life sciences.

The chapters follow the work of an edit. The origin of the system and the design of the guide come first, followed by the cut and the repair outcomes that determine what the edit becomes. Multiplex and base editing extend the approach, and the later chapters address validation, off-target effects and the reporting of an edited line. Each stage is a point at which an apparently successful edit can be misread.

The central risk is the gap between the intended edit and the one actually made. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that is accepted without verification can mislead, and in genome editing the verification is the central step. Platt (1964) argued that a claim should be tested in a way that could show it to be wrong, and sequencing the edited locus and its predicted off-target sites is that test.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the repair pathways that resolve the break, and those pathways set the outcomes that every edit must reckon with.
