Genome editing raises ethical questions that depend on the target of the edit. A somatic edit changes cells of an individual without passing the change to offspring, and its effects end with that individual. A heritable edit changes the germline and is inherited by future generations, which raises questions that reach beyond the person or animal edited. Cong et al. (2013) demonstrated editing in cultured cells, which is the setting in which most research is done, and the ethics of research use depends on the consent, the welfare of animals and the potential for application that the work may have.

The ethics of research also include the honest reporting of what was done. A reader must be able to judge whether the reported outcome was achieved, and a selective report that omits failed edits or unintended changes gives a false impression of the method. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that is reported without the full set of attempts cannot be trusted, and the report of an editing project should include the number of guides tested, the clones examined and the outcomes that did not succeed.

The report of an edited line states the cell line and its source, the guides with their sequences and scores, the donor template if used and the delivery method. The sequence of each allele at the edited locus is given, together with the zygosity, the off-target sites examined and the method and limit of detection. The passage of the line, its authentication and its mycoplasma status are reported, as described for cell culture.

Platt (1964) recommended that a claim be stated so that it can be tested. An edited line is described well only if another laboratory can obtain the same edit and check it.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the heritable genome and the germline, whose distinction underlies the ethical difference between the two kinds of edit.
