An off-target effect is a change made at a site other than the intended target. The guide tolerates some mismatches, particularly outside its seed region, and the enzyme may cut sites that resemble the target without being identical to it. Mali et al. (2013) observed such activity in their early experiments, and the problem has been studied since. The risk is that an unintended change in a gene may produce the phenotype that is then attributed to the intended edit.

Validation has two parts. The predicted off-target sites are sequenced, and the frequency of changes at each is measured. Unbiased methods, which search the genome for cut sites without relying on the prediction, reveal sites the prediction missed. The choice of method depends on the sensitivity required and the cost, and the method used is reported with its limit of detection.

The on-target edit must also be confirmed on each allele. A cell may carry the intended change on one allele and an unintended change on the other, and a clone judged only by one allele would be misclassified. The sequence of both alleles is read, and the zygosity is reported. A clean line is one in which the intended edit is present on the expected alleles and no unintended change is found at the sites examined.

Platt (1964) argued that a validation should be able to fail, and the search for off-target sites is designed to reveal an error that would otherwise remain hidden. Ioannidis (2005) described how a negative search that is not sensitive enough is taken as proof of absence, and the detection limit of the method must be stated so that a negative result is interpreted correctly.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the genome features that make some sites more prone to cutting than others, and these explain why the predictions are imperfect.
