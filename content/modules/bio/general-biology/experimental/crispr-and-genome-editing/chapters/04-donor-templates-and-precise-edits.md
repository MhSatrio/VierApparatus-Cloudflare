A precise edit requires a template, and the template is a DNA molecule carrying the desired sequence flanked by stretches that match the genome on either side of the cut. These homology arms are the region through which the cell recognises where the new sequence belongs. Watson and Crick (1953) established that complementary strands pair with their match, and the homology arms rely on the same pairing to align the donor with the broken ends. Their length and the exact sequence determine how efficiently the template is used.

Mali et al. (2013) demonstrated that a donor carrying a defined change can be used to introduce it into human cells, and the efficiency of the precise edit was much lower than the efficiency of the cut. The donor may be single-stranded or double-stranded, and it may be delivered as a plasmid or a short oligonucleotide. Each form has a different efficiency and a different risk of random integration, and the choice is reported.

A cut that is not protected will be re-cut after the edit is made, because the donor sequence still matches the guide. The donor is therefore designed to change the protospacer adjacent motif or the guide match, so that the edited locus is no longer a target. This protection is a central feature of a precise edit, and its absence is a common reason an edit disappears from a population over time.

Selection of precise edits is difficult, because the desired clone is rare among many cells. Single-cell cloning, followed by sequencing of each clone, identifies the correct edit, and the sequence of both alleles must be confirmed. A clone carrying the edit on one allele only is heterozygous, and this must be stated.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a rare outcome requires a large number of clones to be examined before it can be claimed, and the screening effort should be reported. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the homologous recombination machinery that uses the template.
