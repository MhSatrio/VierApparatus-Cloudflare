CRISPR stands for clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats, a pattern of repeated DNA sequences separated by short unique spacers that was found in the genomes of many bacteria. The spacers are copies of DNA from viruses that previously infected the cell. When the same virus returns, the bacterium transcribes the array into short RNAs, which guide Cas proteins to destroy matching DNA. The system is an acquired immune memory, and it was recognised as such before its molecular mechanism was known.

Jinek et al. (2012) showed that the guide RNA can be made of two parts, a CRISPR RNA and a trans-activating RNA, and that a single protein, Cas9, uses this pair to cut the DNA that matches the guide. The protein recognises a short sequence next to the target, called the protospacer adjacent motif, and it cuts both strands of the DNA at a defined position. The guide defines the site, and the enzyme does the cutting, which separates the two functions and makes the system programmable.

The protein's three-dimensional structure allows the guide to bind and the DNA to be unwound and cut. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the activity of a protein depends on its fold, and the folded architecture of Cas9 is what allows the RNA guide and the DNA to be held in the correct geometry for cutting.

Watson and Crick (1953) described the double helix whose two strands are separated and matched by the guide. The principle that a complementary sequence binds its partner is the basis of the guide design.

The biological origin of the system explains its limitations. The enzyme evolved to cut viral DNA in bacteria, and its behaviour in the genome of a mammalian cell must be measured rather than assumed. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular environment in which the editing takes place.
