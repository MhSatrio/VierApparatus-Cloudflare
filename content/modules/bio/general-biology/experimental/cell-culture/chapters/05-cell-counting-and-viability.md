A cell count gives the number of cells per millilitre, and it determines how many cells are seeded into an experiment. A haemocytometer is a glass slide with a grid of known volume, and cells counted in the squares are converted to a density by the volume of the grid and the dilution. Automated counters give the same measure faster, but their results depend on the gating and the size range chosen, and the settings should be recorded. Hayflick and Moorhead (1961) counted cells to follow the growth of their cultures, and the same measure underlies the population doubling calculation.

Viability is the proportion of cells that are alive. Trypan blue is a dye that enters cells with damaged membranes and stains them blue, while live cells exclude it and remain clear. The viable fraction is the clear cells divided by all cells. A count without viability can be misleading, because a culture that appears dense may contain many dead cells that would not survive in an experiment.

Counting is a source of error. Clumps are counted as single cells, and cells that are not evenly suspended give different values in each square. The suspension must be mixed thoroughly, and several squares are counted and averaged. The dilution must lie within the range where counting is precise.

Platt (1964) argued that a measurement should be designed so that it can reveal an error, and the viability stain serves that purpose by distinguishing a healthy culture from one that only appears full. A density that is high but not viable would give a false impression of growth.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the apparent number of cells can be treated as exact when it is an estimate with substantial error, and the count and its variation should be reported. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell death pathways that trypan blue reveals.
