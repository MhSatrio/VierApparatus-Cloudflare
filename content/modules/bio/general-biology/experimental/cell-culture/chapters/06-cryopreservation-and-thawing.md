Freezing preserves a culture so that it can be restarted at a known passage, and it protects the work against loss by contamination or equipment failure. The danger during freezing is the formation of ice. Ice crystals inside a cell disrupt its membranes and organelles, and ice outside the cell draws water from it, shrinking it and concentrating its solutes. A cryoprotectant, most often dimethyl sulphoxide at about ten per cent, reduces the crystal damage, and the cells are cooled at a controlled rate, typically about one degree per minute, before transfer to liquid nitrogen.

The rate of cooling must be right. Too fast a cooling traps water inside the cell and forms intracellular ice, while too slow a cooling allows prolonged exposure to the concentrated solution. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the structure of a protein depends on its environment, and the protein damage that occurs during freezing reflects the same sensitivity. A freezing container that cools at the correct rate is the usual way to achieve this.

Thawing must be rapid. The vial is warmed quickly in a water bath until only a small ice fragment remains, and the cells are transferred into warm medium so that the cryoprotectant is diluted. Dimethyl sulphoxide is toxic at room temperature, so the cells are washed or diluted promptly. Viability is measured after thawing, and recovery varies with the line.

Hayflick and Moorhead (1961) showed the value of banked cells, since a culture kept at high passage can be replaced by one thawed from an early stock. The record should show which stock was used, its passage and its freezing date.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the variability of freezing and thawing can change a result, and the stock should be authenticated after each thaw. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the membranes whose damage makes the cells vulnerable to ice.
