A culture medium supplies everything that a cell cannot make for itself. The basal salts provide the inorganic ions that maintain the membrane potential and the osmotic balance. Glucose supplies energy, amino acids supply the building blocks of protein, and vitamins supply cofactors for enzymes. A buffer, usually bicarbonate in an atmosphere of carbon dioxide, keeps the pH stable as the cells produce acid. Hayflick and Moorhead (1961) worked with media of this kind, and the composition of the basal medium determines which cells can survive and divide.

Serum is added to many media. It is the fluid fraction of blood, and it contains growth factors, hormones, lipids and binding proteins that support proliferation. Its composition varies from batch to batch, and the same serum can behave differently in two laboratories. Serum is also a source of contamination with viruses and of proteins that affect the experiment, which makes it a variable that must be controlled.

Defined and serum-free media replace the serum with a known set of components. They reduce batch variation and allow the experimenter to know which factors are present, and they are increasingly used for work in which the signalling of a specific factor is being studied. The defined medium is itself a model, and the cells may respond differently in it than in serum.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that proteins fold under the conditions of their environment, and the ionic strength and pH of the medium influence the structure and activity of the proteins the cells secrete and respond to. The composition of the medium is therefore part of the experimental conditions.

Ioannidis (2005) described how an unreported change in the medium, including a new batch of serum, can change results, and the source, lot and composition should always be recorded. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the nutrients and signals that the medium is designed to replace.
