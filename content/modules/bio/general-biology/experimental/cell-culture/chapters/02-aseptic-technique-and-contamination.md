A cell culture is a medium that suits many organisms as well as the cells, and bacteria and fungi grow in it faster than the cells do. Aseptic technique is the set of practices that prevents them from entering the culture. Work is done in a biological safety cabinet with its airflow directed away from the operator and the culture, surfaces and gloves are disinfected, and only sterile media, pipettes and vessels are used. The cabinet and its airflow must be checked, since a poorly maintained cabinet can itself be a source of contamination.

Contamination is recognised by its signs. Bacteria produce turbidity and often a sudden drop in the pH, which turns the indicator in the medium yellow. Fungi form filaments or spores that are visible under the microscope. Mycoplasma is more difficult, because it does not cause turbidity and can change the behaviour of the cells without obvious damage. The cells may also show a loss of growth or a change in morphology, and these changes should prompt testing.

Hayflick and Moorhead (1961) relied on careful culture of human diploid cells, and the history of culture shows that contamination has repeatedly invalidated published work. Cross-contamination by another cell line is a further risk, and the handling of several lines at the same time requires that each is kept separate and labelled.

When contamination is found, the culture is usually discarded rather than treated, because antibiotics can mask the problem and select for resistant organisms. The lab should keep a stock frozen before the contamination appeared, so that the work can be restarted from a clean source.

Ioannidis (2005) described how errors that are hidden by treatment or ignored can produce results that do not replicate, and the handling of contamination should be recorded in the laboratory notebook. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell surfaces that microbes exploit to enter the body, a parallel that explains why the culture is vulnerable.
