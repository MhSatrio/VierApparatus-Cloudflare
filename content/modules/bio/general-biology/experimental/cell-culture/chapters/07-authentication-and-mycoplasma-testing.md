A cell line is named for the cells from which it was derived, but over years of passage in many laboratories its identity can change. Lines have been found to be other lines, and a result from a misidentified line describes the wrong biology. Authentication confirms the identity by comparing a genetic profile with a reference. The most common profile is short tandem repeat analysis, which measures the length of repeated sequences at a set of loci. Each individual gives a characteristic pattern, and the pattern of the culture is matched against the reference deposited for the line.

The amplification of these loci is a PCR, the same method described by Saiki et al. (1988), and the fragment lengths are read by capillary electrophoresis. A match of the profile to the reference, with the mismatches counted, gives the authentication. Ioannidis (2005) described how the identity of a material must be established before its results can be interpreted, and authentication is that step for cell culture.

Mycoplasma is a bacterium with no cell wall, which passes through standard filters and does not make the medium turbid. It alters gene expression, metabolism and the response to treatment without visible signs, and it can be present in a large proportion of cultures. Testing is done by PCR, which amplifies a conserved mycoplasma sequence, or by a DNA-binding stain that reveals the organism's DNA in the cytoplasm. A positive culture is usually discarded and replaced with a clean stock, since treatment is often incomplete.

Platt (1964) argued that a test is most informative when it could give a clear negative, and mycoplasma and identity tests are of this kind, since a clean result is a strong statement about the culture.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell surface and genome features that the profiles and the PCR target. The laboratory record should include the date of each test, its method and the reference used.
