Cells in culture are passaged when they reach a density at which growth slows or contact between them begins to alter their behaviour. Adherent cells are detached from the surface, usually with a protease such as trypsin or with a chelating agent that removes the calcium needed for adhesion, washed, counted and transferred to a fresh vessel at a chosen split ratio. The enzyme must be inactivated or washed away, since prolonged exposure damages the surface proteins and reduces viability. Suspension cultures are diluted into fresh medium without detachment.

The split ratio sets the interval until the next passage. A ratio of one to four passaged every three days suits many lines, and a higher ratio gives a longer interval. The choice affects the density at which the cells are harvested, and the density influences their state. Hayflick and Moorhead (1961) observed that normal cells slow their division as the culture ages, and the density at passage is part of the conditions that the experimenter must control.

The passage number counts how many times the culture has been split since it was established. It is recorded for every experiment, because cells change with passage. A line used at passage five may behave differently from the same line at passage fifty, through drift in gene expression or the selection of subpopulations. Experiments should be performed within a defined passage range, and the range should be stated.

Passaging also introduces risk. Each transfer exposes the cells to contamination and to the variation of the detachment step, and the number of passages should be kept as low as the needs of the work allow. A frozen stock, described in a later chapter, provides a source that returns the culture to a low passage number.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a result obtained at one passage may not be obtained at another, and the passage number should be part of the record. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell adhesion that the detachment step disrupts.
