Cell culture removes cells from a tissue and supplies them with the nutrients, gases and surfaces they need to survive and divide. A primary culture is taken directly from tissue and reflects the cell type it came from, but it is a mixture that changes with each passage and cannot be expanded indefinitely. An established cell line has been selected or transformed so that it divides continuously, which makes it convenient and reproducible, but it may differ substantially from the cells in the body.

Hayflick and Moorhead (1961) showed that normal human diploid cells divide only a limited number of times before they stop, a phenomenon now known as replicative senescence. The finding established that cultured normal cells have a finite lifespan, and that a continuously dividing line has usually lost this constraint through some change. The distinction matters because a result obtained from an immortalised line may reflect the change that made it immortal rather than the normal cell.

The cell in culture is also removed from its tissue. Neighbouring cells, the extracellular matrix, mechanical forces and gradients of oxygen and signals are all absent or altered, and these affect gene expression and behaviour. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the interactions with the matrix and with other cells that the culture replaces with an artificial substrate.

The culture should therefore be described as a model. The question is whether the model is adequate for the claim being made, and a finding in culture is an approximation of the behaviour in the body rather than a direct report of it.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the convenience of a cell line can encourage generalisation beyond what the line represents, and the source and passage of each culture should be stated. The chapters on authentication and reporting give the means to make this clear.
