# Cell Culture

Cells grown outside the organism make it possible to study a single cell type under controlled conditions, and most of the experiments in this curriculum rely on them at some stage. The Microscopy and Flow Cytometry topics examine cells that have been cultured, and the Cell Biology module described the cells themselves. This topic treats culture as a method in its own right: the environment that keeps cells alive, the discipline that keeps them free of contamination and the records that show what was done.

The chapters follow the working life of a culture. The principles of culture come first, starting from the work of Hayflick and Moorhead (1961), who showed that normal human cells divide a limited number of times in culture. Aseptic technique, media and supplements, passaging, counting and viability follow. The later chapters cover cryopreservation, authentication and contamination testing, and the reporting of a culture so that the experiment can be repeated.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a biological result from a cell line that has drifted or been contaminated can be entirely artefactual, and authentication is a precondition for trusting the result. Platt (1964) argued that the most informative experiment is one that can distinguish between explanations, and the controls in culture serve that purpose.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell biology that the cultures are meant to reproduce, and the chapters identify where culture departs from the cell in the organism.
