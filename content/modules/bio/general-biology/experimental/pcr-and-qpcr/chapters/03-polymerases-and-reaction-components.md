The polymerase is the enzyme that makes the PCR practical. Early amplification used a polymerase that was destroyed at the denaturation temperature, so fresh enzyme had to be added after every cycle. Saiki et al. (1988) isolated a thermostable polymerase from a bacterium that lives in hot springs, and the enzyme survives repeated heating to near boiling, which allows the reaction to be run in a closed tube with all the reagents present from the start. Mullis and Faloona (1987) had established the principle of repeated synthesis, and the thermostable enzyme made it routine.

A reaction contains the template DNA, the two primers, the four deoxynucleoside triphosphates, the polymerase and a buffer. The deoxynucleotides are the building blocks of the new strands, and their concentration should be balanced, since an imbalance increases the error rate. The buffer maintains the pH, and magnesium ions are an essential cofactor for the polymerase.

Magnesium concentration affects both yield and specificity. Too little magnesium lowers the activity of the enzyme, and too much stabilises mismatched primer binding and increases off-target products. The optimum is usually found by titration, and it depends on the concentration of the nucleotides, since they bind magnesium.

The template must be of sufficient quality and quantity. Excessive template can inhibit the reaction, and contaminants such as heparin, haemoglobin or residual phenol carried from extraction reduce the activity of the polymerase. A dilution series confirms that the reaction responds to the template in the expected way.

Ioannidis (2005) described how reaction conditions that are not reported can prevent replication. Each component, its concentration and its supplier should be recorded, and a change in the lot of any reagent should be treated as a variable that must be tested.
