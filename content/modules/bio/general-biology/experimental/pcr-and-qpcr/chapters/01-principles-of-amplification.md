PCR copies a defined stretch of DNA by repeating three steps. The double helix described by Watson and Crick (1953) is separated by heating, which is the denaturation step. The temperature is lowered so that two short synthetic primers, each complementary to one end of the target, bind to their sites on the separated strands. The polymerase then extends each primer along the template, producing a new copy of the target between the two primers. Each cycle therefore creates new copies that can serve as templates in the next, and the amount of product approximately doubles per cycle.

Mullis and Faloona (1987) demonstrated the principle of repeated primer-directed synthesis, and Saiki et al. (1988) made it practical by using a thermostable polymerase. Because the enzyme survives the denaturation temperature, it does not have to be added again after each cycle, and the reaction can be run automatically in a thermal cycler.

The exponential phase continues while the primers and nucleotides are abundant and the enzyme is active. As the reagents are consumed and the product accumulates, the efficiency falls, and the reaction enters a plateau. Quantitative use of PCR depends on measuring the product while it is still in the exponential phase, which is the basis of real-time quantification described in a later chapter.

Each cycle has a small probability of error, and errors accumulate across the cycles. The fidelity of the product therefore depends on the polymerase and on the number of cycles, and excessive cycling can introduce artefacts and reduce the faithfulness of the final population.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the replication of DNA that PCR imitates in the cell. Ioannidis (2005) noted that the choice of cycle number can change the apparent result, and the cycle number should be fixed in the protocol.
