PCR amplifies DNA, so RNA must first be converted into a DNA copy when the target is a transcript. Reverse transcriptase, an enzyme that synthesises DNA from an RNA template, makes this copy, called complementary DNA. The reaction uses the same base-pairing logic described by Watson and Crick (1953), except that the template is RNA and the product is a DNA strand complementary to it. The product is then amplified by the PCR described by Saiki et al. (1988).

The first strand can be primed in several ways. Oligo(dT) primers bind the poly-A tail of messenger RNA and copy the transcript from the end. Random hexamers prime at many sites and copy all RNA, including molecules without a tail. A gene-specific primer gives the most targeted copy, but it copies only the chosen sequence. The choice depends on the question, and the priming method must be stated, since it changes which transcripts are represented.

In a two-step RT-PCR, the reverse transcription is done first and the complementary DNA is stored, then a portion is used in a separate amplification. This allows one complementary DNA sample to be assayed for many targets. In a one-step reaction, both steps occur in one tube, which reduces handling and the risk of contamination, but the complementary DNA cannot be reused for other targets.

Genomic DNA contamination is a central risk. If it is present in the RNA, the amplification detects the gene itself and not the transcript. A control reaction without reverse transcriptase reveals the contamination, and primers that span an intron give products of different size from genomic DNA.

Bustin (2000) emphasised these controls for quantitative work, and Alberts et al. (2022) describe the processing of messenger RNA that makes the poly-A tail a useful target. Ioannidis (2005) noted that uncontrolled contamination can produce a false result that looks like a real signal.
