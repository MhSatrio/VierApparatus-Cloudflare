The primers determine what PCR amplifies, so their design is the most important decision in the method. Each primer is a short single-stranded DNA of typically eighteen to twenty-five bases, complementary to one strand of the target. The two primers must anneal to opposite strands, facing each other, so that extension between them produces the product. Saiki et al. (1988) used primers of this kind to direct amplification of a defined sequence, and the same logic applies today. The complementarity follows the base-pairing rules that Watson and Crick (1953) established.

The melting temperature of a primer depends on its length and on its GC content, since G and C pairs are held by three hydrogen bonds and A and T pairs by two. Primers of similar melting temperature are paired so that both anneal efficiently at a common temperature. The GC content should be moderate, and runs of identical bases or a stretch of four or more G residues at the end should be avoided, because they promote mispriming.

The annealing temperature is usually set a few degrees below the lower melting temperature of the pair. Raising it improves specificity, because mismatched primers no longer bind, while lowering it increases the yield but allows off-target products. A temperature gradient run is an efficient way to find the best value for a new primer pair.

Primers can anneal to each other, forming primer-dimers, which are short products that consume reagents. They can also bind to unintended sites in the genome. Both are recognised on a gel as bands of unexpected size, and the remedy is to redesign the primers or to optimise the annealing conditions.

Bustin (2000) emphasised that primer specificity must be checked before quantitative use. Ioannidis (2005) described how a single well-amplified band can mislead when a second product is hidden in it, so the product should be confirmed by size and by a melting curve or sequencing.
