A PCR run is only as trustworthy as its controls, and a set of controls is required for every run. The no-template control contains all the reagents except the template, and any product in it reveals contamination or primer-dimer. The no-reverse-transcriptase control, used with RNA samples, reveals genomic DNA. A positive control of a known template confirms that the reaction works, and the absence of its product indicates a failed run rather than a negative sample. Saiki et al. (1988) showed that the sensitivity of the method makes such controls essential, since trace contamination is amplified along with the target.

Amplicon contamination is the most serious risk. Products from earlier runs, carried on pipettes, benches or gloves, can enter a new reaction and produce a false positive. Physical separation of pre- and post-amplification work, dedicated pipettes, filter tips and enzymatic carry-over prevention reduce this risk. Contamination is detected by the no-template control, and a contaminated run should be discarded.

Bustin (2000) set out the information that a quantitative report should contain. It includes the sample type and its handling, the RNA quality and quantity, the reverse transcription conditions, the primers and probes, the efficiency and the cycling programme. Without these, another laboratory cannot reproduce the result, and the reader cannot judge its reliability.

Ioannidis (2005) described how results that are presented without the conditions that produced them are hard to trust, and the reporting should allow the work to be repeated. Platt (1964) argued that a method is most informative when it tests alternatives directly, and the controls play this role by distinguishing a real signal from artefact.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecules that the reactions measure. The laboratory record should include each control, its result and the action taken when a control failed.
