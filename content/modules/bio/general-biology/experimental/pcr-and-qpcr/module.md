# PCR and qPCR

The polymerase chain reaction copies a specific stretch of DNA exponentially, and its quantitative form, qPCR, measures how much of a target a sample contains. The Laboratory Instrumentation and Electrophoresis modules described the equipment and the gel readout. This topic treats PCR as a complete method: the design of the reaction, the choice of enzyme and reagents, the reverse transcription of RNA, real-time quantification and the normalisation and reporting that give the numbers meaning.

The chapters follow the order in which an experiment is planned. The principle of amplification comes first, as set out by Mullis and Faloona (1987) and by Saiki et al. (1988), who showed that a thermostable polymerase makes repeated cycles practical. Primer design, reaction components and thermal cycling follow. Reverse transcription and real-time detection are then described, along with the quantification strategies, the normalisation and the controls required by current reporting guidance.

Amplification is sensitive to small changes in primers, template and contamination, and Bustin (2000) emphasised that quantitative results depend on careful technique and complete reporting. Ioannidis (2005) described how results that depend on unreported conditions are hard to reproduce, and the chapters identify the parameters that must be recorded for every run.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the genes and transcripts that PCR measures, which provide the biological context for each target.
