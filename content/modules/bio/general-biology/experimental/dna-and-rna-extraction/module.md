# DNA and RNA Extraction

Every nucleic acid experiment depends on the quality of the material extracted at the start. The Electrophoresis module described how an agarose gel reveals the size and integrity of a preparation, and the PCR module showed how contaminants inhibit amplification. This topic treats extraction as a complete method, from the breakage of cells to the storage of the purified product, with the choices at each step explained.

The chapters follow the sequence of an extraction. Lysis releases the nucleic acid, and the removal of proteins and lipids follows, using phenol-chloroform and the guanidinium method that Chomczynski and Sacchi (1987) developed for RNA. Precipitation concentrates the nucleic acid, and silica-column and magnetic-bead methods offer an alternative that is faster and more consistent. The final chapters cover the measurement of quantity and purity, and the prevention of degradation by ribonucleases, which are present in almost every laboratory environment.

Watson and Crick (1953) described the double-helical DNA whose extraction is the aim of the first step. The nucleic acid is held in a cell together with proteins, lipids and carbohydrates, and the method must separate it from these while preserving the molecule. Ioannidis (2005) noted that extraction introduces variation between preparations, and the method should be recorded in enough detail to be repeated.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular structures from which nucleic acids are released. The record should include the starting material, the method, the yields and the quality measures for each preparation.
