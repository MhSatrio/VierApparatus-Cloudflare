Chomczynski and Sacchi (1987) described a method that isolates total RNA from cells and tissues in a single step, and it has become the standard for RNA work. The reagent is a monophasic solution of guanidinium thiocyanate, a strong chaotrope, together with an acid buffer, sodium acetate, phenol and glycerol. The guanidinium salt lyses the cells, denatures proteins and inactivates ribonucleases, which are otherwise very active. The acidity of the mixture determines how the nucleic acids partition, and it is the key feature of the method.

Chloroform is added to the homogenate, which separates it into an aqueous upper phase and an organic lower phase. Under the acidic conditions, the RNA remains in the aqueous phase, while the DNA is drawn into the interphase and the organic phase together with the proteins. Chomczynski and Sacchi (1987) showed that this separation is clean enough to give RNA free of most DNA, although a DNase treatment is still advisable for sensitive applications. The aqueous phase is then removed and the RNA is precipitated with isopropanol.

Watson and Crick (1953) described the structure of DNA, and the separation depends on the different chemistry of the RNA and DNA molecules under acidic conditions. The RNA is single-stranded and forms secondary structures, and its partitioning reflects these properties.

The method requires care. The homogenate must be prepared immediately and kept cold, since RNA degrades quickly once the cell is disrupted. The phases must be separated completely, and any carry-over of the interphase reintroduces DNA and protein. The precipitated pellet is washed with ethanol, dried briefly and dissolved in water or a buffer that is free of nucleases.

Ioannidis (2005) emphasised that a method that is easy to carry out can still be applied inconsistently, and the timing, the temperature and the proportions of the reagents should be fixed and recorded. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the messenger RNA whose integrity this method protects.
