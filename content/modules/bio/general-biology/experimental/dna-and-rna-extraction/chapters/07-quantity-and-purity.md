The concentration of a nucleic acid preparation is commonly measured by its absorbance of ultraviolet light at 260 nanometres. The bases of DNA and RNA absorb strongly at this wavelength, and an absorbance of one, in a standard path length, corresponds to about fifty micrograms of double-stranded DNA per millilitre, or forty micrograms of RNA per millilitre. The measure depends on the bases, which Watson and Crick (1953) showed to be the constituents of the double helix, and it cannot distinguish a nucleic acid from other molecules that absorb at the same wavelength.

Purity is assessed by ratios. The ratio of absorbance at 260 to 280 nanometres indicates protein contamination, since proteins absorb strongly at 280. A value close to 1.8 is typical of pure DNA and close to 2.0 of pure RNA, and lower values indicate protein or phenol. The ratio of 260 to 230 nanometres indicates contamination by guanidinium salts, carbohydrates or phenol, and a value well below about 2.0 is a warning. Chomczynski and Sacchi (1987) noted that the guanidinium reagent in their method carries over into the RNA if the wash is incomplete, and the 230 ratio reveals this.

Concentration and purity do not reveal integrity. A preparation can have the right ratios and still be fragmented, and the size distribution is measured by gel electrophoresis, as described in the Electrophoresis module, or by capillary electrophoresis. Intact RNA shows distinct ribosomal bands, and its integrity can be summarised by a numerical score.

Ioannidis (2005) pointed out that a single measurement can give a false reassurance, and the choice of measures should be justified for the intended use. A preparation for PCR may be pure enough for amplification but unsuitable for long-range applications.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecules whose absorbance is measured. The record should include the concentration, both ratios and the integrity result for each preparation.
