Precipitation concentrates nucleic acid from a dilute aqueous solution and removes the salts and small molecules that remain after extraction. The principle depends on the phosphate backbone of the molecule that Watson and Crick (1953) described. Each phosphate carries a negative charge, which attracts water and keeps the molecule in solution. A monovalent cation, typically sodium from sodium acetate or ammonium from ammonium acetate, neutralises these charges, and the addition of an alcohol reduces the dielectric constant of the solution so that the nucleic acid aggregates and falls out of solution.

Ethanol is the traditional choice, and it is typically added at about two and a half volumes to a sample with the salt at a final concentration of around 0.3 molar. Isopropanol is used at about one volume and precipitates nucleic acid at a lower volume, which is convenient for large samples, but it also precipitates more salt, so the pellet must be washed carefully. The choice affects the yield and purity, and the protocol should state which alcohol and what volume were used.

The precipitate is collected by centrifugation, and the pellet is washed with cold seventy per cent ethanol to remove residual salt. The wash must be complete, because salt inhibits later enzymatic reactions. The pellet is then dried briefly, since residual alcohol also inhibits reactions, and is redissolved in a buffer. Over-drying makes the pellet hard to dissolve, and it can lead to loss of material.

Chomczynski and Sacchi (1987) used alcohol precipitation as the final step of their RNA isolation. Ioannidis (2005) pointed out that the volume of the precipitate and its appearance are not reliable indicators of yield, and the amount should be measured.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the solubility of biological molecules, which underlies the method.
