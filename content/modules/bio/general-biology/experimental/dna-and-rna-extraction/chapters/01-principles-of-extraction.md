Nucleic acids in a cell are not free in solution. DNA is packed into chromatin with histone and other proteins, and RNA is bound to ribosomes and to proteins that carry it. Membranes and carbohydrates surround all of them. An extraction must release the nucleic acid from these associations and separate it from the molecules that would interfere with its use. Watson and Crick (1953) described the structure of DNA, and the double helix is robust enough to survive careful handling, but its association with proteins must be disrupted before it can be recovered.

The chemistry of the two nucleic acids differs in ways that affect the method. DNA is stable in mildly alkaline conditions and survives heating, while RNA is readily cleaved by enzymes that are present almost everywhere, and its extraction must suppress this activity from the first step. The single-stranded nature of RNA and its association with other molecules also make it more easily lost during precipitation.

A good extraction is judged by three criteria. The yield is the amount recovered from the starting material. The purity describes the absence of proteins, phenol, salts and other contaminants that inhibit downstream reactions. The integrity describes whether the molecule is intact or has been fragmented. A method that maximises yield can sacrifice purity, and the balance must be chosen according to the intended use.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a method that is judged only by yield can select for preparations that are unrepresentative, and the criteria should be stated and measured. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell biology that determines what is present in a sample. The protocol should define the target criteria before the extraction begins.
