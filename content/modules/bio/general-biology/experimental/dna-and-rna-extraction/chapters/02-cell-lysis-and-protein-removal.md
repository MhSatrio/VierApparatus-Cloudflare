The first step of any extraction is to break the cell open. The plasma membrane is disrupted by a detergent such as sodium dodecyl sulphate, which dissolves lipids and releases the contents. Tough cells, such as plant tissue, bacteria and fungi, need mechanical disruption by grinding or bead beating, and some need enzymes that digest the cell wall. The choice of lysis is determined by the sample, and the aim is to release the nucleic acid without breaking it into small pieces, since long molecules are needed for many applications.

Proteins are the principal contaminants, because they are bound to the nucleic acid in the cell and are released with it. Proteinase K is a robust enzyme that digests proteins and remains active in detergent and at elevated temperature, which makes it useful for the lysate. Watson and Crick (1953) described the double helix, and the proteins bound to it must be destroyed before the molecule can be separated from them. The digestion is typically carried out at about fifty-five degrees Celsius for an hour or more.

Guanidinium thiocyanate is a chaotropic salt that denatures proteins and inactivates ribonucleases at once. Chomczynski and Sacchi (1987) used it in a single-step method that dissolves the cell and separates the RNA from the DNA and proteins that remain in solution. Its effectiveness against RNases makes it the standard lysis reagent when RNA is the target.

Shearing is a risk during lysis. Vigorous mixing or excessive mechanical force breaks long DNA into fragments, and this reduces the integrity measured later on a gel. The lysate should be mixed gently when high molecular weight DNA is needed.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the structures that the lysis must disrupt. Ioannidis (2005) emphasised that the lysis step is a major source of variation between preparations, and its time, temperature and reagents should be recorded.
