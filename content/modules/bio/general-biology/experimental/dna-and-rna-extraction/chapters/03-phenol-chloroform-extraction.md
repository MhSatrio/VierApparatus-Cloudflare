Phenol and chloroform separate nucleic acids from proteins and lipids by partitioning. Proteins and lipids are hydrophobic enough to dissolve in the organic phase, while nucleic acids, which carry a dense negative charge on their phosphate backbone, remain in the aqueous phase. Mixing the lysate with the organic mixture and centrifuging it produces two clear liquids separated by a white layer of denatured protein at the interface. The aqueous phase is removed and retained, and the organic phase is discarded.

The pH of the phenol is critical. Acidic phenol, buffered to about pH 4 or 5, keeps the DNA in the organic phase and the RNA in the aqueous phase, because the acidic conditions protonate the DNA and make it less soluble in water. Chomczynski and Sacchi (1987) relied on this effect to separate RNA from DNA in a single step. Neutral or alkaline phenol carries both nucleic acids into the aqueous phase, and it is used when total nucleic acid is wanted.

The phase separation is delicate. The interphase contains denatured protein and sometimes some nucleic acid, and carrying any of it into the aqueous phase reintroduces contaminants. The aqueous phase should be drawn from the top and the interphase left behind. Repeated extractions remove residual protein more completely, and each extraction should be done with the same care to avoid variation.

Watson and Crick (1953) described the molecule whose partitioning this method depends on. Ioannidis (2005) noted that the reproducibility of the method depends on the pH of the reagent and the handling of the interphase, and these must be recorded.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the chemical properties of the molecules that partition. Residual phenol inhibits later reactions, so the protocol should include a step to remove it.
