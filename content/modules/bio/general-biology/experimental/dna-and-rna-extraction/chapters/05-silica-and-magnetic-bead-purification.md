Silica-based purification binds nucleic acids to a solid surface, which allows contaminants to be washed away before the nucleic acid is released. In the presence of a high concentration of a chaotropic salt, such as guanidinium chloride, the water structure around the molecule is disrupted, and the nucleic acid adsorbs to silica. Proteins and many other molecules do not bind under these conditions and are removed in the wash. Ethanol in the wash buffer keeps the nucleic acid bound while removing the salt. A low-salt elution buffer or water then releases the molecule from the silica.

The method is delivered in two common formats. Spin columns contain a silica membrane, and the solution is forced through by centrifugation. Magnetic beads are coated with silica and are captured by a magnet, which allows the process to be automated in plates. Both give consistent results, and they avoid the handling of organic solvents that phenol-chloroform requires.

Fragment size affects recovery. Very large molecules can be sheared by the force of centrifugation and by vigorous mixing, and the beads or membrane may capture small fragments less efficiently. The binding conditions can be adjusted to recover short fragments, but the trade-off must be recorded. Watson and Crick (1953) described the length of the double helix, which makes long molecules vulnerable to such forces.

The method is easy to use, but it is not free of variation. The binding capacity of the silica is finite, and overloading the column reduces yield and purity. Ioannidis (2005) noted that a convenient kit can encourage uncritical use, and the performance of each lot should be checked against the criteria the protocol defines.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the interactions between charged molecules and surfaces, which underlie the binding chemistry.
