# Flow Cytometry

Flow cytometry measures properties of individual cells as they pass one at a time through a focused beam of light. Each cell scatters light and emits fluorescence from the labels bound to it, and the instrument records these signals for thousands of cells per second. Herzenberg et al. (2002) traced the development of the method at Stanford, where the fluorescence-activated cell sorter was built to separate cells by their labels. The result is a population description that a microscope or a bulk assay cannot give, because it shows the distribution of a property across many cells.

The chapters follow the work of a flow experiment. The principles of the measurement come first, followed by the labelling of cells with fluorescent antibodies, which builds on the principle of Coons et al. (1941). The optics and detection are then described, together with compensation for the overlap of fluorescent colours, the gating strategy that defines populations, the controls that establish their boundaries, and the sorting of cells for further study. The last chapter covers the reporting of the data.

The method produces very large data sets, and the results can be shaped by choices made in the analysis. Platt (1964) recommended that an experiment be designed to distinguish between alternatives, and the controls in flow cytometry are precisely the tests that separate a real population from artefact. Ioannidis (2005) described how analytical flexibility can produce apparent findings, and the gating and analysis must be defined before the data are examined.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell surface markers that most panels measure, which give the biological basis for the populations defined by the gates.
