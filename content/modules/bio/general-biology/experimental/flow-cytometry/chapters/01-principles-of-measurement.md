A flow cytometer draws a suspension of cells into a narrow stream of fluid, which is surrounded by a sheath so that the cells pass through a focused laser beam one at a time. Each cell scatters the light. Forward scatter, measured in the direction of the beam, rises with the size of the cell, and side scatter, measured at right angles, rises with its internal complexity, such as granularity and the structure of its organelles. Herzenberg et al. (2002) described how these two measures allowed the early instruments to separate the main populations of blood cells before any fluorescence was used.

Fluorescence is emitted by labels that are bound to the cell, and each label has a characteristic excitation and emission spectrum. The emitted light is separated by filters and mirrors onto detectors, and each detector records one colour. Every cell that passes the beam gives one value for each detector, so the data are multiparameter. A cell becomes a point in a space whose axes are its measured properties, and a population is a cloud of such points.

The signal is converted from an analogue pulse to a digital value, and the height, the area and the width of each pulse can be recorded. The width distinguishes single cells from doublets, which are two cells passing together and give a wider pulse. Ioannidis (2005) described how a data set can contain artefacts from such events, and the doublet exclusion should be part of the analysis.

The scatter measures describe the cell only roughly. The size and granularity are shared by many cell types, and a population defined by scatter alone is a heterogeneous mixture. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the organelles and cell structures that contribute to scatter, which explains why the measure is informative but not specific.

The principle thus gives a distribution, not an identity. The labels added in later steps are what make the populations specific.
