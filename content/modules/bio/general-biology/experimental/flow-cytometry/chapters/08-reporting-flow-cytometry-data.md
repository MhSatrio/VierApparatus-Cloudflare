A flow cytometry result is a set of gated percentages, and those percentages depend on choices made at every stage. Ioannidis (2005) described how such flexibility can produce findings that do not replicate, and a report is the means by which a reader can see the choices and test them. The report must allow another laboratory to repeat the panel and the analysis, and it should make clear which values are measured and which are inferred.

The panel is listed in full, with each antibody identified by its target, clone, source, fluorophore and dilution, and with the viability dye named. Herzenberg et al. (2002) showed that the reliability of multicolour work rests on this detail, since a change of fluorophore or clone alters the signal. The instrument, its lasers and filters, and the detector voltages are reported, together with the compensation matrix and the controls used to set it.

The gating strategy is reported as a sequence, with each gate defined and the boundaries justified from the controls. The number of events collected in each gate is stated, because a population of a few hundred cells gives a percentage with a wide uncertainty, and the minimum count should be set in advance. Platt (1964) argued that a conclusion should be stated in a form that could be shown false, and a percentage with its count and its confidence interval gives the reader that opportunity.

The statistics applied to the populations are described, including the test, the correction for multiple comparisons when many populations are examined and the number of independent biological replicates. The raw data files are archived so that the analysis can be repeated, which is the strongest support for any gate.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell populations that the panels characterise, and the report should state the biological state of the cells when they were analysed.
