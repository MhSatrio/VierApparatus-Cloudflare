The fluorescence-activated cell sorter was developed to do more than measure: it separates cells that match a gate and collects them. The stream is broken into droplets by a vibrating nozzle, so that each droplet holds at most one cell. When a droplet containing a cell of interest passes the detector, the instrument gives it an electrical charge, and charged plates deflect it into a collection tube. Herzenberg et al. (2002) described the instrument built at Stanford that made this possible, and the principle remains the basis of modern sorters.

Sorting is only as good as the decision made for each droplet. The cell is sorted on the basis of its measured properties, and a cell that is misclassified goes into the wrong tube. Purity is the proportion of sorted cells that truly belong to the population, and recovery is the proportion of the target cells that are collected. The two trade against each other: a stricter gate gives higher purity and lower recovery. Both are measured by re-analysing a sample of the sorted fraction, and they are reported with the sort.

Sorting stresses cells. The shear of the nozzle, the pressure, the time in the instrument and the exposure to the collection buffer can all reduce viability and alter the state of the cells. The sort is performed quickly and cold, in a medium that supports survival, and viability is measured afterwards. Cells that are damaged may show altered behaviour in later experiments, which is a source of error that is easy to overlook.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a population isolated by a sort may be reported as a clean biological entity when it is a mixture, and the purity should be stated with the results. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could show a sort to be wrong, and the re-analysis of the sorted fraction is that test.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell types that sorting isolates and the changes in state that the procedure can induce.
