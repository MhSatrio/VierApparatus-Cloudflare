Each control in a flow experiment answers a specific question, and the experiment needs all of them. The unstained control shows the autofluorescence of the cells, which varies between cell types and may be high in some. It sets the lowest level of background that the instrument records for the sample. The single-stained controls, described in the compensation chapter, measure the spillover. Platt (1964) argued that a claim is strengthened by a control that could show it false, and these controls each test a specific alternative explanation for what is seen.

The fluorescence-minus-one control contains every fluorophore of the panel except one. It shows how the negative population of that marker spreads when the other colours are present, because the spread from the other fluorophores can widen the negative population and push its upper edge into the positive region. Herzenberg et al. (2002) described the importance of this spread in multicolour work, and the boundary of a dim marker is best set with this control.

The isotype control uses an antibody of the same class and the same fluorophore, but with no specificity for the target. It shows the level of nonspecific binding of the antibody. It is widely used, but it is an imperfect guide, because the nonspecific binding of an isotype is not always the same as the binding of the specific antibody to the cells. Using the isotype alone to place a gate can produce an error in either direction, and it should be combined with the fluorescence-minus-one control.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a control that is chosen to give the desired result can mislead, and each control should be defined before the experiment. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell types whose autofluorescence and nonspecific binding differ, which explains why the unstained control must be matched to the sample.

The complete set of controls is the means by which the data are shown to mean what they are said to mean.
