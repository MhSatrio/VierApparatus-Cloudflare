A cell is labelled by adding a fluorescent antibody that binds a surface or internal marker. Coons et al. (1941) established that an antibody can carry a fluorescent group and still bind its target, and every flow panel depends on that principle. The antibody is conjugated to a fluorophore, a molecule that absorbs light at one wavelength and emits at a longer one. Herzenberg et al. (2002) showed that the use of several fluorophores with distinct spectra allows many markers to be measured on one cell at once.

A panel is a set of antibodies designed together. Each marker is assigned a fluorophore, and the assignment must match the abundance of the target to the brightness of the fluorophore. A rare marker needs a bright fluorophore to be detected above background, and an abundant marker can be assigned a dimmer one. Brightness is a property of the fluorophore and of the instrument, and the assignment is checked against the detector's sensitivity.

The concentration of each antibody is titrated. Too little antibody gives a weak signal, and too much increases nonspecific binding and background. The best concentration is the one that gives the greatest separation between the positive and negative populations, which is a ratio rather than the brightest signal. Ioannidis (2005) described how a convenient choice of reagent can be applied without testing, and the titration should be recorded for each panel.

Staining conditions also matter. The temperature, time and buffer affect the binding, and dead cells bind antibodies nonspecifically and must be excluded with a viability dye. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the surface markers that most panels target, and their expression varies with the state of the cell, so the staining is performed on cells in a defined condition.

The labelling is the step that gives the population its identity, and its quality determines the value of everything that follows.
