The optics of the instrument separate the light from each cell into its components. A laser excites the fluorophores, and the emitted light passes through a series of dichroic mirrors that reflect and transmit light according to its wavelength. Band-pass filters then select a narrow range for each detector. Each detector is a photomultiplier tube, which converts the light into an electrical pulse whose size is proportional to the number of photons received. Herzenberg et al. (2002) described how the separation of wavelengths by mirrors and filters made it possible to measure several colours at once.

The choice of filter determines which fluorophores can be measured together. Two fluorophores whose emission overlaps in one detector cannot be separated by filtering alone, and the spillover must be corrected. A fluorophore is placed on a detector where its emission peaks and where the overlap with other fluorophores is smallest. The panel is therefore designed around the instrument, and a panel that works on one machine may fail on another with different lasers or filters.

Detector voltage sets the gain of the signal. A higher voltage amplifies the pulses, which moves dim populations away from the background, but it also pushes bright populations beyond the upper limit of the detector, where the signal saturates and the measurement is lost. The voltage is set so that the brightest expected population lies on scale, and the setting is recorded and kept fixed for comparison between experiments.

Platt (1964) recommended a design that can distinguish alternatives, and the voltage setting is a test of whether the instrument can resolve the populations at all. A voltage set to make a population appear is an error, and it should be set from the controls rather than from the sample of interest.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a setting changed to improve a result can make the result an artefact of the setting. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the fluorescent molecules of biology that the optics measure.
