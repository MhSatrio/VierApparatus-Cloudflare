A spectrophotometer measures how much light of a chosen wavelength passes through a solution. The absorbance is the logarithm of the ratio of incident to transmitted light, and the Beer-Lambert law states that it is proportional to the concentration of the absorbing molecule and the path length of the light through the sample. The law allows a concentration to be calculated from a reading when the molar absorption coefficient at that wavelength is known. Lewis (1916) gave the molecule as the unit of identity, and the absorption of a particular molecule is a property of its structure.

Biological measurements use characteristic wavelengths. Nucleic acids absorb strongly at 260 nanometres, proteins at 280 nanometres because of their aromatic residues, and NADH at 340 nanometres, which allows the activity of dehydrogenases to be followed, as described in the Biochemistry module. The choice of wavelength depends on the absorption of the molecule and on the interference of other components.

The law fails in several conditions. At high concentrations, the absorbance becomes nonlinear because the molecules interact and because the detector responds nonlinearly. Stray light reaching the detector reduces the apparent absorbance at high values. Turbidity from particles scatters light, which increases the apparent absorbance, and an unrecognised contamination can produce a misleading reading. The linear range must be determined and the measurements kept within it.

Blanks must be matched to the sample. A blank that lacks a component present in the sample, such as a buffer or a reducing agent that absorbs at the same wavelength, gives an incorrect baseline. The blank should be prepared in the same way as the sample and measured under the same conditions.

Ioannidis (2005) described how measurements that are not validated can produce misleading conclusions, and spectrophotometric assays should be checked against standard curves and independent methods.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the chromophores and fluorophores of common biological molecules. Good practice is to calibrate the instrument, to determine the linear range and to report the wavelength, path length and blank used.
