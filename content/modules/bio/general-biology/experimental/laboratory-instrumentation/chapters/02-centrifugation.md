A centrifuge spins a sample so that particles sediment at a rate that depends on their size, shape and density, and on the force applied. The force is expressed as relative centrifugal force, a multiple of gravity that depends on the speed and on the radius of the rotor. Larger and denser particles sediment faster, and the separation of particles of different sizes depends on applying a force for a time that lets them diverge without sedimenting into one another.

Differential centrifugation separates particles by stepwise increases in force. A low speed pellets nuclei and cell debris, a higher speed pellets mitochondria, and a still higher speed pellets microsomes and ribosomes. The method is simple and widely used, but each pellet contains a mixture, and the purity of an isolate must be checked by markers and by microscopy. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the organelles isolated in this way and their markers.

Density gradient centrifugation separates particles by their buoyant density in a medium whose density increases along the tube. Each particle sediments until it reaches a layer of the same density, and the particles form bands. The method separates organelles that differ only slightly in size, and it separates nucleic acids by their density in caesium chloride gradients.

Rotor choice affects the separation. Fixed-angle rotors pellet particles quickly, while swinging-bucket rotors form cleaner bands in gradients. The speed must be calculated from the rotor radius to achieve the intended force, since the same speed applied in a different rotor gives a different force.

Ioannidis (2005) described how results from partly purified fractions can be misinterpreted when the purity is not known, and centrifugation must be checked by independent methods before the isolated fraction is studied.
