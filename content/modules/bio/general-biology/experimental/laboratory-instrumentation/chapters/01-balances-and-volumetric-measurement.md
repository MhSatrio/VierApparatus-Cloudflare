An analytical balance measures mass by comparing the weight of a sample with calibrated standards. Modern balances use electromagnetic compensation, in which a current holds a sensing element at a reference position. The reading is accurate only when the balance is level, free from draughts and vibration, and calibrated against certified masses. A balance that is out of calibration gives systematic errors that propagate into every solution prepared from it.

Mass is the basis of the molar concentrations described in the Chemistry for Biology module. Lewis (1916) defined the molecule as the unit of chemical identity, and the conversion of a mass into a number of molecules requires the molar mass, which must be known for the compound actually weighed. A hydrated compound, or one that has absorbed water, contains less of the compound than its mass suggests, and drying or determining the water content is necessary for accuracy.

Volumetric measurement uses calibrated pipettes, flasks and cylinders. Each has a tolerance, and the tolerance can be larger than the precision of a balance. The temperature of the liquid affects its volume, and the meniscus must be read consistently. Glassware should be used at the temperature for which it was calibrated, and automatic pipettes need regular checks of their delivery.

Evaporation changes concentration during weighing and mixing, and volatile solvents require sealed containers. Static charge can make powders adhere to containers, changing the mass delivered. Each of these errors is small, but they accumulate in a series of dilutions.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the solutions used in cell biology and the consequences of concentration errors for the experiments in which they are used. Good practice is to verify key solutions by independent measurement and to record the preparation in the laboratory notebook.
