Electrophoresis separates molecules by their movement through a gel under an electric field. Charged molecules migrate toward the electrode of opposite charge, and their speed depends on their charge, their size and the density of the gel. Nucleic acids have a uniform charge per unit length, so in an agarose gel they separate mainly by length, as the DNA double helix described by Watson and Crick (1953) would predict. The separation of fragments of DNA by size is one of the most routine methods of molecular biology.

Proteins have charges that vary with sequence, so their separation by native electrophoresis depends on both charge and shape. For many purposes the proteins are denatured with sodium dodecyl sulphate, which binds to the chain in proportion to its length and gives the molecules a uniform negative charge per unit mass. The separation then depends almost entirely on molecular mass, and the method allows the sizes of proteins to be estimated against standards of known size.

The gel matrix controls the resolution. A higher percentage of polyacrylamide retards larger molecules more strongly and resolves smaller ones better, while a lower percentage suits larger molecules. Gradient gels cover a wider range of sizes in one run.

Detection requires staining or labelling. Nucleic acids are often stained with fluorescent dyes that bind them, and proteins with dyes that bind their side chains. Each dye has a limit of detection and a linear range, and the quantification of bands must be made within that range. Densitometry measures the intensity of a band, and the measurement is comparable between gels only when the staining and imaging are standardised.

Ioannidis (2005) described the risk of selecting the best-looking gel from many, and the presentation of gel images must include the full field and the controls, not a cropped selection.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecules separated by these methods. Electrophoresis is practised with molecular size markers, loading controls and documented conditions.
