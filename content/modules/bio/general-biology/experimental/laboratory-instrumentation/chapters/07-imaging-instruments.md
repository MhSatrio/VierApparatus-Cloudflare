Imaging instruments convert light or electrons into images that can be measured. The fluorescence microscope uses a detector, usually a camera or a photomultiplier, to record the light emitted by a labelled specimen. Cameras record an image in parallel, while photomultipliers record a single point at a time, and each choice has trade-offs in sensitivity, speed and noise. The detector must be chosen to match the brightness of the signal and the speed of the process being imaged.

The resolution of an instrument is characterised by measuring the smallest feature it can separate, usually with a standard sample of beads or of a known structure. The resolution determined in this way is compared with the theoretical limit described in the Microscopy module. Hell and Wichmann (1994) and Betzig and colleagues (2006) showed that the limit can be surpassed under defined conditions, and the instrument must be calibrated to show that it does so.

Intensity must also be calibrated. The grey value of a pixel is a function of the detector response, and the response may be nonlinear. Images used for quantification must be recorded within the linear range of the detector, with the same settings for all samples, and with background subtraction and flat-field correction applied consistently.

Photobleaching and phototoxicity limit the imaging of living cells. The light dose must be kept low enough that the cells are not damaged and the fluorescence is not lost during the measurement, and the controls must show that the imaging does not change the process being studied.

Ioannidis (2005) described the consequences of selective imaging, in which the most striking field is chosen to illustrate a claim. Images used as evidence must be recorded and reported systematically, and the quantification must be made over the whole set.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular structures that imaging reveals. Good practice is to calibrate the instrument, to record the settings and to report the processing applied to each image.
