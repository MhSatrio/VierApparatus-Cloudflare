Three processes keep laboratory measurements reliable, and they are often confused. Calibration relates an instrument's response to known standards, so that a signal can be converted into a quantity. Validation establishes that a method is fit for its purpose, by showing its accuracy, precision, linear range, specificity and robustness. Quality control checks, during routine use, that the instrument and method continue to perform as they did when they were validated.

Calibration is performed with standards whose values are traceable to recognised references. The calibration curve must cover the range of the samples, and it must be checked periodically. A calibration that is not repeated drifts, and the drift produces systematic errors that are not visible from a single measurement.

Validation requires experiments that separate the sources of error. Accuracy is tested with reference materials of known value, precision by repeated measurement, linearity by a series of concentrations and specificity by the presence of potentially interfering substances. The range within which the method gives reliable results must be stated.

Quality control uses control samples measured alongside the experimental samples. The results are plotted against acceptance limits, and a run is rejected if a control falls outside them. The approach detects drift and failure, and the records allow the history of an instrument to be reviewed.

Platt (1964) argued that science depends on experiments that others can repeat, and calibration and quality control are what make a result comparable with those of other laboratories. Ioannidis (2005) pointed out that unvalidated methods can produce results that do not replicate, and the validation of a method is part of the evidence for any claim based on it. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the measurements whose quality the practice protects. The records of calibration, validation and quality control should be kept with the data they support.
