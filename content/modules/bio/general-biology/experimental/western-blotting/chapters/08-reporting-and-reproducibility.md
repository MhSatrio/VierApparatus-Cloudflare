A western blot is reproducible only if another laboratory can obtain the same antibodies, prepare the same gels, run the same transfer and read the same bands. The report is therefore part of the method. Ioannidis (2005) described how conclusions drawn from unreported conditions cannot be tested, and the western blot is particularly vulnerable because many small choices, such as the blocking agent and the exposure, affect the image.

The full uncropped images must be retained and made available. Cropping to show the band of interest hides the markers, the other bands and the saturation, and it prevents readers from judging whether the band is the target. The markers are reported with their masses, so that the position of the band can be checked. Towbin et al. (1979) described the transfer as a sequence of steps, and each step that affects the result should be recorded.

The antibodies must be identified by source, catalogue number, lot, dilution and validation. The controls must be described, including the loading control, any knockout or knockdown and the reference sample. The quantification must state the method, the linear range and the normalisation, as discussed in the loading chapter.

Replicates require particular care. Technical replicates, which are repeated measurements of the same sample, show the precision of the method but not the variation between samples. Independent biological replicates, prepared from separate cultures or animals, show whether the result holds. Platt (1964) argued that a conclusion is strengthened by tests that could have failed, and independent replicates are that kind of test.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein's properties depend on its structure, and a blot that reports only the total protein may conceal changes in form. The report should state what the antibody can and cannot detect. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the forms of proteins that a single band can conceal.
