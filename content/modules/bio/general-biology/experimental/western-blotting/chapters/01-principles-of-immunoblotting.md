A western blot identifies a protein by two independent properties. The first is its size, which is established by denaturing electrophoresis as Laemmli (1970) described. The second is its recognition by an antibody, which binds a defined region of the protein. The proteins are first separated by size, and then transferred to a membrane, where the antibody can reach them. Towbin et al. (1979) showed that the transfer preserves the separation and makes the proteins accessible to antibodies, and the method combines the two properties in a single read-out.

A band at the expected molecular mass is consistent with the target, but it is not proof of identity. An antibody may cross-react with a different protein of similar size, and a protein may run at an unexpected position because of modification, cleavage or its charge. The position therefore establishes a hypothesis that must be tested by further evidence, such as a knockdown that removes the band, an overexpression that increases it or a second antibody against a different epitope.

The antibody itself is the central reagent. Coons et al. (1941) demonstrated that an antibody can be labelled to make an antigen visible, and this principle underlies the detection step of the blot. The sensitivity of the method depends on the affinity of the antibody and on the amount of the target, and a weak signal can reflect either low abundance or poor antibody performance.

The method is quantitative only within limits. The response of the detection system is not always linear, and a strong band can saturate. The interpretation must therefore be made within the range that the controls establish.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a single line of evidence can be over-interpreted when it agrees with expectation, and the western blot should be treated as one part of a larger case. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell biology of the proteins that blots examine.
