Detection reveals the antibody that has bound the target. In the chemiluminescent method, the enzyme on the secondary antibody converts a substrate into a light-emitting product, and the light is captured on film or by a digital camera. The reaction produces light for a limited time, and the signal can be strong but short-lived. Fluorescent detection uses a secondary antibody that carries a fluorophore, which is excited by light of one wavelength and emits at another. Coons et al. (1941) established the principle of this kind of labelling, and fluorescent detection is linear over a wider range and allows several targets to be seen on one membrane in different colours.

Exposure must be chosen with care. A signal that is too strong saturates the detector, and the saturated band cannot be compared with the others. A series of exposures is taken, and the one in which the band of interest lies in the linear range, without saturation, is used for quantification. The exposure for the loading control and for the target should be taken from the same image set, so that they can be compared.

The image itself is data. Brightness and contrast adjustments applied to the picture change the apparent intensity of the bands, and any processing must be applied equally to all lanes and recorded. The raw image should be archived unaltered, so that the analysis can be repeated.

Towbin et al. (1979) emphasised the reproducibility of each step, and the detection step is one place where the result can change without a change in the sample. The method and the instrument settings should be reported.

Ioannidis (2005) described how selective presentation of the best image can mislead, and the full membrane, with its markers and controls, should be retained. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the chemical reactions of biological signalling that the detection reagents mimic in their specificity.
