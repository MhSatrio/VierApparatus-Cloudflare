The primary antibody binds the target protein at a defined region called the epitope. Its specificity determines whether the band reflects the protein of interest, and an antibody that binds an epitope shared with other proteins gives signals that are difficult to attribute. Coons et al. (1941) introduced the idea of labelling an antibody so that its binding can be seen, and in a blot the labelling is usually performed by a second antibody rather than by the primary.

The secondary antibody recognises the species and class of the primary, for example a goat anti-rabbit antibody for a primary raised in rabbit. It carries an enzyme, such as horseradish peroxidase, or a fluorophore that produces the signal. Because many secondary molecules can bind a single primary, the signal is amplified. The secondary must match the primary, and a mismatch produces no signal, or a signal from cross-reaction with the membrane.

The dilution of each antibody is determined by titration. Too concentrated an antibody increases background and reduces specificity, and too dilute an antibody gives a weak signal. The incubation time and temperature must be fixed, and the antibody solution is commonly reused, which changes its strength over time. Towbin et al. (1979) described the blot as a sequence of such steps, and the consistency of each step determines the repeatability of the result.

The source of the antibody must be reported. Polyclonal antibodies recognise several epitopes and are sensitive to batch variation, while monoclonal antibodies recognise one epitope from a single clone. The catalogue number, the lot, the host species and the clone identifier allow another laboratory to obtain the same reagent.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that depends on an unidentified reagent cannot be reproduced, and the antibody details should be treated as part of the method. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the immune system's antibodies and the antigens they recognise.
