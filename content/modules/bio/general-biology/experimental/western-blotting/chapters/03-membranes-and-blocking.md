The membrane is the solid support on which the transferred proteins are held and probed. Towbin et al. (1979) used nitrocellulose, which binds proteins through hydrophobic and other interactions and retains them firmly. Polyvinylidene difluoride, or PVDF, binds proteins more strongly and tolerates more handling, and it is preferred when the blot is stripped and reprobed or when the protein is small and must be retained. PVDF must be wetted in methanol before use, and it can give higher background if it is not handled carefully. The choice affects the signal, and the membrane should be selected for the detection system and the number of probings planned.

Every membrane has unoccupied binding sites that will bind any protein, including the antibody, and these sites must be covered before the antibody is applied. Blocking is done with a solution of protein, typically non-fat dry milk or bovine serum albumin, which adsorbs to the free surface. The purpose is to reduce background, so that the signal comes from the antibody binding the target rather than the membrane itself. Coons et al. (1941) established the principle that a labelled antibody reports where it binds, and background from nonspecific binding weakens that report.

The blocking agent must be chosen with the antibody in mind. Milk contains phosphoproteins and biotin that interfere with some antibodies and detection systems, and BSA is preferred for phospho-specific detection. The concentration and the duration of blocking also matter, since insufficient blocking leaves high background and excessive blocking can mask the epitope.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the choice of reagent can change a result without the change being noticed, and the blocking agent and its concentration should be recorded for each blot. The background should be measured in a region of the membrane without protein, so that it can be subtracted consistently.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the post-translational modifications, such as phosphorylation, that affect how a protein is recognised.
