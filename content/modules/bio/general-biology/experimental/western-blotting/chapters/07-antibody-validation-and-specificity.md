The antibody is the weakest link in a western blot, and many published bands have later been traced to antibodies that recognised something other than their target. Validation is the process of showing that an antibody binds its target and does not bind other proteins that happen to share an epitope or a similar size. Coons et al. (1941) established that antibodies carry the specificity of their binding, and that specificity must be demonstrated rather than assumed.

The most informative control is a sample in which the target has been removed. A knockout cell line lacks the gene, and a knockdown reduces the transcript, and the band should disappear or fall in proportion. A band that persists in the knockout is produced by another protein. Platt (1964) argued that a test is strong when it can clearly fail, and the knockout provides exactly this kind of test, since the antibody should not give the band if it is specific.

Other approaches add support. Overexpressing the target should increase the band, and a peptide that competes for the antibody's binding site should abolish the band when it is added in excess. An orthogonal method, such as mass spectrometry of the immunoprecipitated protein, identifies the protein independently of the antibody.

A band at the expected molecular mass does not establish specificity, because the antibody may bind a protein of similar size or a modified form of the target. Validation must test the claim that the band is the target, and the result should be reported with the controls used.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a single convincing result can persuade more strongly than the evidence warrants, and the validation should be reported in full. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the post-translational forms of proteins that can be recognised differently by the same antibody.
