The separation is carried out as Laemmli (1970) described, with the proteins denatured by SDS and resolved by mass in a polyacrylamide gel. The gel percentage determines the range of masses that resolves well. A higher percentage separates small proteins better, and a lower percentage separates large ones. The choice is made from the expected mass of the target, and the standards on the same gel show whether the band lies in a region of good resolution.

Towbin et al. (1979) described the transfer of the separated proteins onto a membrane by an electric field applied across the gel, in the direction perpendicular to the gel surface. The proteins move from the gel onto the membrane and bind it, and the pattern of bands is preserved. Transfer depends on the mass of the protein, on the current, the time and the buffer composition. Large proteins transfer slowly and may not leave the gel, while small proteins can pass through the membrane if the transfer runs too long.

Transfer efficiency must be checked. A reversible stain of the membrane, such as Ponceau S, shows whether the bands have transferred and whether the transfer is even across the lanes. A gel stained after transfer shows the bands that remain, and comparison of the two reveals proteins that were not transferred. Uneven transfer, seen as a gradient across the membrane or as a distortion of the bands, indicates a problem with the sandwich or with air bubbles.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the choice of conditions can change which bands are visible, and the gel percentage, the transfer time and the current should be recorded. A protocol that is not repeatable cannot give comparable results between experiments.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecular masses of the cellular proteins, which guide the choice of gel.
