# Western Blotting

The electrophoresis modules described how proteins are separated by size, and the purification module showed how a single band is checked for purity. A western blot extends these methods by identifying a specific protein within a complex mixture. The proteins are separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to a membrane, probed with an antibody that binds the target and revealed by a detection system. The result is a band whose position confirms the size of the target and whose intensity estimates its relative amount.

The chapters follow the order of the method. The principle and the transfer come first, as described by Towbin et al. (1979), who established electrophoretic transfer to nitrocellulose. The membrane, the blocking and the choice of antibodies follow. Detection, loading controls and normalisation are then described, together with antibody validation and the reporting that allows a blot to be judged.

Laemmli (1970) provided the denaturing separation on which most blots depend, and Coons et al. (1941) introduced the labelling of antibodies, the principle on which the detection of the target rests. The main risk of the method is misidentification. A band of the expected size is only as reliable as the antibody that produced it, and Ioannidis (2005) described how such single lines of evidence can be over-read. The chapters treat each control as part of the identification.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cellular proteins that blots most often examine, which gives the biological context for each target.
