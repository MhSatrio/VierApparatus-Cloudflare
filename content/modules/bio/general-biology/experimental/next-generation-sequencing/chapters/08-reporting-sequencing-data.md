A sequencing result is a large set of files, and the analysis is a long chain of decisions. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result cannot be judged or repeated without the choices that produced it, and the report is where those choices are recorded. The report must allow another laboratory to repeat the library, the run and the analysis, and it must allow a reader to see the limits of what the data support.

The sample and the library are described in full. The source of the nucleic acid, its extraction, its quality and the library method are stated, together with the index sequences and the amount loaded. The platform, the run and its date are reported, since the performance of an instrument changes over time. Shendure and Ji (2008) showed that the chemistry and the platform shape the reads, and the report identifies both.

The quality of the data is reported with its measures: the distribution of quality scores, the number of reads before and after trimming, the proportion that aligned to the reference, the depth and the coverage across the target. The regions with low coverage are listed. Platt (1964) argued that a claim should be stated so that it could be shown false, and the coverage of a region determines whether a negative result there is informative.

The software is named with its version and its parameters, and the reference genome is identified by its version and source. The filters applied to the variants or the counts are stated with their thresholds. The deposit of the raw reads in a public archive, with an accession number, allows the analysis to be repeated, and the analysis code is archived with the data.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the consequence of a sequence change depends on the structure it produces, and the report should state the functional consequences only as far as the data support them. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the genome and transcriptome that the sequencing measures.
