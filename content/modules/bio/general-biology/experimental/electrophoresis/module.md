# Electrophoresis

The Laboratory Instrumentation module explained how a gel separates molecules in an electric field. This topic treats electrophoresis as a set of complete methods, each designed for a class of molecule and a range of size. The methods are among the most used in biology, and their results are interpreted from bands whose position and intensity depend on many choices of gel and buffer.

The chapters cover agarose gels for nucleic acids, the denaturing protein gel of Laemmli (1970) and the Tricine system that Schägger and von Jagow (1987) developed for small proteins, native gels that preserve complexes, two-dimensional separation, pulsed-field methods for very large DNA and the detection and quantification of bands. Each chapter describes the logic of the separation and the points at which the results can mislead.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the selection of the best-looking result from many gels can distort the evidence. The chapters emphasise the controls and the complete presentation of gels as part of the method.

Readers should note that electrophoresis is often the final check of a purification or a cloning, and its results are only as reliable as the markers and controls run on the same gel.
