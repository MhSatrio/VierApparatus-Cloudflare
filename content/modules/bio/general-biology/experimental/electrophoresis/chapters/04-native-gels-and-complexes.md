Native gels separate proteins and complexes without denaturation, so they retain their folded structure and their interactions. The migration in a native gel depends on the net charge, the mass and the shape of the molecule, and two proteins of the same mass can separate if their shapes or charges differ. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the folded structure is what determines the behaviour of a protein, and a native gel reports that structure in a way that the denaturing gel cannot.

Native electrophoresis is therefore useful for detecting complexes. A protein that forms a dimer or a larger assembly migrates more slowly than the monomer, and the position of the band reveals the state. The interpretation is complicated because the shape and charge also affect migration, so a band is not a direct measure of mass. Comparison with native standards of known composition helps.

The electrophoretic mobility shift assay uses a native gel to detect the binding of a protein to a nucleic acid. The labelled nucleic acid migrates as a band, and the binding of a protein shifts the band to a slower position. The method is widely used to test whether a factor binds a sequence, and it requires controls that show the shift depends on the specific sequence, using a competitor with a mutated sequence.

Watson and Crick (1953) described the nucleic acid whose complex with proteins the shift assay reveals. The binding conditions, including the salt and the carrier, determine whether the complex survives the gel, and a dissociating complex gives a false negative.

Ioannidis (2005) described the risk of interpreting a single shifted band without controls, and the specificity of a shift must be shown by competition experiments. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the complexes that native methods study. The record should include the buffer, the gel, the binding conditions and the controls.
