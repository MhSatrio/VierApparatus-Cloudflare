Laemmli (1970) described a method for separating the structural proteins of a bacteriophage, and the procedure he developed became the standard denaturing gel of the field. The sample is boiled with sodium dodecyl sulphate, which binds the chain in proportion to its length and unfolds it, and with a reducing agent that breaks disulfide bonds. The denatured proteins then carry a uniform negative charge per unit mass, and in the gel they migrate according to their size. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the native fold depends on the disulfide bonds and the sequence, and the denaturation removes this dependence so that the gel reports only mass.

The stacking gel is a large-pore gel above the separating gel, at a different pH and ionic strength, which concentrates the proteins into a narrow zone before they enter the separating gel. The stacking produces sharp bands, and it is one of the reasons the method gives good resolution. A stacking gel that is incorrectly made produces broad or distorted bands.

The molecular mass of an unknown is estimated from a set of standards of known mass. The distance travelled is plotted against the logarithm of the mass for the standards, and the unknown is read from the curve. The estimate is approximate, because some proteins bind SDS unevenly or migrate abnormally, and glycoproteins and highly charged proteins often deviate from the expected mass.

Protein modifications and complexes are not separated by this method. Complexes are dissociated, and the subunits are seen separately, which is useful for identifying subunits but not for studying assemblies.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the presentation of gel images can be selective, and the complete gel with its standards should be shown. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the proteins separated by these methods. The record should include the gel percentage, the buffer, the standards and the staining method.
