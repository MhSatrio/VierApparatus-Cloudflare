Two-dimensional electrophoresis separates proteins in two directions at right angles. In the first dimension, proteins are separated by isoelectric focusing, in which a pH gradient is established and each protein migrates to the pH at which its net charge is zero. In the second dimension, the strip is laid on an SDS gel of the Laemmli type, which separates the proteins by mass. Because the two properties are largely independent, the combination resolves many more proteins than either dimension alone, and a complex mixture can produce a map of hundreds or thousands of spots.

The method has been central to proteomics, because it allows the comparison of protein patterns between samples, and spots that change can be excised and identified by mass spectrometry. The power of the comparison depends on the reproducibility of the gels, which is limited, so that spots must be matched with software and confirmed by replicate gels.

The method has limits. Membrane proteins are poorly soluble and often do not enter the first dimension, so they are underrepresented. Very basic proteins and very large or very small ones are difficult to resolve. Proteins that are present in low amounts are hidden by abundant ones, and the sensitivity of the stain limits detection.

Ioannidis (2005) described how spot-matching and the selection of changed spots can produce large numbers of apparent differences, many of which are not reproducible. The criteria for calling a spot changed must be set in advance, and the number of comparisons must be corrected for.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the proteins that the gels reveal. The record should include the pH range, the gel composition, the stain, the matching method and the replicate structure.
