In an agarose gel, DNA fragments migrate in proportion to the logarithm of their length, because their charge is proportional to their length and the gel retards larger fragments more strongly. Watson and Crick (1953) described the double helix, whose uniform charge per base pair makes this size separation possible. The resolution depends on the agarose concentration: a low concentration of about one per cent separates large fragments well, and a higher concentration resolves smaller ones. Choosing the concentration is the first decision of the method.

A size marker, a ladder of fragments of known length, must be run beside the samples. It converts the position of each band into an approximate size, and it reveals whether the gel has run as expected. Without a marker, a band cannot be assigned a size, and the position of a band relative to the dye front is not a reliable substitute.

Integrity is shown by the sharpness of the bands. Intact genomic DNA runs as a compact high-molecular-weight band, while degraded DNA appears as a smear. Intact RNA shows two distinct ribosomal bands in eukaryotes, and a smear or the loss of the upper band indicates degradation. The gel is therefore a quick check on the quality of a preparation before it is used.

Contamination is also visible. Protein contamination can distort the migration, and salts or residual solvents can change the shape of the bands. Samples that run unexpectedly should be re-extracted or re-purified, rather than interpreted as they stand.

Ioannidis (2005) described how gels are sometimes selected for presentation, and the full gel with the markers should be retained and reported. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the nucleic acids and their extraction, which determine the quality of the sample on the gel. The record should include the agarose concentration, the buffer, the voltage, the time and the marker used.
