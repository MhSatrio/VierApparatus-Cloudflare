Electrophoresis is sensitive to many variables, and its results often show artefacts that can be mistaken for biology. Smiling, in which the bands curve upward or downward across the gel, comes from uneven heating, which changes the local mobility. Streaking and smearing come from overloaded samples, from nucleases or proteases in the sample, or from salts and residual solvents that distort the migration. Ghost bands, which appear where no band should be, can come from contamination of the buffer or from aggregation of the sample.

The loss of signal has its own causes. A stain that has deteriorated, a transfer that failed or a sample that was degraded will all give faint or absent bands. The absence of a band should not be interpreted as the absence of the molecule until the controls have shown that the procedure can detect it. A positive control of a known amount of the molecule is the only reliable test.

Laemmli (1970) described a procedure whose reproducibility depends on the preparation of the gel and the sample, and each step can introduce an artefact. Sample buffers must be fresh, the reducing agent must be added just before use and the gel must be poured evenly, since bubbles and uneven polymerisation produce distorted bands.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the interpretation of an unexpected band as a biological finding, without checking for artefact, can mislead. Each unexpected band should be tested by an independent method or by a control that removes its suspected source.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecules that the gels separate and the degradation processes that produce unexpected bands. The laboratory should keep a record of artefacts and their causes, which makes the method more reliable over time.
