A band is visible only after it has been stained, and the stain determines what can be seen and how well it can be measured. Nucleic acids are commonly stained with intercalating dyes that fluoresce when bound, which gives high sensitivity and a wide linear range. Proteins are stained with dyes that bind the side chains, such as Coomassie or silver. Each stain has a detection limit, a linear range and a set of molecules to which it responds, and the choice determines the band intensities that are measured.

Silver staining is more sensitive than Coomassie, but its response is less linear and varies between proteins, so it is less suited to quantification. A stain chosen for detection of a trace protein may give a poor measure of the relative amounts of abundant ones. The choice of stain should follow the question rather than convenience.

Densitometry measures the intensity of a band from a scanned image. The background must be subtracted consistently, and the measurement must be made within the linear range of the stain and of the scanner, since saturated bands underestimate the amount. A band that is saturated in one lane and not in another gives a misleading comparison.

Comparability between gels requires standard conditions. The same stain, the same staining time, the same scanner settings and the same gel composition must be used, and a standard loaded on each gel allows the intensities to be normalised. Without these, the apparent change between gels may reflect the conditions rather than the samples.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the processing of images can change the result, and the raw scan should be archived and the processing steps reported. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecules whose bands are quantified. The record should include the stain, the scanner settings, the background method and the normalisation.
