In a standard agarose gel, the separation of DNA by size fails above about twenty kilobases, because the largest molecules all migrate in the same way and cannot be distinguished. The DNA double helix described by Watson and Crick (1953) is very long, and a chromosome may be many megabases. Pulsed-field electrophoresis addresses this by periodically changing the direction of the electric field, so that large molecules must reorient before they can move, and the time spent reorienting depends on their length.

Each switch forces a molecule to change its conformation, and the longest molecules take the longest to reorient. The net migration therefore depends on the length of the molecule and on the pulse time. By choosing the pulse time and the angle of the switch, the range of separable sizes can be tuned, and chromosomes of yeast and bacteria can be resolved as separate bands.

The method is used in genome analysis, where a restriction digest of a chromosome yields a set of large fragments whose sizes reveal the map of the genome. It is also used to confirm the identity of chromosomes in strains and to detect large rearrangements.

The conditions are demanding. The run takes many hours to days, the field must be uniform, and the temperature must be controlled, since small changes alter the migration. The DNA must be prepared in agarose plugs to avoid shearing, and the preparation can damage the sample if it is not careful.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the reproducibility of long runs is sensitive to conditions, and the parameters of each run should be recorded and the standards run in parallel. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the chromosomes whose sizes the method measures. The record should include the pulse program, the voltage, the temperature, the time and the markers.
