The Laemmli system uses glycine as the trailing ion, and it resolves proteins well above about twenty kilodaltons. Below that range, the bands crowd together and are poorly separated, because the small proteins and peptides are retarded by the gel in a way that compresses them. Schägger and von Jagow (1987) addressed this by replacing glycine with Tricine, a different trailing ion, and by adjusting the gel composition. The modification extends the resolving range down to small peptides and up to about one hundred kilodaltons.

The Tricine system resolves small proteins because the trailing ion and the gel allow the small species to separate in the same region where the Laemmli system would compress them. The improvement is a property of the chemistry of the buffers, and it is achieved without changing the logic of the denaturing separation that Laemmli (1970) described.

The Tricine gel must be run with its own buffers, and the gel composition differs from that of the Laemmli gel. Mixing the two systems produces poor separation, so the protocol must specify the system used.

Small peptides can be lost from the gel by diffusion or by passing through the bottom, and the fixation and staining must retain them. Sensitive stains are often needed, and the detection limit of the stain must be known for the species of interest.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the choice of method can change which bands are visible, and the method should be selected and reported in advance. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the peptides and small proteins that the system separates. The record should include the gel system, the composition, the standards and the stain.
