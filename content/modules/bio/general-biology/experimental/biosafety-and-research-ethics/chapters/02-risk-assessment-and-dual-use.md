A risk assessment identifies the hazards of a planned experiment, estimates how likely they are to cause harm and decides which controls are needed. For work with biological agents, the hazards include the agent itself, the equipment, the procedures that generate aerosols or sharps injuries, and the waste. The assessment is written before the work begins, and it is reviewed by someone other than the investigator, since the investigator may not see the hazards that are familiar to them.

Dual-use research is research that is intended for a beneficial purpose but could be misused to cause harm. Experiments that increase the transmissibility or virulence of a pathogen, for example, could provide knowledge that is useful for vaccine development and could also be used to make an agent more dangerous. Finlay and Falkow (1997) described the mechanisms of pathogenicity, and knowledge of these mechanisms can serve both purposes.

Dual-use concerns require review beyond the laboratory. Institutional committees and national bodies weigh the expected benefit of the work against the potential for misuse, and they decide whether the research should proceed, be modified or be restricted in its publication. The judgement is difficult, because the benefit and the risk are both uncertain, and the review must be transparent about the reasons for its decision.

Investigators have a responsibility to recognise dual-use potential in their work and to raise it with the appropriate committee. The responsibility applies even to work that appears routine, since a change in method or in the agent can alter the risk.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biology of the agents and processes that such review considers. The risk assessment and the review should be recorded, so that the decisions and their reasons can be examined later.
