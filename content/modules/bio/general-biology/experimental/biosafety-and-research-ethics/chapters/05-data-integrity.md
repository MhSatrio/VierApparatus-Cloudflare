Data integrity is the accuracy and completeness of the data on which a finding rests. It requires that data be recorded at the time they are collected, in a form that cannot be altered without leaving a trace, that the raw data be kept, and that the analysis be traceable from the raw data to the reported result. These practices are the foundation of trust in research, because they allow others to check what was done.

Misconduct takes several forms. Fabrication is the invention of data, and falsification is the manipulation of data or results. Both are serious violations, and they are relatively rare, but they can have severe consequences for the field and for the people who rely on it. Selective reporting, in which only the favourable results are reported, is more common and equally damaging, because it distorts the evidence without any single false statement. Ioannidis (2005) identified selective reporting as one of the main causes of unreliable findings.

Errors happen, and they must be corrected. An investigator who finds an error in published data has an obligation to report it, to issue a correction or retraction and to inform those who have used the result. The obligation is part of the self-correcting character of science, which Platt (1964) described in terms of the progress of fields that test their claims.

Detection depends on the records and on the willingness of others to check them. Raw data, analysis code and detailed protocols make the work reproducible and make misconduct easier to detect. Statistical screening can identify unusual patterns, such as implausibly similar images or values, and these tools are increasingly used.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the conventions of recording and reporting in the molecular and cell biology literature. Investigators are responsible for the integrity of their data throughout their careers, and the habits formed early are the most reliable protection.
