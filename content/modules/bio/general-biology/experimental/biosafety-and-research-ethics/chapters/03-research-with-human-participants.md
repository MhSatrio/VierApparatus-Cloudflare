Research with people is governed by principles that were developed after abuses in the twentieth century made their necessity plain. Respect for persons requires that individuals be treated as autonomous agents, able to decide whether to take part, and that those with diminished autonomy be given additional protection. Beneficence requires that the research minimise harm and maximise benefit, and justice requires that the burdens and benefits of research be distributed fairly across groups.

Informed consent is the process by which a person agrees to take part with an understanding of what the study involves. The information given must include the purpose, the procedures, the risks and benefits, the alternatives, the right to withdraw without penalty and the arrangements for privacy. The consent must be given freely, without undue pressure, and it must be documented. Consent is not a single signature but a process, and it should be reviewed when the study changes in a way that affects the participant.

Studies must be reviewed by an ethics committee before they begin. The committee weighs the risks against the benefit, checks the consent process and considers the protection of vulnerable groups, such as children, people with cognitive impairment and those who depend on the investigator for care. The review is independent of the investigator, and it can require changes or refuse approval.

Ioannidis (2005) described how studies that are small, flexibly analysed or selectively reported can mislead, and this is an ethical as well as a scientific concern, since participants accept risk on the expectation that the study will produce reliable knowledge. A study that is badly designed exposes participants to risk without a sound prospect of benefit.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biology of the processes that human research investigates. The ethical conduct of research depends on the design, the consent, the review and the honest reporting of the results.
