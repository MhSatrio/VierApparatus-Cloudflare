Authorship is the public record of who is responsible for a piece of research. It carries credit, which affects careers, and responsibility, which means that the author must stand behind the work. The criteria for authorship therefore combine a contribution to the conception, the design, the data or the analysis with a role in writing or revising the report, and with agreement to be accountable for the result. Contributors who do not meet these criteria should be acknowledged rather than listed as authors.

The responsibilities of authors include checking the accuracy of the data and the analysis, approving the final version and reporting any conflicts of interest. An author who did not check the work can be held responsible for its errors, and the requirement that all authors approve the report reflects this. Ioannidis (2005) described how the incentives of the publication system can shape what is reported, and the pressure to add or remove authors is one of those incentives.

Disputes about authorship are common, and they are best prevented by agreeing on roles at the start of a project and by revisiting the agreement as the work changes. The order of authors often signals the relative contribution, and the convention differs among fields, so the conventions should be stated. Where a dispute cannot be resolved between the investigators, an institutional process should be used.

Technicians, core facility staff, statisticians and others who contribute to a study should be acknowledged, even when they are not authors. Their work is part of the evidence, and omitting them distorts the record of who made the study possible.

Platt (1964) argued that the progress of science depends on the ability to identify who did what and to build on it, and clear authorship and acknowledgement are the means of doing so. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the conventions of scientific reporting in which authorship is recorded.
