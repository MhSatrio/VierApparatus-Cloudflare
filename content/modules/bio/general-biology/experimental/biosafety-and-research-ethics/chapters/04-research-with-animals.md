Animals are used in biological research because their biology resembles that of people and of other species, and because their experiments can be controlled in ways that human studies cannot. The use of animals carries an obligation to minimise their suffering, and the principles that guide this obligation are summarised as the three Rs. Replacement means using methods that do not involve animals wherever possible, such as cell cultures, computer models or tissues obtained without harm. Reduction means using the fewest animals that will give a valid result. Refinement means changing procedures to reduce pain, distress and harm.

Reduction depends on a sound design. An experiment with too few animals may fail to answer its question and must then be repeated, which uses more animals in total. The power calculation described in the Experimental Design module determines the number needed, and it should be used to avoid both waste and the use of animals in studies that cannot succeed.

Harm is assessed in advance. The procedure, the expected severity of pain or distress, the humane endpoints that will stop the experiment and the care that will be given are all specified in an application that is reviewed by an animal welfare body. The review weighs the expected benefit of the research against the harm, and it can refuse or modify the procedure.

Ioannidis (2005) described how poorly designed studies waste resources, and in animal research a study that is underpowered or flexibly analysed uses animals without producing a reliable result. The ethical obligation to use animals responsibly therefore includes the quality of the design and the reporting of all results, including those that were negative.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biology of the animal models in common use. The responsible use of animals depends on choosing the right model, designing the study well and reporting it fully.
