Biological research informs decisions about health, food, the environment and the regulation of technologies, and biologists have a responsibility to communicate their findings to people who make those decisions and to the public. The responsibility is greater than that owed to the scientific community, because non-specialists cannot easily judge the strength of a claim, and they rely on the accuracy of what they are told.

Claims are commonly overstated when they leave the laboratory. A finding from a cell culture may be presented as a treatment for disease, a correlation may be presented as a cause, and a preliminary result may be presented as established. Ioannidis (2005) described how early and small studies can be more exciting and less reliable than later ones, and the press and the public often receive the early version first. Biologists should state the scope and limits of their findings when they communicate them.

Honest communication includes uncertainty. A reader who is told that a result is probable, that the effect is small or that the evidence is preliminary can make a better decision than one who is given a confident simplification. The communication of uncertainty is not a sign of weakness but a requirement of accuracy, and the ranges and the quality of the evidence should be explained.

Biologists should correct misrepresentations of their work when they see them. The obligation applies to statements that they made and to statements made about their results by others, and it reflects the responsibility of the source for the accuracy of what is attributed to it.

Platt (1964) described the progress of science as the elimination of alternatives by decisive tests, and communication should convey the state of that process accurately, including what has been excluded and what remains open. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the scientific basis of the claims that are communicated. The responsibility to the public is part of the practice of biology, not an addition to it.
