A conflict of interest exists when an interest that is not the research itself could influence the design, conduct, analysis or reporting of a study. The interest may be financial, such as a payment from a company whose product is being tested, a shareholding or a patent. It may also be non-financial, such as a commitment to a theory, a rivalry with another group or a desire for a particular outcome. The existence of a conflict is not misconduct, but it creates a risk that must be disclosed and managed.

Conflicts bias research without deliberate dishonesty. An investigator who expects a result may design the study to favour it, may interpret ambiguous data in its favour or may report the outcomes that fit. Ioannidis (2005) identified such pressures among the causes of unreliable findings, and the evidence from industry-funded trials suggests that the funding can shape the results, even when the investigators are honest.

Platt (1964) recommended that investigators design experiments to test their own hypotheses against alternatives, and the discipline of strong inference is one protection against the bias that interests can produce. Blinding, pre-registration and independent analysis, discussed in the Experimental Design module, are the practical safeguards.

Disclosure is the first step in managing a conflict. Investigators should declare their interests to journals, to funders and to the institutions that review the work, and readers should be able to see the declarations alongside the results. Management may require that the investigator not be responsible for the analysis, that an independent committee oversee the study or that the interest be divested.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the reporting of research, in which the disclosure of interests forms part of the record. A reader who knows the conflicts can weigh the evidence appropriately, and the disclosure is a form of respect for the reader.
