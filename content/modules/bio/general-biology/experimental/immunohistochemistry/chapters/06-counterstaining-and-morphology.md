A specific stain shows where the target is, but it does not by itself show the cells that surround it. A counterstain supplies this context. Haematoxylin is the most common, and it stains the nuclei blue, so that every cell in the section is visible whether or not it expresses the target. The nuclei give the count of cells against which the proportion of positive cells is measured, and they show the structure of the tissue, its layers, its glands and its vessels. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell nucleus and the tissue organisation that the counterstain makes visible.

The counterstain must be balanced. A strong counterstain can obscure a weak specific stain, and the brown or red of the target may be hard to distinguish from the blue of the nuclei. The intensity of the counterstain is set so that the specific signal stands out, and the same counterstain is used in every section of a comparison. A section that is over-stained cannot be scored reliably, and the time in the counterstain is fixed.

Morphology decides which cells are positive. A stain that is present in a cell of the expected type, in the expected compartment, is more credible than a stain that appears in a random pattern across the tissue. Coons et al. (1941) established that the label reveals binding, and the reader must judge whether the binding lies where the protein should be. Staining in dead cells, in debris, in blood or in the edges of the section is an artefact, and it must be excluded.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the interpretation of an image can be guided by expectation, and the criteria for positivity by morphology should be written before the scoring. The features used to decide that a cell is positive are stated in the report.

The counterstain is therefore not decoration. It supplies the frame in which the specific stain is read.
