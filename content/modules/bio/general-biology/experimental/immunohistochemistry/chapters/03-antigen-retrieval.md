Cross-linking by formalin can hide the epitope that the antibody requires, so that a protein is present but not detected. Antigen retrieval reverses some of this masking. Heat-induced retrieval heats the section in a buffer, commonly at about ninety-five degrees Celsius for some minutes, which breaks some of the cross-links and allows the antibody to reach the epitope. Enzymatic retrieval uses a protease to digest the tissue around the epitope, and it is used for some antigens that heat does not expose. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the folding of a protein is sensitive to its environment, and retrieval works by restoring a configuration that the fixation has altered.

The buffer and its pH are important. A citrate buffer at about pH 6 and a Tris or EDTA buffer at a higher pH suit different antigens, and the choice follows the antibody's data sheet and the validation for the target. The pH affects both the exposure of the epitope and the damage to the tissue, and the wrong buffer can leave the antigen masked or destroy the morphology.

Retrieval must be optimised. Too little heat or time leaves the antigen masked, and too much destroys the tissue and produces high background, as the antibody binds nonspecifically to damaged structures. The conditions are chosen by testing a range, and the best combination gives strong staining of the expected cells with low background in the regions that should be negative. Platt (1964) argued that a method is best tested against alternatives, and a retrieval series is a test of this kind.

The result of retrieval is judged against the morphology. A stain that becomes strong after aggressive retrieval but spreads to cell types that should be negative has been produced by the retrieval, not by the target.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the strength of a stain can be increased until it appears, and the optimisation must be reported with the conditions chosen. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell structures whose damage shows the limit of retrieval.
