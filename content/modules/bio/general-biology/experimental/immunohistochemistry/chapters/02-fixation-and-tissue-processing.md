Fixation stops the processes of the cell, preserves its structure and prevents the tissue from decaying. The most common fixative is formalin, a solution of formaldehyde, which forms chemical cross-links between proteins. These cross-links hold the tissue in place, but they also alter the proteins, and the epitope recognised by an antibody may be covered or changed. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the structure of a protein determines its recognition, and cross-linking alters that structure, which is why fixation is a central variable in the method.

The duration of fixation must be controlled. Too short a fixation leaves the tissue vulnerable to autolysis and the antigens exposed to degradation. Too long a fixation increases cross-linking and masks more epitopes, so that the staining weakens. The time is set for each antibody and tissue type, and it is recorded. Coons et al. (1941) worked with fresh material, and the preservation of antigens in fixed tissue is a later adaptation that requires its own validation.

Processing follows fixation. The tissue is dehydrated through graded alcohols, cleared in a solvent and embedded in paraffin wax, which supports it during sectioning. Each step can change the tissue, and the solvents can extract lipids and some antigens. The thickness of the section, usually a few micrometres, determines how much of each cell is visible, and the section must be thin enough for single cells to be resolved.

The tissue must be handled promptly after removal. Delay between the death of the tissue and its fixation allows changes in gene expression and protein levels, and these changes alter the stain. The time of fixation is therefore reported with the sample.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the conditions of tissue collection can produce differences that are then attributed to the biology of the donors. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell structures that fixation preserves.
