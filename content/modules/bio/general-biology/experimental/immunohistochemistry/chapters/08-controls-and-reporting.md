A stain is interpretable only with its controls, and every run needs them. A positive control tissue, known to express the target, shows that the procedure can reveal the protein. A negative control tissue, known to lack it, shows that the stain is not produced by the reagents alone. The omitted-primary control, in which the primary antibody is left out, shows whether the detection system stains the tissue without the antibody. Platt (1964) argued that a claim is strengthened by a control that could show it false, and these controls each test a particular explanation for the stain.

An isotype control uses an antibody of the same class but no specificity for the target, and it shows the level of nonspecific binding of the antibody. Coons et al. (1941) established that labelled antibodies reveal their binding, and the isotype control shows how much of the signal is binding that is not specific. It is a useful guide but an imperfect one, since nonspecific binding of an isotype does not always match that of the specific antibody.

Knockout tissue, from an animal or cell lacking the target, is the strongest negative control. The stain should be absent in the knockout, and its presence shows that the antibody binds something else. Where knockout tissue is not available, a peptide that blocks the antibody can be used to show that the stain depends on the specific binding site.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that is not accompanied by its controls cannot be judged, and each run should carry its controls with the results. The report should state the controls, their results and the action taken when a control failed.

The report must also state the antibody, its dilution and source, the retrieval and fixation conditions, the detection system, the scoring method and the number of sections and animals or donors examined. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the tissues whose biology the stain is meant to reveal, and the report should state the state of the tissue at the time of collection.
