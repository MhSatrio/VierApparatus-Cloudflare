Scoring turns a stained section into a number, and the number is only as good as the procedure that produced it. Two measures are usually combined. The intensity of staining is graded on a fixed scale, such as absent, weak, moderate or strong, and the proportion of positive cells is estimated in defined fields. The product of the two gives a score that can be compared across sections. The scale must be written down with examples, so that a score of moderate means the same thing in every section and to every scorer. Coons et al. (1941) established that the label reveals binding, and the score translates the strength of that binding into a category.

Image analysis offers a quantitative alternative. The stained area or the intensity of the signal is measured by software after the colours are separated. The method is more objective, but it depends on the thresholds set and on the colour separation, and these choices must be fixed and reported. A threshold chosen after seeing the images is a form of selection, as Ioannidis (2005) described.

Blinded scoring reduces bias. The scorer does not know the group to which the section belongs, and the slides are labelled with codes until the scoring is complete. Scoring that is not blind can be influenced by expectation, and the influence is strongest for borderline cases. The method of blinding is stated in the report.

Agreement between scorers is measured. Two or more scorers score the same sections, and their agreement is quantified, commonly with a statistic that corrects for chance. Low agreement shows that the scale is not clear enough, and the scale is refined and the scorers retrained before the main scoring. Platt (1964) recommended a test that could fail, and an agreement measure is such a test of the procedure.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell types and compartments scored, and the score should be read against them.
