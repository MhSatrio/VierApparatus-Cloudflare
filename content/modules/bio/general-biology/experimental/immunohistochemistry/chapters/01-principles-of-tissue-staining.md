A tissue section is a thin slice through an organ, and the cells within it keep their positions, their neighbours and their architecture. Immunohistochemistry applies an antibody to such a section and reveals where it binds. Coons et al. (1941) established that an antibody carrying a visible label can show the location of its antigen, and the tissue stain is the application of that principle to a whole organ. The information is spatial: the stain shows which cells express the protein, in which layer and in what proportion.

A homogenate measures the total amount of a protein in a sample, and the information about location is lost when the tissue is ground up. The two methods answer different questions. A blot can tell that a protein is present at a higher level in one tissue, and a stain can tell that the increase lies in a specific cell population or in a specific compartment. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the tissue organisation that makes the location meaningful, since a protein in one cell type may have a function that is absent in another.

The stain is an interpretation of the section, and the interpretation depends on the cells recognised. A positive cell must be identified by its morphology, and the stain is read against a counterstain that shows the nuclei and the structure of the tissue. Ioannidis (2005) described how a result that is read by eye can be influenced by expectation, and the scoring should be defined in advance and performed blind where possible.

The section is also a sample of a larger organ. A single section may not represent the whole tissue, and the sampling must be planned so that the regions examined are representative. Platt (1964) argued that a test should be designed to distinguish between explanations, and the controls and the sampling are the means by which such a distinction is made.

The location of a stain is therefore as informative as its intensity, and the method should be used to answer questions about place.
