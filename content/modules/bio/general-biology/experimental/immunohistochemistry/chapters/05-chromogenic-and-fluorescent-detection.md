Chromogenic detection uses an enzyme on the secondary antibody to convert a soluble substrate into an insoluble coloured product that deposits at the site of binding. The product remains on the slide, so the section can be examined under a standard microscope, stored for years and reviewed by others. Diaminobenzidine is a common substrate that gives a brown precipitate with peroxidase. The result is permanent and suited to clinical and archival use, but only one target is usually revealed per section, unless the colours are separated by sequential staining.

Fluorescent detection uses a fluorophore conjugated to the secondary antibody, which is excited by light and emits at a longer wavelength. Coons et al. (1941) introduced this approach, and it allows several targets on one section, each in a different colour, which is valuable when the relation between two proteins is the question. The signal is also linear over a wider range than many chromogenic reactions. The disadvantage is fading: the fluorophore is destroyed by light, and the section must be protected, imaged promptly and compared under identical conditions.

Imaging must be controlled. The exposure, the gain and the background settings are fixed for all sections in a comparison, and the raw images are archived unaltered. A brightness adjustment applied to one image but not another changes the apparent staining, so the processing is applied equally and recorded.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the choice of method can change which results appear, and the detection system should be stated with each result. The choice between the two methods follows the question: a chromogenic stain is better for a single target in a permanent record, and a fluorescent stain is better for co-localisation.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the co-localisation of proteins within cells, which fluorescent detection is able to reveal.
