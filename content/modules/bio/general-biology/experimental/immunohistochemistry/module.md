# Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry shows where a protein is located within a tissue. An antibody is applied to a thin section, and the bound antibody is revealed by a label, so that the stain marks the cells and structures that contain the target. Coons et al. (1941) introduced the labelling of antibodies to reveal their binding in tissue, and the method extends that principle to the architecture of the organ. The result combines the identity of the target with its place in the tissue, which a homogenate cannot provide.

The chapters follow the sequence of a section. The principles of the method come first, followed by the fixation and processing of the tissue, which preserve its structure while altering the antigens. Antigen retrieval reverses some of the masking that fixation causes, and the incubation with antibody and the detection by chromogenic or fluorescent labels follow. Counterstaining reveals the morphology against which the target is judged, and the scoring of staining is then described, together with the controls and the reporting.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein's structure determines its recognition, and fixation can alter the structure so that an epitope is hidden or changed. The method therefore depends on steps that are not in the antibody itself. Ioannidis (2005) described how a stain that looks convincing in one section can be a product of the conditions, and Platt (1964) recommended controls that can reveal the artefact. The chapters treat these controls as central to the method.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the tissue architecture and cell types that the stains reveal, which gives the biological frame for each finding.
