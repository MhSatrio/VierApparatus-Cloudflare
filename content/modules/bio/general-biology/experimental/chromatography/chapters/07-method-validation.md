A chromatographic method produces results only if it has been shown to work for its intended purpose. Validation establishes this by testing the parameters that determine reliability. Selectivity shows that the method separates the analyte from the other components of the sample, so that no other compound contributes to its peak. Linearity and range show the concentrations over which the response is proportional. Accuracy compares the measured value with a known reference, and precision measures the scatter of replicate measurements.

Recovery is the proportion of a known amount added to a sample that is measured after processing. Low recovery shows that the analyte is lost during extraction or separation, and it must be corrected or the method redesigned. Platt (1964) recommended tests that can distinguish between alternatives, and recovery and selectivity tests are of this kind, since they separate the analyte from losses and from interference.

Robustness is the resistance of the method to small changes in its conditions, such as the temperature, the pH of the buffer, the flow rate or the batch of the column. A method that is sensitive to small changes will give different results in different laboratories, and robustness testing reveals which conditions must be controlled.

The validation must be documented. The protocol, the acceptance criteria and the results are recorded, so that the method can be reproduced and its performance judged. Transfer to a new laboratory requires a repeat of the key tests.

Ioannidis (2005) described how results that depend on unreported conditions can fail to replicate, and validation makes the conditions and the limits of the method explicit. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the analytes that such methods measure. The validation report should be retained with the data it supports.
