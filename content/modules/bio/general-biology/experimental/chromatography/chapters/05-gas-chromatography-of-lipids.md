Fatty acids are carboxylic acids with long hydrocarbon chains, and their free form is too polar and too involatile for gas chromatography. They are converted into fatty acid methyl esters, in which the carboxyl group is methylated, which are volatile enough to vaporise in the injector and pass through the column. The derivatisation is typically carried out with methanol and an acid or base catalyst, and it must be complete for the profile to represent the sample.

The methyl esters are separated by boiling point and polarity. Shorter chains and more saturated esters generally elute earlier, and the degree of unsaturation changes the retention in a way that depends on the column. The separation of cis and trans isomers requires a polar column with a long length. The profile of a sample therefore reflects its fatty acid composition, which is the property of the membrane lipids discussed in the Biological Macromolecules module.

Identification is by retention time, compared with standards of known fatty acids, and confirmed by mass spectrometry when needed. Quantification is by peak area, normalised to an internal standard, typically a fatty acid that is not present in the sample, such as one with an odd number of carbons.

The profile is a relative measure. The percentage of each fatty acid is the proportion of the total detected, which depends on the derivatisation and on the detector response for each compound. Absolute amounts require calibration with standards for each fatty acid.

Ioannidis (2005) described how profiles can be over-interpreted when the differences are small and the sample is limited. The variation between replicates should be estimated, and differences should be compared with it.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the membrane lipids whose composition is measured. The protocol should record the extraction, the derivatisation, the column, the temperature program and the internal standard.
