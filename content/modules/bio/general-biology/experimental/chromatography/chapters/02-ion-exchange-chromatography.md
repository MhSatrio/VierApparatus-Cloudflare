Ion-exchange chromatography separates molecules by their net charge. The resin carries fixed charges, and molecules of opposite charge bind to it reversibly. A cation exchanger, with negative groups, binds positively charged molecules, and an anion exchanger, with positive groups, binds negatively charged ones. The net charge of a protein depends on the pH of the buffer relative to its isoelectric point, a relation that Anfinsen (1973) did not address directly but that follows from the ionisable side chains that the folded structure presents.

The choice of resin depends on the pH. A protein with an isoelectric point of 5 bound to an anion exchanger at pH 8 carries a net negative charge and binds, while the same protein at pH 4 carries a net positive charge and passes through. The chromatographer selects the pH so that the target binds and most contaminants do not, which is a central principle of the method.

Bound proteins are eluted by increasing the ionic strength of the buffer, typically with a salt gradient, or by changing the pH to neutralise the protein's charge. A linear gradient separates proteins with different binding strengths, and a step elution is quicker but gives less resolution. The gradient shape must be recorded, since small changes in its slope change the separation.

Column capacity limits the amount of protein that can be loaded. Overloading causes proteins to pass through unbound, and the fraction of the target lost in the flow-through must be measured. The capacity depends on the resin and the protein.

Ioannidis (2005) described how results that are sensitive to conditions can fail to replicate, and the pH, the buffer and the gradient are conditions that should be specified precisely and reproduced exactly.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the charged groups of proteins and their role in binding. The protocol should record the resin, the pH, the buffer and the gradient, and it should report the recovery of the target.
