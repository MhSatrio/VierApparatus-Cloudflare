Size-exclusion chromatography separates molecules by passing a sample through a column packed with porous beads. Small molecules enter the pores and take a longer path, so they elute later. Large molecules cannot enter the pores and pass around the beads, so they elute first. The separation depends on size and shape rather than on charge or binding, which makes the method gentle, and proteins usually retain their activity through it. Anfinsen (1973) showed that a folded protein has a defined size and shape, and the elution volume reports them.

The column is calibrated with standard proteins of known mass. The elution volume of each standard is plotted against the logarithm of its mass, and the calibration allows the apparent mass of an unknown to be estimated. The estimate is only apparent, because the shape of a protein affects its elution, and an elongated protein elutes earlier than a compact one of the same mass. Comparison with a standard of similar shape gives a better estimate.

The method can reveal the oligomeric state of a protein. A protein that elutes at the mass of a dimer is probably a dimer, though a dimer and an elongated monomer can be difficult to distinguish. Combining the elution with a second method, such as light scattering or mass spectrometry, resolves the ambiguity.

Resolution depends on the bead range and the column length. The separation range is limited, so the appropriate column must be chosen for the size of the molecules. Dilution during the separation lowers the concentration of the sample, and the protein may need to be concentrated before loading.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the interpretation of a single chromatogram can be overinterpreted, and the calibration, the standards and the criteria for assigning a species should be recorded and fixed before the analysis.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the proteins whose size is characterised by this method. The protocol should record the column, the standards, the buffer and the flow rate, since each affects the elution volume.
