Thin-layer chromatography separates compounds on a plate coated with a thin layer of adsorbent, usually silica. The sample is spotted near one edge, and the plate is placed in a shallow solvent, which rises by capillary action and carries the compounds at different rates. Compounds that adsorb strongly to the silica move less far, and those that dissolve well in the solvent move further. The method is quick, cheap and needs little equipment, which makes it valuable for screening many samples at once.

The position of each compound is expressed as the retention factor, the distance it travels divided by the distance travelled by the solvent front. The value is characteristic of the compound under fixed conditions, but it depends on the solvent, the plate and the temperature, and it is not an absolute property. A reference compound run alongside the sample gives a comparison that is more reliable than the absolute value.

Visualisation depends on the compounds. Some are visible under ultraviolet light, and others are revealed by spraying with a reagent that reacts with them to produce a colour. The choice of reagent determines which compounds are seen, and the absence of a spot does not mean the compound is absent if the reagent does not react with it.

Thin-layer chromatography is a screening method rather than a quantitative one. Spot intensities can be measured by densitometry, but the response is variable, and the result is less precise than that of column chromatography. The method is best used to decide which samples warrant a more rigorous analysis.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a method that is fast and visually interpreted can be influenced by expectation, and the reading of plates should be done blind to the treatment where possible. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the compounds that such screening can reveal. The protocol should record the plate, the solvent system, the reference compounds and the visualisation method.
