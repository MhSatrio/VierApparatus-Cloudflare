Reversed-phase chromatography uses a nonpolar stationary phase, usually a silica support carrying long hydrocarbon chains, and a polar mobile phase of water with an organic solvent such as acetonitrile or methanol. Hydrophobic molecules bind the stationary phase strongly and elute only when the proportion of organic solvent is high, while polar molecules elute early. A gradient that increases the organic solvent gradually separates a mixture of metabolites by their hydrophobicity, which is the property discussed in the Water module.

Many biological metabolites are polar, such as sugars, nucleotides and amino acids, and they are poorly retained on a standard reversed-phase column. They elute close to the void volume, where they are not separated. Ion-pairing reagents, which carry a charge opposite to the metabolite and a hydrophobic tail, bind the metabolite and increase its retention. Alternatively, hydrophilic interaction chromatography uses a polar stationary phase that retains polar compounds, and the choice between the approaches depends on the metabolites of interest.

The method is widely used in metabolomics, where a sample may contain hundreds of compounds. The separation is combined with mass spectrometry, as described in the Laboratory Instrumentation module, and the identification depends on both the retention time and the mass. Retention times shift with small changes in the column and the mobile phase, so an internal standard or a retention time standard is needed to align the data across runs.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the choice of method can change which compounds are detected, and metabolomic studies must report the method so that the coverage of the metabolome can be judged.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the metabolites measured and the pathways that produce them. The protocol should record the column, the gradient, the mobile phase, the ion-pairing agent if used and the internal standards.
