# Chromatography

The Laboratory Instrumentation module explained how a chromatograph separates a mixture and how its detector responds. This topic treats chromatography as a set of complete methods, each chosen for a particular property of the molecules to be separated. Choosing the method, designing the separation, validating it and reporting it are the work that makes a chromatographic result useful.

The chapters cover the methods in the order that a biologist is likely to need them: size-exclusion for separation by size, ion-exchange for separation by charge, affinity for separation by a specific binding interaction, reversed-phase for metabolites, gas chromatography for fatty acids, thin-layer chromatography for rapid screening, and the validation and preparative use of the methods. Each chapter gives the logic of the separation and the points at which the method fails.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that a protein's properties follow from its structure, and the separation methods exploit the properties that structure produces: its size, its charge distribution and its binding sites. Ioannidis (2005) described how the choices made in a separation can change the result, and the chapters identify the choices that must be fixed in advance.

Readers should note that most chromatographic methods are used in combination, and a purification or an analysis typically combines two or more of them.
