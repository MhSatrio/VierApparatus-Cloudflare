Fluorescence is detected against a dark background, so it is far more sensitive than absorbance, which measures a small loss of light against a large signal. A fluorescent measurement can detect quantities a thousand times smaller than an absorbance measurement, which is why fluorescence is used for low-abundance samples and for single-molecule work of the kind Betzig and colleagues (2006) described. The sensitivity is bought at a price, because the fluorescence is more sensitive to conditions than absorbance is.

Dyes that bind nucleic acids are widely used for quantification. Their fluorescence increases many times on binding DNA, and the signal is proportional to the amount of double-stranded DNA within a range. Because the dye binds specifically to nucleic acid, it is less affected by protein contamination than absorbance, which makes it a useful check on the absorbance measurement described in the previous chapter.

The inner filter effect occurs when the sample absorbs the excitation or emission light, so that the measured fluorescence is less than the true value. It is serious at high concentrations and in coloured samples. Quenching, the reduction of fluorescence by other molecules, can have the same effect. Both are reduced by dilution, and both can be corrected by measuring the absorbance of the sample at the excitation and emission wavelengths.

Quantitative fluorescence requires a calibration curve made with the same dye, buffer and instrument settings as the sample. The response is linear only within a range, and the samples must be diluted into it. The sensitivity of the instrument must be fixed and recorded, since a change of gain changes the reading without changing the sample.

Ioannidis (2005) described how sensitive methods can detect effects that are not biologically meaningful, and fluorescence measurements require the same validation as other assays. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the fluorophores of common biological molecules. The protocol should report the dye, the wavelengths, the calibration and the correction for inner filter effects.
