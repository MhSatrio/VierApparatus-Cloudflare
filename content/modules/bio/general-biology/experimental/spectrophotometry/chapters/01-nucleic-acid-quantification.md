Nucleic acids absorb ultraviolet light strongly at 260 nanometres, because their bases are aromatic. The bases of DNA and RNA, which Watson and Crick (1953) described in the double helix, are responsible for the absorption. A standard conversion gives the concentration of double-stranded DNA as about fifty nanograms per microlitre for an absorbance of one at a one-centimetre path length, and single-stranded RNA and oligonucleotides have different conversion factors. The factor used must match the molecule, or the concentration will be wrong.

Purity is judged by the ratio of absorbance at 260 nanometres to that at 280 nanometres. Proteins absorb at 280 nanometres through their aromatic amino acids, so a ratio near 1.8 for DNA and 2.0 for RNA indicates little protein contamination. A low ratio suggests protein or phenol carryover from the extraction. The ratio at 260 to 230 nanometres detects contaminants such as guanidinium salts, carbohydrates and residual solvents, which absorb near 230 nanometres, and a low value indicates contamination that may interfere with enzymes.

The ratios are indicators, not proof. A sample can give an acceptable ratio and still contain contaminants that absorb at similar wavelengths, and a degraded sample can give misleading concentrations because fragments absorb as well as intact molecules. Gel electrophoresis, described in the Electrophoresis topic, shows the size and integrity of the nucleic acid, and it should accompany the spectrophotometric measurement.

The blank must be the same buffer as the sample. Water is often used, but a sample in a buffer that absorbs at 260 nanometres will be overestimated unless the buffer is subtracted. The measurement must be made in the linear range, and very concentrated samples should be diluted before measurement.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a single reading can be misleading when it is not checked. The concentration and purity should be confirmed by a second method for samples that matter, such as quantitative fluorescence with a dye that binds the nucleic acid specifically.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the nucleic acids and the extraction methods that produce them. The protocol should record the instrument, the path length, the blank and the conversion factor used.
