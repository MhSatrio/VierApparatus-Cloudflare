Every spectrophotometric assay can be disturbed by something other than the molecule it is designed to measure. Buffers contain reducing agents, detergents, salts and chelators, and many of them absorb light or alter the chemistry of the dye or the enzyme. Bradford (1976) noted that the dye-binding response depends on the composition of the sample, and interference of this kind is the most common cause of inaccurate protein measurements.

Turbidity is a frequent problem. Particles in the sample scatter light, which increases the apparent absorbance at every wavelength, and the scattering is often mistaken for the absorbance of the molecule. A reading at a wavelength where the molecule does not absorb can reveal turbidity, and the sample should be clarified by centrifugation or filtration before measurement.

Coloured compounds, such as the pigments of plant extracts or the haemoglobin of blood, absorb at the wavelength of the assay and add their own absorbance. A sample blank that contains everything except the molecule of interest identifies this contribution, and it must be subtracted.

Reducing agents such as dithiothreitol and mercaptoethanol interfere with several protein and copper-based assays. Each assay has its own tolerance, and the standards must contain the same concentration of the agent as the samples. Chelators such as EDTA interfere with metal-dependent enzymes and with some colorimetric reagents.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the failure to check for interference can produce findings that do not replicate. The controls that reveal interference are simple: spike a known amount of the standard into the sample and measure the recovery, and measure a sample lacking the analyte. A recovery far from one hundred per cent indicates that the matrix alters the response.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the molecules in cells and extracts that can interfere. The protocol should describe the matrix of each sample and report the recovery and the blank controls used.
