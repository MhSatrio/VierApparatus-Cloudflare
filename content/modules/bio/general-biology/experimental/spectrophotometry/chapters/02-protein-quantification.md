Bradford (1976) described a rapid assay in which the dye Coomassie Brilliant Blue binds protein and shifts its absorbance. The dye binds mainly to arginine and to aromatic residues, and to the protein in general, and the increase in absorbance is proportional to the amount of protein within a range. The assay is quick, inexpensive and compatible with many buffers, which made it one of the most widely used methods in biochemistry.

The response depends on the protein. Two proteins of the same mass but different composition give different responses, because the dye binds some residues more strongly than others. The standard should therefore be a protein similar to the sample, and bovine serum albumin is commonly used. If the protein differs greatly from the standard, the concentration is only an estimate, and the error must be stated.

Interference is common. Detergents, reducing agents and high concentrations of salt can alter the dye response, and the standards must be made in the same buffer as the sample. Ultraviolet absorbance at 280 nanometres is a simpler method for purified proteins with aromatic residues, but it is affected by nucleic acids, which absorb at 260 nanometres and contribute at 280 nanometres. The bicinchoninic acid method is less sensitive to some detergents, but it responds to reducing agents and to the peptide bonds themselves.

The calibration curve must cover the range of the samples, and it should be linear or fitted with a model that describes its curvature. Dilution of the sample into the range of the curve is usually required, and the dilution must be accounted for.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the choice of method and standard can change a result, and a protein concentration used to set a loading amount or an activity should be checked by a second method when it matters.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the proteins that these assays measure and the purification steps that precede them. The protocol should record the standard, the buffer, the curve and the method used.
