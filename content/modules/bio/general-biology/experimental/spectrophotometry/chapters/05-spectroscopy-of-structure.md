Spectroscopy reports the structure of biological molecules without the crystallisation that X-ray crystallography requires. Circular dichroism measures the difference in absorption of left- and right-circularly polarised light, which is sensitive to the arrangement of the peptide backbone. Alpha helices and beta sheets produce characteristic signals in the far ultraviolet, and their proportions can be estimated by fitting the spectrum to reference spectra. Anfinsen (1973) showed that the structure of a protein determines its function, and the spectrum reports that structure in solution.

Intrinsic fluorescence arises from tryptophan and tyrosine residues. Their emission depends on their environment, and a change in folding or in ligand binding often shifts the emission wavelength or the intensity. The measurement is sensitive to the local environment of the residues, which makes it useful for following folding and binding in real time.

Ultraviolet difference spectra detect changes in the absorbance of aromatic residues or of bound ligands upon binding, and they can determine binding constants when the change is measured across a range of ligand concentrations. The same principle applies to nucleic acid structure, where the hyperchromicity of melted DNA reports the separation of its strands.

The estimates have limits. The secondary structure fitted from a circular dichroism spectrum depends on the reference set used, and the estimates for disordered proteins and for membrane proteins are less reliable. Ioannidis (2005) described how a model fitted to noisy data can give apparently precise results that are not robust, and the uncertainty of spectroscopic estimates should be reported.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the structural features that these methods detect. The protocol should report the buffer, the concentration, the path length, the reference set and the fitting procedure, since each affects the result.
