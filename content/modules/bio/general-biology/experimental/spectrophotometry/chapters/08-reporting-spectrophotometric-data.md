A spectrophotometric value means little unless the steps that produced it are known. A report should state the instrument and its calibration, the wavelength, the path length, the buffer and the blank, the standards and their fit, the controls and the number of replicates. It should also show how the reported value was calculated from the raw signal, including any dilution, correction or conversion factor. Without these details, a reader cannot judge the value or repeat the measurement.

The raw signal, the derived concentration and the reported value are different quantities, and each should be recorded. The raw absorbance or fluorescence is the observation. The concentration is the observation converted through a standard curve. The reported value may be an average of replicates, corrected for dilution and expressed with its uncertainty. Mixing these levels without saying which is which is a common source of confusion.

Uncertainty must be reported. A single number without an error hides the precision of the measurement and invites the reader to treat small differences as real. The standard deviation or confidence interval of the replicates, and the uncertainty of the standard curve, should appear with the result, and differences smaller than the uncertainty should not be interpreted as effects.

Ioannidis (2005) described how selective reporting distorts the literature, and spectrophotometric data are no exception. Failed assays, excluded replicates and rejected standards should be reported with the reasons, so that readers can see the full set of measurements that informed the result.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the conventions for reporting biochemical measurements in the molecular and cell biology literature. Raw data should be archived with the report, so that the calculation can be checked and the result reanalysed if the calibration or the conversion is later found to be wrong.
