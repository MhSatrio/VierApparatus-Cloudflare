Pre-registration is the public recording of a study's hypotheses, design and analysis plan before the data are collected. It allows readers to distinguish between confirmatory work, in which a prediction stated in advance is tested, and exploratory work, in which patterns are sought in data that have already been seen. Both are valuable, but they carry different levels of evidence, and the results of exploratory work need replication before they are trusted.

The content of a pre-registration usually includes the question, the hypotheses, the sample size and its justification, the design and allocation, the primary outcome, the exclusion criteria and the primary analysis. Secondary analyses can be listed as well, and any analysis not in the plan should be labelled as exploratory when it is reported. The registration is time-stamped, so that the plan can be shown to precede the data.

Registered reports go further. The protocol is reviewed by journals before the data are collected, and the decision to publish is made on the basis of the question and the design rather than on the result. The format removes the incentive to report only positive findings, which Ioannidis (2005) identified as a major source of unreliable results.

Pre-registration does not guarantee good science. A poor design registered in advance is still poor, and an exploratory study can be useful even if it is not confirmatory. The benefit is that readers can see what was planned, and they can judge the strength of the evidence accordingly.

Platt (1964) recommended that experiments be designed around explicit alternatives, and pre-registration makes the alternatives and the decisive test public. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the measurements that a registration specifies. Investigators who register studies commit to reporting the results whatever they are, which is the central requirement of the practice.
