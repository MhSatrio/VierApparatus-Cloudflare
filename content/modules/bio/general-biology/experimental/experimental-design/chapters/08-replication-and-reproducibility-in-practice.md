Reproducibility means that an analysis can be repeated on the same data and give the same result. Replication means that the experiment can be repeated with new data and give a consistent result. The two are related but distinct. A reproducible analysis guards against errors in the processing of the data, while a replication guards against findings that depend on the particular sample, laboratory or conditions in which they were first observed.

Ioannidis (2005) predicted that many findings would fail to replicate, and several fields have since undertaken large replication projects that confirmed the concern in some areas. The failures do not always mean that the original finding was false. A replication can fail because the original effect was overestimated, because the replication used a different population or because the conditions differed in ways that matter.

The interpretation of a failed replication requires care. The investigator should ask whether the replication had adequate power, whether the methods matched the original, and whether the effect size in the replication is consistent with the original within its uncertainty. A replication with low power that fails to detect an effect is uninformative, while a well-powered replication with a clear null result is strong evidence against the original effect.

Practices that improve reproducibility include sharing the raw data, the code and the protocols, recording the versions of reagents and software and describing the methods in enough detail that another group can follow them. Platt (1964) argued that science progresses when the findings of one laboratory can be used by others, and the sharing of methods is the means of achieving this.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the reporting conventions of molecular and cell biology. The best protection against irreproducible results is a combination of pre-specified designs, transparent analysis and independent replication.
