The power of a study is the probability that it will detect an effect of a stated size, if that effect is real. Power depends on four quantities: the size of the effect, the variability of the measurement, the significance threshold chosen for the test, and the number of samples. Increasing the number of samples increases power, reducing the variability increases power, and a larger effect is easier to detect. A study designed to detect a small effect with few samples has low power, and most of its results will be inconclusive.

The sample size needed for a given power is calculated before the experiment. The investigator states the smallest effect that would matter, estimates the variability from previous work or a pilot, chooses the significance threshold and the desired power, typically eighty per cent, and solves for the number of samples. The calculation is an explicit statement of what the study is able to show, and it prevents the common error of collecting too few samples and hoping for a clear result.

Underpowered studies are a serious problem. They are unlikely to detect real effects, and when they do report a significant result, the estimated effect is likely to be exaggerated, because only the chance overestimates pass the threshold. Ioannidis (2005) identified low power as one of the main reasons that a large share of published findings may be false.

Power calculations depend on assumptions. The effect size is often taken from earlier studies that may themselves be overestimated, and the variability may differ in the new setting. The calculation should therefore be repeated under a range of plausible assumptions, and the chosen sample size should give adequate power across that range.

Platt (1964) argued that an experiment should be capable of distinguishing between alternatives, and power is the quantitative expression of that capability. An experiment with no realistic chance of distinguishing the hypotheses is not informative, however carefully it is performed.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the biological variability of the systems studied. Power is improved by reducing variation through controlled conditions and by increasing samples, and the trade-offs are a matter of experimental judgement.
