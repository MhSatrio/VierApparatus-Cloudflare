# Experimental Design

The Foundations module introduced the logic of experiments: a comparison, a control and replication. This module develops that logic into the planning of real studies. Advanced experimental design asks how many samples are needed to detect an effect of a given size, how to allocate samples to treatments so that bias is excluded, how to arrange several factors in one experiment, and how to commit to an analysis before the data are collected.

The chapters address the question in the order that a researcher meets it. They begin with the question of power and sample size, then turn to randomisation and blinding, the choice of design for several factors, the handling of repeated and nested data, the control of bias and the pre-registration of studies. Each chapter explains why a choice matters for the reliability of the conclusion.

Ioannidis (2005) showed that many published findings are unlikely to be true when the studies are small, flexibly analysed and selectively reported. Good design is the first defence against these problems, and the module treats the design as the place where reliability is secured.

Platt (1964) argued that fields progress fastest when experiments are designed to exclude alternative explanations. The module applies that principle throughout.
