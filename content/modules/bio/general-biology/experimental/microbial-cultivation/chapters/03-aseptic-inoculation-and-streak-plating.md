Streak plating dilutes a mixed sample across the surface of an agar plate, so that some cells land far enough apart to form separate colonies. The inoculating loop picks up material from the sample and draws it across one section of the plate. The loop is then sterilised, cooled and drawn through the last streak into a fresh section, and the process is repeated across the plate. Each pass thins the cells, and the final sections carry too few cells to touch one another, which allows them to grow as isolated colonies.

A pure culture is established from a single colony. The colony is picked with a sterile loop or needle and streaked onto a fresh plate, where it should produce uniform colonies. Staley and Konopka (1985) noted that the isolation of organisms from nature depends on this step, since a colony of one appearance may conceal several organisms with similar growth.

The purity of an isolate is checked by its appearance. Colonies should share their size, shape, colour and edge, and a single streak should show only one type. The cells viewed under the microscope should match in morphology. A plate with two colony types is mixed, and the process must be repeated from a single colony of each type.

Aseptic handling is essential, because the plate is exposed to the air and to the operator. The work is done near a flame or in a cabinet, the lids are opened only as far as needed and the loop is cooled before touching a colony, since heat kills the cells it touches.

Ioannidis (2005) described how the claim of a pure culture rests on a visual check that can miss a second organism, and the isolate should be confirmed by a second method. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell morphology that the microscope reveals.
