A bacterial population placed in fresh medium follows a characteristic pattern. In the lag phase, the cells adjust to the new conditions, synthesising enzymes and building their components, and little division occurs. In the exponential phase, each cell divides at a constant rate, and the population doubles at regular intervals. The stationary phase follows as the nutrients are used up or waste accumulates, and division stops as the number of new cells balances the number that die. The death phase then sees the population decline. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell cycle that underlies these divisions, and the exponential phase is the period in which the cell cycle runs unconstrained.

The doubling time is calculated from the exponential phase, where the logarithm of the cell count rises in a straight line. The slope of this line gives the growth rate, and the doubling time is the time for the population to double, which is the logarithm of two divided by the rate. The calculation is valid only in the exponential phase, and including the lag or the stationary phase distorts it. Staley and Konopka (1985) emphasised that the rate of growth in nature is usually far below the rate in rich laboratory media, and the laboratory value describes the organism under favourable conditions.

Nutrients, oxygen and temperature determine the rate and the final density. A limiting nutrient sets the maximum population, and a change in temperature shifts the rate of every enzyme. Oxygen matters for aerobes and for their respiration, and it may be limited in dense cultures.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a growth curve from one condition can be taken as the behaviour of the species, and the conditions should be stated. Each curve is a description of one culture rather than a fixed property of the organism.
