A bacterium that is alive may still fail to form a colony. Oliver (2010) reviewed the state in which cells enter a dormant condition after stress, such as nutrient starvation, low temperature or exposure to salt, and they remain intact and metabolically active while losing the ability to divide on the medium used. The state is called viable but nonculturable. Cells in it do not form colonies, so a plate count will record them as dead, although they retain the potential to resume growth under other conditions.

The state matters for interpretation. Staley and Konopka (1985) emphasised that the cultivable fraction of microbes in nature is often a small percentage of the total, and a colony count can therefore greatly understate the living population. A low count after an environmental stress, a treatment or a disinfectant may reflect the entry into the dormant state rather than death, and a conclusion of sterilisation based on a plate count is not justified without further evidence.

Independent measures reveal the state. A live-dead stain uses dyes that distinguish intact membranes from damaged ones, and a respiration assay measures activity by the reduction of a tetrazolium dye. Measuring a transcript or an ATP level gives another view of metabolic activity. Platt (1964) recommended a test that discriminates between alternatives, and combining a culture count with one of these measures distinguishes a dead population from a dormant one.

Resuscitation, the return of the cells to growth, is evidence for dormancy. The cells are given a condition that promotes growth, such as a richer medium or a return to a favourable temperature, and the appearance of colonies shows that they were alive. Oliver (2010) noted that resuscitation is not always achieved, so its failure does not prove death.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a method that measures one property can be taken as a measure of another, and the limits of the colony count should be stated when it is used to judge survival. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the stress responses of cells that underlie dormancy.
