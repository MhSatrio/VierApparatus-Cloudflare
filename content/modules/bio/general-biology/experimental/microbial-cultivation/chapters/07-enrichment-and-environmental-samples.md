Environmental samples contain communities of microbes in which the organisms of interest may be rare. Enrichment culture raises their proportion by supplying a substrate that the target uses and that most other organisms cannot use efficiently. A sample from a sediment, for example, might be incubated with a sulphur compound as the sole energy source, so that the organisms able to use it grow and the rest decline. The method reveals organisms with a particular function, which is often invisible in a direct count.

Staley and Konopka (1985) described how the activities of microbes in nature were measured and compared with laboratory cultures, and they showed that the organisms that grow in enrichment may be only a small part of the community doing the work in the habitat. The enrichment therefore answers a question about what can grow under the conditions supplied, which is not the same as a question about what the habitat is doing.

Enrichment changes the community. The selective pressure favours some organisms and excludes others, and the composition after a few transfers may differ greatly from the original. The changes must be followed by sampling at each step, and the community should be characterised by a method independent of the cultivation.

The organisms recovered are interpreted with care. Their presence shows that they can grow on the substrate in the laboratory, and their importance in the environment depends on their abundance and activity there. Oliver (2010) noted that the active fraction may include cells that do not grow on the enrichment medium, and the enrichment will miss them.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a method that selects for an outcome can make that outcome appear common, and the enrichment conditions should be reported with any claim about the habitat. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the metabolic pathways that the enrichment is designed to test.
