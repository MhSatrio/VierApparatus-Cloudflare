A complex medium contains undefined components, such as peptone, yeast extract or meat extract, that supply a broad range of nutrients. It supports many organisms and is the usual choice for isolation from mixed samples, where the aim is to grow whatever is present. A defined medium lists every component at a known concentration, which gives reproducibility and control over the nutrients, but it may not support organisms that need factors present only in complex materials. Staley and Konopka (1985) noted that many environmental microbes grow poorly on the rich media used in the laboratory, and the medium therefore determines which organisms can be seen.

Solid media are made by adding agar, a polysaccharide that remains solid at the temperatures at which microbes grow. A single cell that lands on the surface forms a colony, which is a clone of its descendants. The colony is the basis of isolation, since a pure culture can be picked from a single colony.

Selective media contain substances that suppress most organisms while allowing the target to grow. Antibiotics, dyes, high salt and extreme pH are common selectors, and the choice follows the properties of the target. A differential medium contains an indicator that reveals a metabolic property, for example a colour change when a sugar is fermented. Selective and differential media are often combined.

The limits of selection must be recognised. A selective medium excludes most organisms, but some non-target organisms may grow, and some target organisms may be inhibited. The selectivity is tested with known strains before the medium is used on samples.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a method that reports only the organisms that grow may give a misleading picture of the community, and the limits of the medium should be stated with the results. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the nutrients and metabolic pathways that the media exploit.
