The colony count estimates the number of cells that can form a colony, which is a measure of viability in the culture sense. The sample is diluted in a series, usually by a factor of ten at each step, and an aliquot of each dilution is spread on a plate. After incubation, the colonies on each plate are counted. A plate that carries between about thirty and three hundred colonies is in the countable range, where the count is most precise. Plates with too many colonies are crowded and merge, and plates with too few give large relative errors from chance.

The concentration is calculated from the count on the chosen plate, the dilution factor and the volume plated. If 150 colonies grow from 0.1 millilitre of a dilution of one in ten thousand, the original sample contains 150 divided by 0.1, multiplied by ten thousand, colony-forming units per millilitre. The result is reported as colony-forming units, not cells, since a colony may arise from a clump of cells or from one.

The uncertainty of a plate count is largely statistical. Colonies arise from random cells landing on the plate, and the variance of such a count is close to its mean. The standard error therefore falls as the count rises, and a count of ten has a relative error of about thirty per cent. Replicate plates reduce the error, and the uncertainty should be reported.

Oliver (2010) reviewed the state in which bacteria remain alive but do not form colonies, and this is a major limit of the method. A low count may reflect dead cells, cells that are alive but not cultivable under the conditions, or cells that have been damaged in the sample. Platt (1964) recommended an alternative test to distinguish these possibilities, such as a live-dead stain.

Ioannidis (2005) described how a count that is reported without its uncertainty can appear more precise than it is, and the dilutions and the plates used should be reported. Alberts et al. (2022) describe the division that gives rise to each colony.
