Incubation sets the environment in which the population grows. Temperature governs the rate of every enzyme, and each organism has an optimum, below which growth slows and above which proteins denature. Atmosphere determines whether aerobes receive the oxygen they need, and anaerobes must be kept free of it, often in a sealed chamber with a reducing agent. Agitation in liquid culture keeps the cells suspended, distributes oxygen and prevents the formation of gradients. Staley and Konopka (1985) noted that natural conditions differ widely from those of the incubator, and the chosen setting describes one condition only.

A growth curve is measured by sampling the culture at intervals. Optical density, the scattering of light by the cells, is quick and continuous, and it is the common measure. It is proportional to the cell mass only over a limited range, and at high density the light is absorbed by cells that shadow one another, so the reading flattens. Dilution of dense cultures before reading keeps the measure in the proportional range.

Viable counts, obtained by plating dilutions and counting colonies, measure the number of cells that can form colonies. They reveal the proportion of living cells, which optical density cannot. The two measures are reconciled by calibration: the optical density is plotted against the viable count across a range of densities to establish the relationship for the strain.

Platt (1964) argued that a measurement should be chosen to discriminate between alternatives, and the comparison of optical density with viable counts discriminates between growth and simple accumulation of dead cells. Ioannidis (2005) described how a single measure of growth can be taken to represent the whole process, and the calibration should be reported.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell division that the growth curve reflects.
