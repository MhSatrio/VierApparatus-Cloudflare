Microbial cultures can be hazardous, and the risk depends on the organism. Biosafety levels, from one to four, set the containment, the protective equipment and the work practices that match each level of hazard. Most environmental and educational cultures fall at the lowest levels, but a pathogen requires more stringent containment, and the level must be checked against the organism before work begins. Inactivation is the process that kills cultures before disposal, typically by autoclaving, and it must be verified for each type of waste so that no viable organism leaves the laboratory.

Safety is part of the method rather than an addition to it. A contaminated culture can invalidate a result, and an unsafe practice can endanger the person and the community. Ioannidis (2005) described how the cost of an error depends on what is done with the result, and in microbiology the cost may include harm beyond the laboratory.

The report must state the strain with its identifier, its source and the date it was obtained. The medium is described in full, including its selective components, and the incubation temperature, atmosphere and duration are stated. The counting method, the dilutions and the plates used are recorded, together with the uncertainty of the count.

Platt (1964) argued that a claim should be stated so that it can be tested, and a microbial result is testable only if the strain, the medium and the conditions are known. Staley and Konopka (1985) showed that results from environmental samples depend strongly on the conditions chosen, and the report should state the habitat and the sampling method.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell biology of the microbes that the biosafety levels classify, which underlies the hazards they present.
