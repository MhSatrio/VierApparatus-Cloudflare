# Microbial Cultivation

Microbes are the most abundant living things, and most of them cannot be studied without being grown first. Cultivation gives a pure population from a mixed sample, supplies enough material for analysis and allows a strain to be kept and shared. The Microscopy and Cell Culture topics treated cells that were grown for study, and this topic applies the same logic to organisms that live independently: bacteria, yeasts and fungi grown on defined media.

The chapters follow the work of isolation. The principles of microbial growth come first, followed by media and selective agars, aseptic inoculation and streak plating, incubation conditions and growth curves, and the counting of colony-forming units. Enrichment culture from environmental samples is then described, as developed in the work reviewed by Staley and Konopka (1985), who showed how activities of microbes in natural habitats can be measured. The final chapters address the recognition of viable but nonculturable cells, which Oliver (2010) reviewed, and the biosafety and reporting of cultures.

The count of colonies is a measure of cultivability, not of life, and the difference matters. Ioannidis (2005) described how a method that measures only one property can be taken for a measure of the whole, and Platt (1964) argued for tests that distinguish between alternatives. Culture-based counts are most informative when they are compared with an independent measure of viability.

Alberts et al. (2022) describe the cell biology of the microbes and the processes of growth and division that the cultures follow.
