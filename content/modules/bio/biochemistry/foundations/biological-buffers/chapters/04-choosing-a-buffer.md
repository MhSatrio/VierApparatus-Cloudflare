Many buffers can control the pH of a given experiment, so the choice among them depends on other criteria as well. A buffer that works well chemically may still be unsuitable if it binds the enzyme under study, absorbs ultraviolet light or reacts with a reagent in the mixture. Good et al. (1966) set out a set of criteria that remain the basis of buffer selection in biology, and they are summarized in this chapter.

The first criterion is the pKa. The buffer should have a pKa close to the target pH, so that its buffer range covers the experiment. The second criterion is solubility. The buffer must dissolve completely at the concentrations used, without forming precipitates when the solution is cooled or combined with other components. The third is membrane impermeability. A good biological buffer should remain outside cells, so that it does not change the internal chemistry of the cell in an uncontrolled way.

Good and colleagues also required that buffers should not bind metal ions strongly. Many enzymes depend on metal ions such as magnesium or zinc, and a buffer that binds these ions would remove them from the reaction. Buffers that show weak metal binding were therefore preferred for enzyme studies, and this criterion remains important today.

Stability is the next criterion. A buffer should not decompose in the presence of light, air or heat during storage and use. Compounds that are unstable can change their pKa or release products that interfere with the experiment. Good buffers are stable over the time scales of ordinary laboratory work, and their stability is tested before they are adopted.

Po and Senozan (2001) pointed out that the choice of buffer also depends on the temperature dependence of its pKa. A buffer whose pKa changes greatly with temperature is unreliable in experiments that are run at different temperatures. Chemists therefore prefer buffers with small temperature coefficients when the experiment will be warmed or cooled.

Optical clarity is another practical concern. Some buffers absorb light at wavelengths used in spectrophotometric assays, and their absorption can distort the measurement. A buffer chosen for such an assay should be transparent at the relevant wavelength, and the laboratory should check its absorbance before use.

Ball (2008) reminded readers that a buffer is part of a complex system and can interact with the solvent and with the molecules it contacts. Water structure, ionic strength and dissolved gases all influence buffer behavior, so a choice that works in one laboratory may need adjustment in another. A buffer should be tested under the conditions of the experiment, not only under ideal conditions.

It is useful to write down the reasons for choosing a buffer. A record of the criteria, such as pKa, metal binding, temperature dependence and absorbance, helps others understand the choice and decide whether it is appropriate for a related study. Such records are part of good experimental design.

A useful way to apply the criteria is to write them as a checklist before the buffer is ordered. The checklist should include the target pH, the pKa, the expected reagents, the wavelength of any measurement and the temperature of the experiment. Each buffer that passes the checklist is then tested in a pilot run, which reveals problems that no list can predict. This process takes time, but it saves far more time later in the research.

To summarize this chapter, a buffer is chosen by its pKa, solubility, impermeability to membranes, weak metal binding, stability, low temperature dependence and transparency at the relevant wavelengths. These criteria were set out by Good and colleagues and are still used today. The next chapter describes the phosphate and bicarbonate systems that operate inside living organisms.
