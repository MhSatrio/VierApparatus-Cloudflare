In the laboratory, buffers appear in almost every experiment that involves living material or biological molecules. An enzyme assay is run in a buffer that holds the reaction at the pH where the enzyme is most active. A cell culture medium contains a buffer that keeps the cells in a stable environment between feeds. A protein purification uses buffers at several stages to control binding and elution. In each case, the buffer is chosen and checked as carefully as any other reagent.

An enzyme assay illustrates the practice well. The researcher selects a buffer with a pKa near the optimal pH of the enzyme, prepares the buffer at the assay temperature and adds the substrate and the enzyme. The reaction rate is then measured over time. If the buffer lacks capacity, the reaction may release enough acid or base to shift the pH, and the measured rate will reflect the changing conditions rather than the enzyme's true behavior.

Good et al. (1966) described buffers that were developed specifically for such assays, with properties that avoided interference from the molecules being studied. Their compounds were chosen so that they did not bind the enzymes or cofactors of interest, and this made them valuable in quantitative studies of enzyme activity.

Cell culture uses buffers in a more complicated way. The medium contains a bicarbonate system that works with the carbon dioxide level of the incubator, and many laboratories add a second buffer, such as HEPES, to hold the pH steady when the cells are handled outside the incubator. The choice of buffer must not harm the cells, so the compounds used are tested for toxicity before adoption.

Po and Senozan (2001) noted that buffer calculations should be verified in practice. A buffer prepared from the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation should be measured with a calibrated pH meter at the working temperature, and the result recorded in the laboratory notebook. Small differences between the calculated and measured values are normal and can be corrected by adding acid or base.

Quality control is an ongoing task. Buffers that are stored for long periods can change through microbial growth, absorption of carbon dioxide or evaporation. Laboratories therefore label each buffer with its preparation date, store it in sealed containers and discard solutions that show signs of contamination or cloudiness. These habits ensure that the buffer used in an experiment is the buffer that was intended.

Ball (2008) reminded readers that a buffer is part of a larger system that includes the water, the sample and the apparatus. A buffer that works well in one assay may behave differently in a sample that contains high levels of protein or lipid, and the researcher must test the buffer in the presence of the actual sample. The buffer is therefore evaluated in context, not in isolation.

Good buffer practice also supports reproducibility. When other scientists want to repeat an experiment, they need the buffer name, concentration, pH, temperature and source of each component. Reporting these details is part of the standards of biological research, and it allows results to be compared across laboratories.

Students should also remember that a buffer must be compatible with the rest of the experiment. A buffer that is harmless to one assay may inhibit another, and a list of compatibility notes kept by each laboratory helps avoid repeating such problems.

To summarize this chapter, buffers are essential in enzyme assays, cell culture and protein work, and each use requires a buffer chosen, prepared and checked for its conditions. Quality control and clear records keep buffer performance reliable.
