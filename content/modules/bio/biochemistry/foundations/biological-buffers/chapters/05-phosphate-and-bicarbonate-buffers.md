Two buffer systems are the most important in living things. The phosphate system operates inside cells, where it keeps the cytoplasm near neutral pH. The bicarbonate system operates in blood and in many other body fluids, where it keeps acidity close to 7.4. Both are mixtures of a weak acid and its conjugate base, and both are chosen by nature because their pKa values fall close to the pH they must control.

The phosphate system uses dihydrogen phosphate, written H2PO4-, as the weak acid and hydrogen phosphate, written HPO4 2-, as the conjugate base. Its pKa for the second dissociation is close to 7.2, which is why it works so well near neutral pH. When acid is added, hydrogen phosphate takes up the protons; when base is added, dihydrogen phosphate releases them. The cell uses this property to buffer the many acids produced in metabolism.

Good et al. (1966) included phosphate buffers among their comparisons, and they noted that phosphate was a natural choice for many biological studies. Its pKa is suitable for experiments near neutral pH, and its chemistry is well understood. Phosphate buffers are still widely used in enzyme assays and cell culture, although some of their components can precipitate metal ions.

The bicarbonate system is based on carbonic acid, which forms when carbon dioxide dissolves in water. Carbonic acid releases hydrogen ions to form bicarbonate, and bicarbonate can take up hydrogen ions to form carbonic acid again. The system is unusual because one of its components, carbon dioxide, can be removed from the blood by breathing, which gives the body a rapid way to adjust the acid balance.

Po and Senozan (2001) pointed out that the bicarbonate system is an open system, meaning that it exchanges gases with the atmosphere. The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation applies to the bicarbonate system, but its pH depends on the partial pressure of carbon dioxide as well as on the ratio of bicarbonate to carbonic acid. This makes the system responsive to breathing and explains why respiratory problems can change blood pH quickly.

Ball (2008) noted that both systems rely on water as the medium in which the reactions take place. Carbon dioxide must dissolve in water to form carbonic acid, and phosphate ions must be surrounded by water to remain soluble. The buffering of the body is therefore inseparable from the chemistry of the solvent, and the same water that carries the buffers also gives them their behavior.

The two systems also work together. Inside red blood cells, the bicarbonate system is linked to the movement of chloride ions and to the activity of enzymes that convert carbon dioxide to bicarbonate. Phosphate contributes to buffering within the cells, while bicarbonate buffers the blood plasma. The combined effect keeps the internal and external environments of the body in balance.

Students should remember that the phosphate system has a pKa near 7.2 and the bicarbonate system has a pKa near 6.1 when measured in simple solutions. These values explain why the phosphate system is more effective inside cells and why the bicarbonate system needs the lungs to maintain its ratio. The difference in pKa is one reason that the body uses more than one buffer.

Students who meet these systems should also remember that the body is not in equilibrium with the atmosphere. Both systems are actively maintained, and the balance can be disturbed when intake, output or breathing changes. Understanding the chemistry is the first step toward understanding these disturbances.

To summarize this chapter, the phosphate system buffers the cell interior near neutral pH, and the bicarbonate system buffers blood and extracellular fluid by working with carbon dioxide and breathing. Both rely on water and on the Henderson-Hasselbalch relationship. The next chapter describes the buffering capacity of a solution and why concentration matters.
