A buffer is a solution that resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added to it. Pure water has almost no resistance: a few drops of strong acid can change its pH by several units. A buffer, by contrast, may absorb the same amount of acid with only a small change in pH. This property is what makes buffers so valuable in biology, where a shift of a few tenths of a pH unit can be enough to disturb an enzyme or a membrane.

Most buffers are mixtures of a weak acid and its conjugate base. The weak acid can release a proton when base is added, and the conjugate base can take up a proton when acid is added. Each form of the pair acts as a reserve that neutralizes the added reagent. The pH stays close to the pKa of the acid, because the ratio of the two forms changes only slightly when a small amount of reagent is introduced.

Po and Senozan (2001) explained the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation that describes this behavior. The equation shows that the pH depends on the logarithm of the ratio between the conjugate base and the acid. Because the logarithm changes slowly when the ratio is near one, the pH changes slowly too. This is the mathematical basis of buffer action.

A simple image helps. Imagine a bucket that is half full of water, with a sponge placed beside it. Pour a little water into the bucket and the level changes a great deal. Now imagine a sponge that absorbs water and releases it as needed. The sponge keeps the level steady, because it takes in extra water when there is too much and gives it back when there is too little. A buffer works in a similar way with hydrogen ions.

Good et al. (1966) emphasized that a useful buffer must do more than resist pH change. It must also dissolve well, stay chemically stable, avoid binding metal ions and remain inert toward the molecules of interest. These requirements explain why the buffers they described were chosen so carefully, and they set the standard for buffer choice in biological research.

The capacity of a buffer is limited. Each buffer contains a finite amount of acid and conjugate base, and once one form is used up, the buffer no longer resists change. A large excess of strong acid can exhaust a buffer completely, and the pH will then fall sharply. Biochemists therefore use buffers at concentrations high enough to handle the expected changes in their samples.

Ball (2008) noted that cells rely on buffers that are built into their own chemistry. Proteins, phosphates and carbonic acid all contribute to the buffering of the cell's interior. Their combined capacity keeps the pH near its set point even when the cell produces acids during metabolism, and it gives the cell time to correct the balance by other means.

It is useful to remember what a buffer does not do. It does not remove acid or base from a solution, and it does not hold the pH at one exact value. It simply limits how far the pH can move when a reagent is added or produced. This limit is the essence of buffer action, and it is the starting point for the chapters that follow.

To summarize this chapter, a buffer resists pH change because a weak acid and its conjugate base absorb added acid or base, keeping the pH near the pKa. The effect is limited by the amount of buffer present, and cells use built-in buffers to stay within a narrow range. The next chapter describes the buffer range, the pH interval over which a buffer works well.
