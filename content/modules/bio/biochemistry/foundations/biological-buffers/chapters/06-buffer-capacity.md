Buffer capacity measures how much acid or base a buffer can absorb before its pH changes significantly. It is distinct from the buffer range, which describes the pH interval over which the buffer works. Two buffers can have the same pKa and therefore the same range, but one may have far more capacity if it is present at a higher concentration. Buffer capacity tells the experimenter how robust a buffer is against the reagents and reactions it must withstand.

Buffer capacity is greatest when the pH equals the pKa. At that point the acid and its conjugate base are present in equal amounts, and both forms are available to absorb added reagent. As the pH moves away from the pKa, one form becomes scarce, and the capacity falls. The capacity therefore follows the same pattern as the buffer range, with its maximum at the center.

Concentration is the most direct way to increase capacity. A buffer at 100 millimolar can absorb twice as much acid as the same buffer at 50 millimolar before its pH shifts by the same amount. This is why biochemists choose the concentration of their buffer with care: a dilute buffer may appear to work in the bottle but fail when a reaction releases acid or base into the solution.

Po and Senozan (2001) explained that the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation predicts the pH change for a given addition, and that the change depends on the total amount of buffer present. When the amounts of acid and base are large compared with the added reagent, the pH changes only slightly. When they are small, the change is large, and the buffer has little capacity.

Good et al. (1966) reported the capacity of their buffers in addition to their pKa values. They noted that high capacity is useful in experiments where a reaction releases acid or base steadily, and that a buffer with low capacity may be overwhelmed before the experiment is complete. Their work emphasized that capacity must be checked for each application.

Ionic strength also affects capacity in practice, although the effect is secondary. High concentrations of salts can change the activities of the buffer ions and slightly shift the pKa, which changes the effective capacity. Biochemists who run experiments in high-salt conditions therefore recheck the pH of their buffers after adding salt.

Ball (2008) pointed out that the capacity of a cell's buffers is limited, and that a cell must balance its production of acids against its capacity to absorb them. When acid production exceeds capacity, the cell must remove acid by transport or breathing, and if this fails, the pH drops. Capacity is therefore a resource that the cell manages constantly.

When designing an experiment, a student should estimate how much acid or base the reaction will generate and choose a buffer concentration that leaves a comfortable margin. A common practice is to use a buffer at least ten times as concentrated as the expected change in reagent. This margin prevents the pH from drifting during the course of the experiment.

A simple laboratory test shows capacity clearly. A buffer is titrated with a strong acid, and the volume of acid needed to move the pH by one unit is recorded. A buffer with high capacity needs a larger volume, and the result can be compared between buffers at the same pH. Such tests are useful when a new buffer is proposed for a specific application.

To summarize this chapter, buffer capacity is the amount of acid or base a buffer can absorb with little pH change. It is greatest at the pKa and increases with concentration, and cells must manage their capacity as a limited resource.
