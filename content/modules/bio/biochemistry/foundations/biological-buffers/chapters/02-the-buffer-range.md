Every buffer works well over a particular range of pH, called its buffer range. Inside this range the buffer resists changes in acidity effectively, and outside it the buffer is much less useful. The buffer range is usually taken as the pKa of the buffer plus or minus one pH unit. Within that interval, the weak acid and its conjugate base are both present in amounts large enough to absorb added reagent.

The reason for this range follows from the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation. When the pH equals the pKa, the two forms are present in equal amounts. When the pH is one unit above or below the pKa, the ratio of the forms is ten to one or one to ten. At these ratios, one form still dominates enough to be useful as a reserve, but the reserve on the other side has been reduced. Beyond one pH unit the ratio becomes so lopsided that the buffer loses most of its capacity.

Po and Senozan (2001) explained that the buffer range is a consequence of the logarithmic relationship between pH and the ratio of the two forms. The logarithm makes the buffer effective over a useful span but not over an unlimited one. This is why each buffer is described with its own pKa, and why no single buffer works across the whole pH scale.

A practical consequence is that a buffer should be chosen so that its pKa lies close to the pH that must be controlled. A buffer with a pKa of 7.2 is therefore suitable for an experiment at pH 7.4, while a buffer with a pKa of 4.8 would be unsuitable, because the target pH lies far outside its range. Choosing the wrong buffer is one of the most common mistakes in laboratory work.

Good et al. (1966) reported buffers that cover different ranges, and they designed their compounds to fill the gaps left by earlier buffers. Their selection gave biologists a set of buffers that could be matched to almost any experiment in the range from about 6 to 8.5, which is the region where most biological processes take place.

Students should note that the buffer range is a rule of thumb rather than a sharp boundary. Within about a unit of the pKa, the buffer is useful, but its capacity is greatest close to the pKa itself. As the pH moves toward the edge of the range, the buffer becomes less effective, and the change in pH produced by a fixed amount of reagent grows.

Ball (2008) pointed out that cells maintain their pH in ranges that match the buffers available inside them. The phosphate system is effective near neutral pH, and it is used in the cytoplasm for this reason. The bicarbonate system works in a different range and is used in blood. Each system covers the region where it is most needed.

Buffer range also depends on conditions. Temperature, ionic strength and the presence of metal ions can shift the pKa of a buffer, and the range shifts with it. Biochemists therefore check the buffer range at the temperature of their experiment, and they confirm the pH after preparation rather than trusting the nominal value on the label.

The buffer range also helps when two buffers are compared. A buffer with a wider useful range can tolerate a larger change in conditions, but its capacity may be lower at each point. A reader should consider both properties together, since a wide range alone does not guarantee that the buffer will absorb a large addition of reagent.

To summarize this chapter, a buffer works best within about one pH unit of its pKa, which is its buffer range. Outside this range the ratio of the two forms becomes too lopsided to resist change effectively. The next chapter shows how a buffer of a chosen pH is prepared.
