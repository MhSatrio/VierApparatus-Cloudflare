Preparing a buffer is a routine task in a biochemistry laboratory, and it follows a clear sequence. The chemist chooses a buffer whose pKa lies close to the target pH, calculates the ratio of conjugate base to acid that will give that pH, weighs or measures the components, dissolves them in water and checks the final pH with a calibrated meter. The calculation is the part that needs care, and the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation is the tool that makes it possible.

The ratio is found by rearranging the equation. If the target pH is written as pH and the pKa as pKa, the ratio of conjugate base to acid is ten raised to the power of the difference between pH and pKa. For example, a buffer with a pKa of 7.2 that is needed at pH 7.4 requires a ratio of ten raised to the power of 0.2, which is about 1.6. The conjugate base must therefore be about 1.6 times as concentrated as the acid.

Po and Senozan (2001) gave the derivation of this relationship, and they showed how it leads to practical tables. Many laboratories keep such tables for common buffers, so that the ratio can be read off directly. Whether the chemist calculates or looks up the ratio, the principle is the same: the pH sets the balance between the two forms.

Once the ratio is known, the total concentration of the buffer is chosen. A common value is about fifty millimolar, which provides enough capacity for most experiments without introducing too much ionic strength. The amounts of acid and base needed are then found from the ratio and the total concentration, and the solution is made up to its final volume with water.

Good et al. (1966) prepared their buffers by mixing a weak acid with its salt, and they adjusted the pH with a strong acid or base when needed. Their procedures emphasized checking the pH after the solution had reached the temperature of use, because the pH of many buffers changes with temperature. This practice is still followed in most laboratories.

The final pH should always be measured rather than assumed. Calculated values are approximate, because the calculation ignores the effects of ionic strength and the exact activity of the ions. A calibrated pH meter gives the true value, and small corrections are made by adding acid or base until the target is reached. A buffer that is off by even a few tenths of a unit may produce misleading results.

Ball (2008) pointed out that the water used to prepare a buffer also matters. Water that contains dissolved carbon dioxide or traces of metal ions can change the pH and the behavior of the buffer, so careful laboratories use purified water and store solutions in sealed containers. The solvent is part of the recipe, and it deserves the same attention as the buffer components.

Preparation should be recorded. A good laboratory notebook gives the buffer name, the target pH, the temperature at which the pH was measured, the concentrations used and the date of preparation. Such records allow an experiment to be repeated exactly, and they help identify the cause of an unexpected result.

Some laboratories prepare buffers in bulk and dilute them when needed. In this case the stock is calculated at a higher concentration, and each dilution is checked. The same logic applies: the ratio of base to acid stays fixed, and only the total concentration changes.

To summarize this chapter, a buffer is prepared by choosing a pKa near the target pH, calculating the base-to-acid ratio from the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation, mixing the components in water and checking the final pH with a calibrated meter.
