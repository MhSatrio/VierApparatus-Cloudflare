A buffer's pKa is not a fixed number. It depends on temperature, on the ionic strength of the solution and on the presence of other substances. Because the pH of a buffer depends on its pKa, these influences change the pH of the buffer as well. A buffer that is correct at room temperature may be noticeably wrong at the temperature of a cell culture incubator or of a patient's body, and laboratories must allow for this shift.

Temperature has the largest effect for most buffers. The pKa of a weak acid changes with temperature because the equilibrium constant of the dissociation reaction changes with temperature. For many common buffers, the pKa falls as the temperature rises, so a buffer prepared to pH 7.4 at room temperature may read lower when warmed to 37 degrees Celsius. The size of the change differs from buffer to buffer, and it can be large for some.

Good et al. (1966) reported the temperature dependence of their buffers, and they emphasized that a buffer should be adjusted at the temperature at which it will be used. They recognized that a buffer with a small temperature coefficient is more reliable in experiments that cross a range of temperatures, and this property was one of their selection criteria.

Po and Senozan (2001) explained that the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation uses the pKa at the temperature of the solution. When the pKa changes, the calculated ratio of base to acid must also change to reach the same pH. Chemists who prepare buffers at one temperature and then use them at another must recalculate or measure the pH at the working temperature.

Ionic strength affects the apparent pKa. High concentrations of salts change the activities of the ions, and the measured pH can differ from the value predicted from concentrations alone. The effect is usually modest in dilute solutions but can be significant in concentrated salt solutions used in some protein preparations.

Dilution also changes the pH of some buffers. When a buffer is diluted with water, the ratio of its components is unchanged, but the activities of the ions shift slightly, and the pH may move a small amount. Most laboratories therefore check the pH of a stock solution after dilution, rather than assuming that the diluted buffer has the same pH.

Ball (2008) pointed out that the water around a buffer molecule changes with temperature, and that the hydrogen-bond network responds to heat. These structural changes influence how strongly the buffer components hold their protons, which is the physical reason for the temperature dependence. The behavior of buffers is therefore linked to the physical chemistry of the solvent.

Practical advice follows from these points. A buffer should be prepared and checked at the temperature of use, the pH should be measured after any dilution or addition of salt, and the temperature of the measurement should be recorded. These steps are simple, but they prevent many errors in the laboratory.

Temperature also affects the stability of the buffer components themselves. Some compounds break down faster when warmed, which changes both the pH and the concentration of the buffer over time. Laboratories that run long incubations therefore check their buffers at the start and at the end of the experiment, and they record any change. This precaution is part of careful experimental design, especially for enzyme studies that run for hours.

To summarize this chapter, the pKa of a buffer changes with temperature, ionic strength and dilution, so the pH must be checked under the conditions of use. Good and colleagues chose buffers with small temperature coefficients for this reason. The next chapter describes how buffers are used and checked in laboratory practice.
