## Overview

Water is the most common molecule in living things, and it is the medium in which nearly every biochemical reaction takes place. A cell is mostly water by weight, and the proteins, sugars, fats and nucleic acids inside it all spend their working lives surrounded by it. Yet water is not a passive background. It dissolves some molecules, rejects others, stabilizes large structures and takes part in chemical reactions. Understanding water is therefore one of the first steps in understanding biochemistry.

Each important term appears in quotation marks the first time it is used, with a plain explanation. The module builds directly on the Atoms and Chemical Bonds module, especially on the ideas of polarity, partial charge and hydrogen bonding. A student who is comfortable with those ideas will find this module a natural next step. A student who is not should review the earlier module first, because the explanations here rely on them.

The module has eight chapters. The first describes the structure of the water molecule and explains why it is bent and polar. The second covers hydrogen bonding between water molecules, which gives water its unusual properties. The third and fourth explain how water dissolves polar molecules and ions. The fifth describes the shells of water that surround dissolved ions. The sixth covers the hydrophobic effect, which explains why oil and water separate and how that separation shapes membranes and proteins. The seventh describes how water responds to temperature, and the eighth ties these properties to the living cell.

The module keeps returning to a small number of examples. Table salt, sugar, oil and a simple buffer appear repeatedly, so that a reader can see the same ideas applied to different substances. Each chapter ends with a summary and a pointer to the next one, so the module can be read straight through or in sections as a reference.

The references at the front of the module were chosen because they connect directly to the content. Lewis (1916) gave the electron-sharing picture on which water's polarity depends. Ball (2008) reviewed how water acts inside cells, and Good et al. (1966) described buffers that work in water-based solutions. Readers who want to go further can start with these sources and follow their citations.

The aim is that a student who finishes this module can predict, from a molecule's structure, whether water will dissolve it, whether it will form a shell of water around itself, and whether it will be pushed into a cluster with other nonpolar molecules. Those predictions underpin the study of enzymes, membranes and metabolism in the modules that follow.

Readers may find it helpful to read this module alongside a glass of water and a few household substances, such as salt, sugar and cooking oil. Each chapter describes what happens to those substances, and watching them in a real glass makes the abstract ideas concrete. The module is designed to be read slowly, with pauses to check each prediction against what the glass shows.

Some concepts in this module, such as the hydrogen bond network, are simplified for beginners. The simplified picture is accurate enough for the rest of the course, and the chapters point out where the picture becomes more complicated at higher levels.

Good practice in the laboratory also depends on water. Every buffer, reagent and sample preparation in a biochemistry lab begins with a choice of solvent, so this module includes practical notes on why water is used and when it is not suitable. A student who understands water's properties will make better decisions in the lab and will be able to explain the results of experiments with more confidence.
