Ionic solids such as table salt dissolve readily in water, even though the ions in the solid are held together by strong electrical attractions. The reason lies in the competition between the ions' attraction to one another and their attraction to water. Water is so effective at pulling ions apart that the ions in the crystal separate and spread through the solution.

When a crystal of sodium chloride is placed in water, the negative oxygen ends of water molecules gather around the positive sodium ions, and the positive hydrogen ends gather around the negative chloride ions. Each ion is surrounded by a shell of water molecules that partially shields its charge. The shielding weakens the attraction between sodium and chloride enough that the ions can drift apart.

Lewis (1916) explained how atoms gain or lose electrons to become ions, and so the charges that water surrounds are the result of that electron transfer. The ions in solution keep their charges, and it is these charges that make them attractive to water's polar ends.

The energy involved in dissolving an ion is large. Water must supply enough attraction to pull each ion out of the crystal, and the surrounding water molecules must rearrange around it. Hydrogen bonds between water molecules are broken and reformed in the process. The overall balance is favorable for many salts, which is why they dissolve so readily.

Ball (2008) described how the water shell around an ion is not fixed. Water molecules around each ion exchange with the bulk water constantly, so the shell is always changing. This dynamic exchange allows ions to move through the cell, and it is part of how cells carry electrical signals and regulate mineral balance.

Good et al. (1966) selected buffer compounds with charged groups that dissolve well in water and remain in solution across a range of acidity. The buffers work because their ionized forms are surrounded by water and can take up or release hydrogen ions without precipitating. A buffer that formed a solid when it gained or lost charge would be unsuitable for biological work.

Not all ions dissolve equally well. Very highly charged ions, or ions that are large and strongly bonded to other ions, may dissolve poorly, while small, singly charged ions usually dissolve easily. Calcium and phosphate can form insoluble salts under some conditions, and cells control their concentrations carefully to avoid forming solid deposits.

Ions in water also conduct electricity. The charged particles can move through the solution when a voltage is applied, carrying charge from one place to another. This is why salty water conducts and pure water does not, and it is the principle behind the electrical signaling in nerves and muscles.

A helpful picture is a crowd of people surrounding a group of celebrities. The celebrities are the ions, and the crowd is the water. The crowd pulls each celebrity away from the group and holds them in the middle of the room, where they can move about freely. The crowd is not fixed, and people move in and out, which keeps the ions circulating.

Salt concentrations matter in cells. When ions are present at high concentration, the water shells around them overlap, and the ions can interfere with one another. This is one reason biochemists report the ionic strength of a solution, because the same ion can behave differently at different concentrations.

To summarize this chapter, water dissolves ionic compounds by surrounding each ion with a shell of oriented water molecules, which weakens the attraction between oppositely charged ions. The shells are dynamic, and dissolved ions carry charge through solutions. The next chapter describes these shells in more detail and explains how they affect the behavior of ions.
