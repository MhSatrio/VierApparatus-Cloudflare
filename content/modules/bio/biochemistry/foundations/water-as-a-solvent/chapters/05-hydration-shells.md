When a substance dissolves, the molecules or ions that have left the solid are not floating free. Each one is surrounded by a layer of water molecules that are oriented around it and held in place by attraction. This layer is called a hydration shell, or a solvation shell in a more general sense. Hydration shells are one of the main reasons that dissolved substances behave differently from the same substances in a solid or a gas.

The structure of a hydration shell depends on the charge and size of the dissolved particle. A small, highly charged ion such as sodium attracts a tight shell of water molecules whose oxygen ends face it closely. A larger ion with less charge attracts a looser shell. Polar molecules such as sugars form shells that depend on where their hydrogen-bonding groups are located on the surface.

Lewis (1916) described the electron distributions that determine these charge patterns. The partial and full charges on the dissolved particle decide where water molecules align, and the arrangement of electrons in the particle decides how strongly the alignment is held.

Hydration shells are not static. Water molecules in the shell exchange with those outside it constantly, so the shell is a changing region rather than a fixed coat. The rate of exchange depends on how strongly the particle holds its water. Some ions hold tightly and exchange slowly, while others release water quickly.

Ball (2008) emphasized that hydration shells help control how dissolved substances move and react. A large molecule that is well hydrated may diffuse more slowly than a small one, because it drags its shell along with it. Reactions that require two molecules to meet may depend on whether their hydration shells can be partly removed.

Good et al. (1966) designed buffers with hydration in mind. A buffer compound that is strongly hydrated remains soluble and does not separate from the solution, and its hydration shell can respond to changes in acidity without causing the molecule to precipitate. The selection of buffers therefore took into account how each compound was surrounded by water.

It is important to remember that a hydration shell involves real molecular contacts. Each water molecule in the shell has a particular orientation and forms hydrogen bonds or electrostatic attractions with the dissolved particle. Those contacts can be measured, and they affect how much energy is needed to move the particle through the solution.

Hydration matters for enzymes and receptors too. A substrate that enters an enzyme's active site must lose some of its hydration shell before it can bind closely. The energy needed to strip away the shell is one of the factors that shapes how tightly the substrate binds. Biochemists therefore consider water as part of the binding process, not just as the background in which binding occurs.

A simple way to picture a hydration shell is as a group of people standing around a speaker in a noisy room. The people are the water molecules, and the speaker is the dissolved ion. The people face the speaker and move slightly as the speaker moves, but they also step away and are replaced by others, so the group changes while keeping its general shape.

Shells also form around larger molecules, such as sugars and proteins, where their polar surfaces face the water. The shape of the surface therefore influences how water is arranged, and two molecules of the same mass can have very different hydration shells if their polar groups are placed differently.

To summarize this chapter, a hydration shell is a layer of oriented water molecules around each dissolved particle, and its strength and exchange rate depend on the particle's charge and size. Shells affect how molecules diffuse, react and bind to enzymes. The next chapter describes why nonpolar molecules are excluded from water and how that exclusion drives many biological structures.
