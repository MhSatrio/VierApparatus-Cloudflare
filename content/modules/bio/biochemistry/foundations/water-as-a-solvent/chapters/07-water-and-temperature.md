Temperature is a measure of how fast molecules move. In water, faster movement breaks hydrogen bonds more often and reforms them less often. Because of the strong hydrogen-bond network, water absorbs a large amount of heat before its temperature rises much. This property, called high heat capacity, is one reason that living organisms can keep their temperatures stable despite changes in the environment.

Water also has a high boiling point for a molecule of its size. Breaking enough hydrogen bonds to turn water into a gas requires a large input of energy. The boiling point of water is far higher than that of similar small molecules without hydrogen bonding, and this difference is a direct sign of how much the molecules attract one another.

Lewis (1916) gave the electron arrangements that make hydrogen bonding possible. The bond network is a consequence of those arrangements, and its thermal effects follow from the energy needed to break the attractions. Temperature changes therefore affect water through its molecular bonds, not only through its overall motion.

Water is densest at about four degrees Celsius. As water cools below this temperature, its hydrogen bonds begin to hold molecules farther apart in an open arrangement, so the liquid expands slightly before it freezes. Ice is less dense than liquid water for this reason, and ice floats. This behavior protects aquatic life in winter, because the surface freezes while the water beneath stays liquid.

Ball (2008) described how temperature changes the way water supports cell chemistry. At higher temperatures, water molecules move more freely, which speeds up reactions but can also destabilize large molecules whose structures depend on hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic packing. Cells therefore keep their temperature within a narrow range, and many organisms have evolved to tolerate only small variations.

Good et al. (1966) noted that buffer properties can change with temperature. A buffer that is well suited for one temperature may shift its acidity at another, so biochemists specify the temperature when they describe a buffer. This is a practical example of how the physical behavior of water affects laboratory work.

The thermal properties of water also help the body. Sweating cools the skin because evaporating water absorbs a large amount of heat, a process made possible by the energy needed to break hydrogen bonds at the surface. The same property lets water store heat from the environment and release it slowly, which moderates temperature changes in lakes and oceans.

Temperature affects dissolved substances as well. In general, more molecules dissolve in warm water than in cold, although the effect varies from substance to substance. Gases, however, dissolve less well in warm water, which is why warm lakes contain less oxygen for fish. These trends matter for both cell biology and environmental science.

A helpful picture is a crowded dance floor. When the music is slow, dancers hold hands and move together in a loose pattern. When the music speeds up, they break hands more often and move faster. The floor takes longer to heat up, because the dancers must spend energy breaking their connections before they can move more freely.

Temperature also changes how strongly the hydrophobic effect acts. Warming can weaken it in some systems, which is one reason that heating a protein can unfold it. Biochemists control temperature carefully during experiments so that the balance of forces stays the same from one run to the next.

To summarize this chapter, water's hydrogen bonds give it high heat capacity and a high boiling point, and they make ice less dense than liquid water. Temperature changes the balance between bond formation and breaking, which affects cells, buffers and dissolved gases.
