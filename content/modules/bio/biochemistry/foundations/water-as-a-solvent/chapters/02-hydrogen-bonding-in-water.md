Because water molecules are polar, a partly positive hydrogen on one molecule is attracted to a partly negative oxygen on a neighbor. This attraction is a hydrogen bond. It is much weaker than a covalent bond, but in water there are enormous numbers of them, and together they organize the liquid in a way that shapes its behavior at every scale.

Each water molecule can, in principle, form up to four hydrogen bonds. Two of its hydrogens can point toward the oxygen atoms of neighbors, and its own oxygen, with its two lone pairs, can accept hydrogen bonds from two more neighbors. In liquid water these arrangements are constantly forming, breaking and reforming, so each molecule is in contact with several others at any moment.

Lewis (1916) described the electron distributions that make such attractions possible. The partial positive charge on a hydrogen and the partial negative charge on an oxygen are the direct result of the uneven electron sharing he described. Hydrogen bonds are therefore a natural consequence of the polar bonds in each water molecule.

The strength of a hydrogen bond in water is modest. Breaking one takes only a small fraction of the energy needed to break a covalent bond. This is important, because it means that the network of hydrogen bonds can change quickly. Water can flow, mix and react while still being held together by a large number of attractions. A network that was rigid and permanent would behave very differently.

Ball (2008) described the water network as dynamic rather than fixed. Although each molecule forms several hydrogen bonds at a time, the bonds are constantly shifting. The network is best described as a changing pattern, with clusters that form and dissolve over very short times. That dynamic character is one reason that water can respond rapidly to the presence of dissolved molecules.

Hydrogen bonding explains several of water's most noticeable properties. Water boils at a much higher temperature than similar small molecules would, because a large amount of energy is needed to pull molecules apart from their hydrogen-bonded neighbors. Water also has a high capacity for storing heat, because energy is absorbed in breaking bonds before the temperature rises much. Both properties help living things keep stable conditions.

Good et al. (1966) selected buffers partly for how they interact with the hydrogen-bonded water around them. A buffer molecule that could form hydrogen bonds with water would stay in solution more easily, and it would be less likely to disturb the network. Buffers designed for biology therefore took advantage of the same hydrogen-bonding behavior that makes water such a good solvent.

A common picture is a crowd of people holding hands in a loose, shifting pattern. Each person is connected to several others, but the connections are not permanent, and people can move in and out of the crowd. The water network behaves in a similar way, with many short-lived connections forming a flexible whole.

Students should note that the hydrogen-bond network is strongest when molecules are cold and weakest when they are hot. As water warms, its molecules move more vigorously, breaking hydrogen bonds more often. This temperature dependence is important for the stability of biological molecules, and the chapter on water and temperature explores it in more detail.

To summarize this chapter, each water molecule can form up to four hydrogen bonds with its neighbors, and these bonds are weaker than covalent bonds but very numerous. The network is dynamic, which explains water's high boiling point and its capacity to store heat. The next chapter describes how water uses its polarity to dissolve polar molecules and ions.
