A typical cell is made mostly of water, and almost every process inside it takes place in that water. Water is the solvent that carries nutrients, ions and signals. It is also a reactant in many enzyme-catalyzed reactions, a structural influence on large molecules and a participant in the regulation of acidity. Understanding these roles is the final step in seeing water as more than a background for biochemistry.

As a solvent, water keeps sugars, salts, amino acids and nucleotides dispersed so that they can meet and react. The concentration of each dissolved substance is carefully controlled, and the cell uses membranes and transport proteins to move molecules across boundaries that water alone cannot cross. Water's polarity makes this control possible, because polar molecules remain in the watery compartments while nonpolar molecules are restricted to membranes.

Water is also a reactant. Hydrolysis reactions split larger molecules by adding a water molecule across a bond, releasing smaller units. Starch, proteins and fats are broken down in this way during digestion and within the cell. Lewis (1916) described the electron rearrangements that allow water to supply its hydrogen and hydroxyl parts to a reacting molecule.

Ball (2008) reviewed the view that water in cells is an active participant rather than a passive medium. Water molecules bound to proteins can influence their shape and their activity, and water can be present in specific positions within an enzyme's active site. The cell's chemistry is therefore shaped by water at a very local level as well as in the bulk solution.

Water also supports the structures of large molecules. The folding of proteins depends on the hydrophobic effect, and the double helix of DNA depends on hydrogen bonding between bases in a watery environment. Remove the water and these structures would lose their shape. The cell's architecture is in this sense built around water's properties.

Acidity inside the cell is held near a narrow value, and buffers help maintain it. Good et al. (1966) described buffer compounds designed for work with living systems, and cells use similar molecules to resist sudden changes in hydrogen ion concentration. Because water is the medium in which these buffers act, their effectiveness depends on water's properties.

The cell also controls its water content. Ions and molecules inside the cell create osmotic pressure, which draws water in or pushes it out across membranes. Cells must balance these pressures to avoid swelling or shrinking. Organisms that live in salty or dry environments have evolved special strategies to manage water loss and keep their internal concentrations stable.

Students should remember that water inside cells is not identical to pure water. It contains dissolved ions, proteins, sugars and other substances, and it is often crowded. The crowding changes how molecules move and react, and biochemists study these conditions carefully because they affect the results of experiments.

A helpful picture is a busy city, where water is the road network. Nutrients travel along the roads, buildings are supported by the ground, and traffic lights are controlled by the layout of the streets. Remove the roads and the city would stop functioning, just as a cell would stop working without water.

Water's role can also be seen in the cell's energy supply. Many of the reactions that release energy from food require water as a reactant or a product, and the rate of these reactions depends on how easily water can reach the active site of each enzyme.

To summarize this chapter, water in the cell acts as a solvent, a reactant, a support for large structures and a partner in acid-base control. Its polarity, hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic effect shape the chemistry of the cell in many ways.
