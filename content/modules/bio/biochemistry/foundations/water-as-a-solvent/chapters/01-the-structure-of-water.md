A water molecule is made of one oxygen atom and two hydrogen atoms, written H2O. Each hydrogen is joined to the oxygen by a covalent bond in which the shared electrons spend more time near the oxygen. The oxygen therefore carries a small negative charge, and each hydrogen carries a small positive charge. These partial charges are the source of almost everything unusual about water.

The molecule is not straight. The two hydrogen atoms sit at an angle of roughly one hundred and five degrees from one another, so the molecule has a bent or V shape. The oxygen atom also carries two pairs of electrons that do not take part in bonding, and these lone pairs add to the negative region near the oxygen. The bent shape keeps the partial charges from cancelling out, which is why water is strongly polar.

Lewis (1916) described how shared electron pairs create bonds and how the distribution of electrons within a molecule can be uneven. His picture explains why the oxygen-hydrogen bonds are polar even though they are still covalent. In water, the sharing is unequal enough to produce a clear difference in charge between one end of the bonds and the other.

A useful way to picture the molecule is as a small magnet with a negative end and a positive end. The oxygen points its negative side toward any nearby positive charge, and the hydrogens point their positive sides toward any nearby negative charge. This simple arrangement lets water join with many different molecules and ions, which is the first clue to why water is such a good solvent.

Students sometimes confuse polarity with charge. A water molecule is electrically neutral overall, because its positive and negative parts balance. Its polarity means that the charge is distributed unevenly across the molecule, creating two regions with opposite partial charges. The difference matters because it determines how the molecule attracts other molecules.

Ball (2008) pointed out that the structure of water is central to its role in cells. The small size of the molecule, the bent shape and the strong pull of the oxygen atom combine to give water properties that no other common liquid shares in the same combination. Those properties affect everything from the boiling point of water to the way it moves through the membranes of living cells.

Good et al. (1966) worked on buffers for use in water-based systems, and their designs depended on the same polarity. Buffer molecules had to dissolve in water without interfering with the biological processes under study. A buffer that did not interact well with water would separate from the solution or change its properties, so the structure of water was part of the buffer design.

The structure also explains why water is a liquid at the temperatures found in living things. The attractions between water molecules are strong enough to hold them together, but not so strong that they lock into a solid at ordinary temperatures. This balance is one of the reasons that water is so useful in biology, and the next chapter describes the attractions in more detail.

Water's small size also matters. The molecule is light and compact, so many water molecules can fit around a single dissolved particle. This allows water to surround ions and molecules closely, and the close packing is one reason water is so effective at pulling dissolved substances apart.

To summarize this chapter, a water molecule is bent, with an oxygen end that is partly negative and hydrogen ends that are partly positive. This polarity comes from unequal electron sharing in the covalent bonds and from the bent shape of the molecule. The next chapter describes how these partial charges join water molecules together through hydrogen bonds.
