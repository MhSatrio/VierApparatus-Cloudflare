Oil does not dissolve in water. Drops of oil floating on a glass of water are not repelled by the water so much as pushed together by it. Nonpolar molecules cannot form hydrogen bonds with water, so water molecules pack around them in a way that wastes hydrogen bonds. The system becomes more stable when the nonpolar molecules join together and expose less surface to the water. This tendency is called the hydrophobic effect.

The term is slightly misleading, because nonpolar molecules are not really afraid of water. The clustering is driven by water, which prefers to keep its own hydrogen bonds intact. When nonpolar molecules gather, the amount of water that must arrange itself around them shrinks, and the water network becomes more ordered in its remaining regions. The result is a net gain in stability.

Lewis (1916) described electron sharing in molecules that have no polar bonds, such as hydrocarbons. These molecules have even electron distributions and no partial charges, so they cannot form the hydrogen bonds that would attract them to water. Their indifference to water is a direct consequence of their structure.

The hydrophobic effect is one of the strongest forces in biology. It makes fats form droplets, causes soap molecules to form micelles, and drives the lipid bilayer of cell membranes to assemble. Nonpolar parts of molecules are gathered into the interior of a structure, away from water, and the polar parts remain on the outside.

Ball (2008) described the hydrophobic effect as a powerful organizing principle in cells. Much of the shape of proteins and membranes results from nonpolar groups being pushed out of the water and packed together. Without this effect, the molecular architecture of the cell would be very different.

Good et al. (1966) avoided nonpolar buffers for this reason. A buffer that contained large nonpolar regions would tend to cluster and separate from the solution, leaving the acidity control uneven. The buffers chosen for biology were polar enough to stay in solution, so that their behavior would be predictable across the whole sample.

Students sometimes imagine the hydrophobic effect as a force pulling nonpolar molecules together. It is more accurate to think of it as water pushing them together, because water's own hydrogen-bond network prefers to exclude them. The distinction matters because it explains why temperature and salt can change the strength of the effect.

Experiments show the effect clearly. Shake a mixture of oil and water and small droplets form, but they quickly merge back into a single layer once shaking stops. The droplets are held together by the water around them, and the larger the nonpolar surface exposed, the more strongly the water pushes the oil to join.

A helpful picture is a group of people pushed together by a crowd that wants to keep its own space clear. The crowd does not dislike the people individually, but it prefers to have fewer people taking up space around it. The people end up standing together in a tight group, and their combined mass is what keeps them there.

The hydrophobic effect also explains why many proteins fold so reliably. A chain of amino acids with nonpolar side chains buried inside and polar side chains on the surface tends to settle into a stable shape. Later modules on protein folding return to this principle in more detail.

To summarize this chapter, the hydrophobic effect drives nonpolar molecules to cluster in water, because water prefers to keep its hydrogen bonds intact. This effect shapes fats, soaps, membranes and the folded structures of proteins. The next chapter describes how temperature changes the behavior of water and of the molecules dissolved in it.
