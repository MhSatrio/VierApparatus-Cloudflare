A solvent is a liquid that can dissolve other substances. Water is called the universal solvent because it dissolves more kinds of substance than any other common liquid, although it does not dissolve everything. Its power as a solvent comes from its polarity. Polar molecules and charged particles can be surrounded and held in solution by water molecules, while nonpolar substances are left out.

When a polar molecule such as sugar enters water, the water molecules gather around it. The negative oxygen ends of water face the positive parts of the sugar, and the positive hydrogen ends face the negative parts. Hydrogen bonds form between the sugar's oxygen and nitrogen atoms and the water's hydrogens, and the sugar molecule is pulled gradually away from its neighbors and into solution.

Lewis (1916) explained the electron sharing that makes these attractions possible. Because the electrons in the sugar's oxygen-hydrogen bonds are also unequally shared, the sugar has its own partial charges. Water and sugar can therefore interact at many points on the molecule's surface, and each interaction adds a little energy that helps the sugar dissolve.

Sugar dissolves easily in water, and the process is reversible. If the water evaporates, the sugar crystallizes again, forming a regular solid in which the sugar molecules attract one another more than they attract water. This reversibility shows that dissolving is a balance of attractions rather than a permanent change in the molecules.

Many biological molecules are polar. Amino acids with charged side chains, sugars with many hydroxyl groups and nucleotides with phosphate groups all dissolve well in water for this reason. Their polar parts form hydrogen bonds with water, and their surfaces are kept in contact with the solvent. Cells rely on these properties to keep their molecules dispersed and available for reactions.

Ball (2008) pointed out that water's role as a polar solvent is not passive. Water surrounds each dissolved molecule, orients around it and can change the molecule's shape or availability. Some reactions in cells require water to be present as a reactant, and the solvent can also stabilize the charged intermediates that form during enzyme catalysis.

Buffers depend on dissolving well in water. Good et al. (1966) designed buffer compounds that are polar enough to dissolve fully at the concentrations used in biology, so that they can take up or release hydrogen ions evenly throughout the solution. A buffer that did not dissolve well would form lumps or films and fail to control acidity.

It is useful to remember that dissolving is not a matter of mixing alone. A substance dissolves when its attractions to water are strong enough to compensate for the attractions it had with its own kind. Polar molecules usually meet this condition in water, and nonpolar molecules usually do not, which is why the next chapters look at ions and at the hydrophobic effect.

A simple experiment shows this principle. Stir a spoonful of sugar into a glass of water and it disappears, while the same amount of oil forms a separate layer on top. The sugar is polar and joins the water network, while the oil is nonpolar and is pushed out of it. The difference is visible in a single glass.

Water also dissolves many small sugars, alcohols and acids, because each of them carries groups that can form hydrogen bonds. A molecule with several such groups is usually very soluble, while a molecule with only a single small polar group may dissolve only partly. Solubility therefore depends on the number and arrangement of polar groups, not simply on whether a molecule contains oxygen.

To summarize this chapter, water dissolves polar molecules by forming hydrogen bonds with their polar regions and surrounding them with water molecules. The process is reversible and depends on a balance of attractions, and it allows sugars, amino acids and buffers to work in cells. The next chapter describes how water dissolves ions.
