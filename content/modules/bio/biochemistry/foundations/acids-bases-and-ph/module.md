## Overview

Acidity is one of the most important conditions in biochemistry. Enzymes work best within a narrow range of acidity, proteins keep their shapes only when their charged groups are in the right state, and the blood must stay close to a fixed value for a person to remain healthy. The concept that describes acidity, pH, is therefore used in almost every biochemistry laboratory and every clinical test. This module explains what pH means, where it comes from and how living systems control it.

Each important term appears in quotation marks the first time it is used, with a plain explanation. The module builds on the earlier modules on atoms, bonds and water. In particular, it relies on the idea that water molecules can give and take hydrogen ions, which is introduced in the first chapters below.

The module has eight chapters. The first defines acids and bases in terms of hydrogen ions. The second explains the pH scale and why it is logarithmic. The third shows that water itself ionizes slightly, and it introduces the ion product of water. The fourth describes weak acids and the pKa value that measures their strength. The fifth presents the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation, which links pH and pKa to the charge state of a molecule. The sixth covers titration curves, which show how acids and bases react as they are added to a solution. The seventh describes acid-base balance in the human body, and the eighth explains how pH is measured in the laboratory.

Several examples recur throughout. Vinegar, which contains acetic acid, and ammonia solution, which is basic, are used to illustrate strength and pH. Carbonic acid and bicarbonate, which control blood pH, appear in the later chapters because they show how a living system uses weak acids and their salts. Phosphate, a buffer that is important inside cells, is also introduced. Returning to these examples helps the reader see the same principles applied in different settings.

The references were chosen for their direct relevance. Po and Senozan (2001) explained the history and limits of the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation, which is used throughout this module. Good et al. (1966) described the buffers that became standard in biological research, and Ball (2008) reviewed how water and its ions act inside cells. Students who want to go further can read these sources and follow their citations.

By the end of this module a reader should be able to estimate the pH of a simple solution, predict whether a weak acid will be mostly charged or uncharged at a given pH, and explain why a buffer resists changes in acidity. These skills are used in the modules on enzymes, membranes and metabolism, where many reactions depend on the charge state of the molecules involved.

Readers who work through the module in order will notice that the same few ideas return again and again: hydrogen ions, the charge state of a group and the balance between two forms of a molecule. Recognizing these patterns makes the calculations easier, because most problems reduce to asking which form dominates at a given pH.

A word of caution is useful at the start. The pH scale is logarithmic, so a change of one unit means a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration. Many students find this surprising at first, and the chapters explain it with examples. Working through the calculations slowly, and checking each result against a sensible range, is the best way to build confidence with this material.

Finally, acidity is always a property of a solution at a stated temperature. Water's ionization changes with temperature, and biochemists therefore report the temperature when they report a pH. Keeping this in mind prevents confusion when comparing measurements made in different laboratories.
