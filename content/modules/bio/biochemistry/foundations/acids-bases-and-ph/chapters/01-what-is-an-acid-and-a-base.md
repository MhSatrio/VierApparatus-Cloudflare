An acid is a substance that releases hydrogen ions, written H+, when it is dissolved in water. A base is a substance that accepts hydrogen ions or releases hydroxide ions, written OH-, into the solution. These two definitions cover most of the acid and base chemistry that a biochemist meets. A hydrogen ion is simply a proton, the positively charged nucleus of a hydrogen atom, with its electron removed, so acid chemistry is really the chemistry of protons moving between molecules.

Everyday acids are easy to recognize. Vinegar tastes sour because it contains acetic acid. Lemon juice contains citric acid. The stomach contains hydrochloric acid, which is strong enough to dissolve metal in the laboratory but is held at a controlled level in the body. Everyday bases include ammonia, which is used in household cleaners, and baking soda, which is sodium bicarbonate. Bases often feel slippery on the skin and taste bitter.

The modern definition of an acid as a hydrogen ion donor and a base as a hydrogen ion acceptor was developed in the early twentieth century, and it remains useful. Lewis (1916) had already described acids and bases in terms of how they handle shared electron pairs, which is a broader idea. Both views help explain why water can act as either an acid or a base, depending on what it is mixed with.

In water, a hydrogen ion does not travel alone. It attaches to a water molecule to form a hydronium ion, H3O+, although biochemists often write it simply as H+ for convenience. This is an important detail, because the hydronium ion is the form that chemists measure when they calculate acidity. The shorthand H+ is used throughout this module, and the reader should understand that it stands for a hydrogen ion in water.

A strong acid releases almost all of its hydrogen ions when dissolved in water. Hydrochloric acid is an example: nearly every molecule gives up its hydrogen ion. A weak acid releases only some of its hydrogen ions, and a large fraction of its molecules remain intact. Acetic acid is weak, and most of its molecules stay as they are in a typical vinegar solution. The difference between strong and weak acids is about how completely they release protons, not about how sour or dangerous they are.

Bases work in the opposite way. A strong base such as sodium hydroxide releases hydroxide ions, which can take up hydrogen ions from the solution and remove them. A weak base, such as ammonia, takes up only some hydrogen ions when placed in water, because it binds them less completely. Again, strength describes how completely a substance reacts with water, not how harsh it feels.

Ball (2008) pointed out that water is central to acid and base chemistry in cells. Water molecules themselves can donate and accept hydrogen ions, so the solvent is never a neutral spectator. The acidity of a cell's interior is therefore the result of many acids and bases reacting with water at once.

Good et al. (1966) selected buffer compounds in part because they could take up or release hydrogen ions without becoming harmful. Their careful choice is a reminder that acids and bases are not simply good or bad for biology. Each one must be understood in its context, including its strength and the conditions under which it works.

To summarize this chapter, an acid releases hydrogen ions in water and a base accepts them or releases hydroxide ions. Strong and weak describe how completely a substance reacts with water. The next chapter explains how the acidity of a solution is expressed on the pH scale.
