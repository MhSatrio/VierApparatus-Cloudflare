Measuring pH is one of the most common tasks in a biochemistry laboratory. Enzyme assays, cell cultures, buffer preparation and clinical samples all depend on knowing the acidity of a solution. The most widely used instrument is the pH meter, which uses an electrode to detect the hydrogen ion activity of a sample and converts that signal into a pH reading. Other methods, such as indicators and test strips, are simpler but less precise.

A glass pH electrode works by generating a small voltage that depends on the hydrogen ion concentration on either side of a thin glass membrane. The voltage is compared with a reference electrode, and the meter converts the difference into pH. Before use, the electrode must be calibrated with standard solutions of known pH, usually at two or three values that bracket the range of interest.

Calibration is the most important step. An electrode that has not been calibrated, or that has been left to dry, can give readings that are off by a substantial amount. Laboratory practice therefore calls for calibration at the start of each session and checking against a third standard when measurements span a wide range. The temperature of the standards and the sample should also be recorded, because pH depends on temperature.

Indicators are dyes that change color over a narrow pH range. Each indicator has its own transition range, and it is chosen to match the pH that needs to be monitored. Indicators are cheap and convenient, but they give only an approximate value, and their color can be affected by the presence of colored or cloudy samples. They are useful for rough checks and for titrations where the color change marks the endpoint.

Good et al. (1966) reported pH values for their buffers using careful electrode measurements, and they described the precautions needed to achieve accuracy. Their work set a standard for reporting buffer pH that is still followed, including the temperature at which the measurement was made and the composition of the buffer solution.

Po and Senozan (2001) noted that pH measurements can be used together with the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation to check results. If the composition of a buffer is known, its pH can be calculated and compared with the measured value. A large difference suggests a preparation error, a problem with the electrode, or a sample that behaves differently from the ideal case.

The reading of a pH meter is an activity rather than the simple concentration of hydrogen ions. In concentrated solutions and in mixed solvents, the two can differ noticeably, and that difference affects the interpretation of results. Careful biochemists specify how they measured pH and in what conditions, so that others can reproduce their work.

Ball (2008) reminded readers that pH measured in a laboratory is a snapshot of a living system that is always changing. A cell's pH can vary between organelles and over short periods, and bulk measurements may hide local differences. Special probes that report pH inside single cells have been developed to address this, and they show how measurements must be matched to the question being asked.

Good laboratory practice also includes storing electrodes correctly, using fresh standard solutions and recording every measurement with its temperature and calibration date. These habits make it possible to compare results across days and across laboratories, and they are essential for any experiment where acidity matters.

To summarize this chapter, pH is measured with an electrode that must be calibrated with standards and used at a recorded temperature, while indicators give faster but less precise estimates. Measurements can be checked against calculated buffer values, and the results must be reported with their conditions.
