A titration is a controlled experiment in which a solution of known composition is gradually mixed with a reagent of known strength, and the pH is measured after each addition. Plotting pH against the volume of reagent produces a titration curve. The shape of the curve reveals the acids and bases present, their pKa values and the amount of reagent needed to change the solution from one state to another.

A titration curve for a weak acid starts at a low pH, because the acid is present in solution. As base is added, the pH rises slowly at first and then more quickly. Near the midpoint of the curve, the pH changes only slowly with further addition, and this flat region is the buffer region. The flat region is centered on the pKa of the acid, where the protonated and deprotonated forms are present in equal amounts.

The steep rise in pH that follows the buffer region marks the equivalence point, the stage at which the added base has converted essentially all of the weak acid into its conjugate base. The equivalence point is not usually at pH 7 for a weak acid, because the conjugate base itself reacts with water and makes the solution slightly basic.

Po and Senozan (2001) explained how the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation accounts for the buffer region. Its shape follows from the logarithmic relationship between pH and the ratio of charged to uncharged forms. The equation predicts a sigmoid curve, and the actual titration curve matches this prediction over the range where the approximation is good.

The buffer region is the most useful part of the curve for biology. In that region, adding a small amount of acid or base changes the pH very little, because the solution can absorb the added hydrogen ions or hydroxide ions by shifting the ratio of its two forms. This property is what makes buffers valuable in cells, where stable pH is essential.

Good et al. (1966) reported the titration behavior of their buffer compounds, showing that each buffer had a useful range centered near its pKa. A buffer is most effective within about one pH unit of its pKa, and a titration curve makes this range easy to see. Biochemists therefore use titration curves to choose the right buffer for a given experiment.

Titration curves are also used to measure the amount of acid or base in a sample. The volume of reagent needed to reach the equivalence point gives the total amount of acid present, regardless of its strength. This method is simple and accurate, and it remains one of the most common ways of analyzing acidity in food, water and clinical samples.

Ball (2008) noted that the buffering of cellular fluids can be understood through similar curves, although the real systems contain many acids and bases at once. Their combined titration curve is smoother than that of a single acid, and it covers a wider pH range. The principle, however, is the same: the flat regions of the curve mark where the cell is most protected against sudden change.

Titration curves can also reveal several acidic groups in one molecule. Each group contributes its own buffer region, so a curve with two flat sections indicates two groups with different pKa values. Reading these features is a useful exercise for anyone studying amino acids later in the course.

To summarize this chapter, a titration curve plots pH against added reagent and shows a buffer region centered on pKa and an equivalence point where the acid is fully converted. The buffer region is the most useful part for biology, and the curve also measures how much acid or base a sample contains.
