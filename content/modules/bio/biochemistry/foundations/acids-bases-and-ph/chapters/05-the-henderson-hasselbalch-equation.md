The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation is the most useful tool in this module. It relates the pH of a solution, the pKa of a weak acid and the ratio between its deprotonated and protonated forms. In its common form it reads pH = pKa + log([A-]/[HA]), where [A-] is the concentration of the conjugate base and [HA] is the concentration of the weak acid. With this equation, a student can predict the charge state of a group at any pH.

The equation is a rearrangement of the equilibrium definition of pKa. If the pH equals the pKa, the logarithm term is zero, which means the two forms are present in equal amounts. If the pH is one unit above the pKa, the ratio of A- to HA is ten to one. If the pH is one unit below the pKa, the ratio is one to ten. These three cases cover most of the practical calculations that a student will meet.

A worked example helps. Suppose a weak acid has a pKa of 6.0, and the solution is at pH 7.0. The difference is one unit, so the ratio [A-]/[HA] is 10. Therefore about ninety percent of the molecules are in the deprotonated, charged form. If the same acid were placed at pH 5.0, the ratio would be one tenth, and about ninety percent would be in the uncharged form.

Po and Senozan (2001) traced the history of the equation and discussed its limits. They noted that it is an approximation that works well when the solution is not too dilute, not too concentrated and not strongly acidic or basic. For most biological work the approximation is good enough, but students should know that more exact methods exist for extreme cases.

The equation can also be used to calculate pH from the composition of a buffer. A mixture of a weak acid and its conjugate base has a pH that depends on the ratio of the two forms. When the ratio changes, the pH changes in a predictable way. This is the basis of buffer preparation in the laboratory, where a chemist mixes known amounts of acid and base to reach a target pH.

Good et al. (1966) prepared their buffers using this principle, and their paper showed how the ratio of protonated and deprotonated forms set the final pH. Preparing a buffer is therefore an exercise in applying the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation, and the calculation is one that biochemists perform many times each week.

The equation has a practical use for drugs and nutrients too. A drug that is a weak acid is more likely to be uncharged in the acidic stomach, where it can cross membranes, and more likely to be charged in the neutral intestine. Knowing the pKa lets a pharmacologist predict where a drug will be absorbed. The same reasoning applies to natural compounds in food and the body.

Ball (2008) reminded readers that the pH inside cells and in the water around them is controlled by many weak acids acting together. The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation applies to each of them, and the overall pH is the result of their combined effects. This is why the equation is so central to understanding the chemistry of living systems.

Students should also check their answers. A result near the pKa means roughly equal amounts of both forms, and a result far from the pKa should show a strong preference for one form. An answer that contradicts this rule signals a calculation error.

To summarize this chapter, the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation, pH = pKa + log([A-]/[HA]), links pH, pKa and the charge state of a weak acid. It predicts that a pH one unit above the pKa gives a ten-to-one ratio of charged to uncharged forms. The next chapter uses this relationship to explain titration curves.
