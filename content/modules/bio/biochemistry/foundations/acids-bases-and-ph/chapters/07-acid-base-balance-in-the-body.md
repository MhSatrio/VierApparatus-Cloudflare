The human body produces acids continuously. Cells break down food and release carbon dioxide, which dissolves in blood to form carbonic acid, and they also generate other acids as products of metabolism. Despite this constant input, the pH of arterial blood is held very close to 7.4. Even a shift of a few tenths of a unit can cause serious illness, so the body has several layers of control that work together.

The first layer is chemical buffering. Blood contains a carbonic acid and bicarbonate system, in which carbonic acid releases hydrogen ions and bicarbonate takes them up. Proteins, hemoglobin in particular, also buffer blood. Together these systems absorb sudden changes in acidity within seconds, before other controls have time to respond.

The bicarbonate system is especially important because it is linked to breathing. Carbon dioxide from respiration is converted into carbonic acid, and the lungs can remove carbon dioxide quickly. When breathing becomes faster, more carbon dioxide leaves the blood, and the pH rises. When breathing slows, carbon dioxide builds up, and the pH falls. The lungs therefore act as a rapid adjustable control.

The kidneys provide the slowest but most powerful control. They can excrete excess acid in the urine and retain bicarbonate, adjusting the balance of the blood over hours to days. The kidneys can also produce new bicarbonate when needed. This slow adjustment corrects longer-term imbalances that the faster systems cannot handle alone.

Po and Senozan (2001) described the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation as the tool used to understand the bicarbonate system. The equation links blood pH to the ratio of bicarbonate to dissolved carbon dioxide, which is why clinicians measure both when they assess acid-base status. A doctor who understands the equation can interpret a blood gas result and decide which control is failing.

Good et al. (1966) provided the buffer chemistry that informed later work on biological systems. Their careful choice of buffers with stable pKa values showed that a buffer can be designed to work in a narrow range, and the human bicarbonate system is an example of a buffer that works in that way. Its pKa, combined with the body's ability to adjust the ratio of its components, keeps the pH steady.

Ball (2008) emphasized that acid-base balance is inseparable from water balance. The kidneys and lungs both lose water as they work, and the concentrations of ions in body fluids depend on how much water is present. Changes in water content can therefore shift acidity indirectly, and clinicians consider both when they treat patients.

Disturbances of acid-base balance have names. Acidosis describes a condition in which blood pH falls below its normal range, and alkalosis describes a rise above it. These conditions can arise from breathing problems, from kidney disease, from uncontrolled diabetes that produces acidic ketones, and from many other causes. Each one reflects a failure of one or more of the body's controls.

It is worth remembering that the body is not a single buffer but a network of buffers, lungs and kidneys. Each part responds to changes in the others, and the overall state of acid-base balance is the outcome of their cooperation. Students who understand this network will find it easier to interpret clinical data and the chemistry behind it.

Clinicians often describe these changes using the pH of arterial blood, which is sampled directly from an artery. The sample must be analyzed quickly, because gases escape from blood and change its measured values.

To summarize this chapter, blood pH is held near 7.4 by chemical buffers, the lungs and the kidneys, which act on different timescales. The bicarbonate system links acidity to carbon dioxide and water balance. The final chapter of this module describes how pH is measured in the laboratory.
