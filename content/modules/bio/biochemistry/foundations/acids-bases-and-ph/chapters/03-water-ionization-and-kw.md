Pure water is not completely pure in a chemical sense. A very small fraction of its molecules split into a hydrogen ion and a hydroxide ion. The reaction is written H2O reversibly forming H+ and OH-, and it is the source of the hydrogen ions that define acidity in water. Although the amount of ionization is tiny, it is the foundation for every pH calculation in aqueous solutions.

At 25 degrees Celsius, one molecule in roughly every hundred million splits in this way. The concentration of hydrogen ions in pure water is therefore about 1 x 10^-7 moles per litre, and the same is true of hydroxide ions. Because these two concentrations are equal, pure water is neutral, with a pH of 7 at this temperature.

The product of the two concentrations is a constant called the ion product of water, written Kw. At 25 degrees Celsius, Kw is about 1 x 10^-14. Multiplying the hydrogen ion concentration by the hydroxide ion concentration always gives this number, whether the solution is acidic, basic or neutral. If one concentration rises, the other must fall in proportion to keep the product constant.

This relationship explains why adding acid to water lowers the pH and at the same time lowers the hydroxide ion concentration. The added hydrogen ions combine with hydroxide ions to form water, and the balance shifts. The same principle works in reverse for bases. Students should learn to use Kw as a check: if they calculate a hydrogen ion concentration, they can find the hydroxide concentration immediately.

Lewis (1916) gave the general picture of how bonds form and break by sharing or transferring electrons, and water ionization is an example of that process. The hydrogen atom in one water molecule moves to another molecule, leaving behind a hydroxide ion. The electrons that were shared in the O-H bond rearrange, and the result is a pair of charged particles.

Po and Senozan (2001) discussed how this water equilibrium underlies many of the equations used for acids and bases. The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation, introduced in a later chapter, assumes that the solvent's ionization is known and that the solution is not too strongly acidic or basic. Understanding Kw therefore helps explain the limits of the simpler equations that students will use.

Temperature matters for Kw. Water ionizes a little more at higher temperatures, so Kw increases and the pH of pure water drops below 7 at body temperature. Pure water at 37 degrees Celsius is still neutral, but its neutral pH is slightly lower than 7. Biochemists who measure pH in warm samples therefore keep the temperature in mind when they interpret results.

Ball (2008) described how water's own ionization contributes to the chemistry of cells, because water is the solvent in which all biochemical acids and bases operate. The equilibrium of water is one of the fixed points against which the behavior of other acids and bases can be measured. Even a cell that carefully controls its acidity cannot escape the basic chemistry of water.

The ionization of water is also affected by dissolved substances, not only by temperature. Salts can change the effective concentrations of the ions, and very high concentrations of acid or base shift the balance so that the simple calculations no longer match measurements. For everyday biology, however, the ideal value of Kw is accurate enough, and the simple relationship between hydrogen and hydroxide ions can be relied upon.

To summarize this chapter, water ionizes into hydrogen and hydroxide ions, and their product, Kw, is about 1 x 10^-14 at 25 degrees Celsius. Pure water is neutral because its two ion concentrations are equal, and temperature changes Kw slightly.
