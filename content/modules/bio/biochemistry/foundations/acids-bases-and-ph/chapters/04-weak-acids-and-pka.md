A weak acid is an acid that releases only part of its hydrogen ions when dissolved in water. Its molecules exist in two forms at once: the protonated form, which still holds its hydrogen ion, and the deprotonated form, which has released it. The two forms are in equilibrium, and the position of that equilibrium depends on how strongly the acid holds its proton and on the pH of the solution.

The equilibrium is written as HA reversibly forming H+ and A-, where HA is the weak acid and A- is its conjugate base. Most of the molecules in a weak acid solution remain as HA, while a smaller fraction releases H+ and becomes A-. The conjugate base is the partner that remains after the acid has lost its proton, and it can take up a proton again when the solution becomes more acidic.

The strength of a weak acid is measured by a constant called Ka, which describes how far the equilibrium lies toward the released ions. Because Ka values often span many powers of ten, chemists usually use pKa instead. The pKa is the negative logarithm of Ka, so a small pKa means a strong acid and a large pKa means a weak one.

A key result is that pKa is the pH at which exactly half of the acid molecules are in the protonated form and half are in the deprotonated form. This point is a convenient reference. Below the pKa, the protonated form dominates, and above the pKa, the deprotonated form dominates. A weak acid with a pKa of 4.8 is therefore mostly protonated at pH 3 and mostly deprotonated at pH 7.

Acetic acid, the acid of vinegar, has a pKa near 4.8. In a vinegar solution at pH 3, most of its molecules are uncharged HA, which is why vinegar tastes sour and can penetrate tissues. At neutral pH, most acetate molecules carry a negative charge, and their behavior in cells is different. This shift in charge with pH is one of the most important ideas in this module.

Po and Senozan (2001) explained how the pKa concept is linked to the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation used in biochemistry. Their account showed that the equation is a direct consequence of the equilibrium definition, and it helps students see why pKa is so useful. Knowing the pKa of a group allows its charge state to be predicted at any pH.

Good et al. (1966) selected buffer compounds according to their pKa values. A buffer works best when its pKa is close to the pH it is meant to control, because then the protonated and deprotonated forms are both present in useful amounts. This choice is a practical application of the pKa concept, and it appears in every biochemistry protocol that specifies a buffer.

Ball (2008) noted that the charge state of a weak acid group inside a protein or a membrane depends on the local pH and on the local environment, which can shift the apparent pKa. Biological molecules therefore may not have exactly the pKa values measured in simple water solutions. Nevertheless, the pKa of a free weak acid is the starting point for understanding these changes.

A useful habit is to compare the pH with the pKa before doing any calculation. If the pH is far below the pKa, the acid is almost entirely protonated, and if it is far above, the acid is almost entirely charged. Only values near the pKa need careful arithmetic.

To summarize this chapter, a weak acid exists partly as HA and partly as its conjugate base A-, and pKa is the pH at which the two forms are equal. Below the pKa the protonated form dominates, and above it the charged form dominates. The next chapter introduces the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation, which turns this idea into a calculation.
