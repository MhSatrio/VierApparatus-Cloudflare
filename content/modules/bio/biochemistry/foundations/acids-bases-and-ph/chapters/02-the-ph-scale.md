The concentration of hydrogen ions in a solution can vary across an enormous range. A strong acid may contain one mole of hydrogen ions per litre, while pure water contains about one ten-millionth of a mole. Writing these numbers directly is awkward, so chemists use pH, a scale that compresses the range into a few convenient numbers. The pH of a solution is defined as the negative base-ten logarithm of the hydrogen ion concentration, written pH = -log[H+].

The logarithm is the key idea. Each whole-number step on the pH scale represents a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration. A solution at pH 5 has ten times as many hydrogen ions as a solution at pH 6, and a hundred times as many as a solution at pH 7. Students who first meet the scale often expect pH values to change in small steps, but the changes are large in terms of the ions involved.

Most solutions have a pH between 0 and 14. Values below 7 are acidic, values above 7 are basic, and a value of 7 is neutral at the standard temperature used in most textbooks. Lemon juice has a pH near 2, vinegar near 3, pure water near 7, blood near 7.4, and household ammonia near 11. These examples show that a difference of only a few units can correspond to large differences in chemistry.

A calculation example helps. Suppose a solution contains 0.001 moles of hydrogen ions per litre, which is written 1 x 10^-3 M. The logarithm of 10^-3 is -3, so the negative logarithm is 3, and the pH is 3. If the concentration were 1 x 10^-7 M, the pH would be 7. The reader should practise these conversions until the link between concentration and pH feels natural.

Po and Senozan (2001) described how the pH idea and its related equations developed historically, and they noted that the logarithmic scale was introduced to make acidity easier to express. Their account is a useful reminder that pH is a convenience for working with a wide range of values, not a separate physical quantity. The ions are what matter, and pH simply summarizes them.

Because the scale is logarithmic, averaging pH values can be misleading. The average of pH 2 and pH 12 is not pH 7, because the hydrogen ion concentrations at those two values differ so greatly. When biochemists combine solutions or report averages, they convert back to concentrations first and then calculate the pH of the result.

Good et al. (1966) described buffers whose performance was reported on the pH scale, and they emphasized that a small change in pH can shift the balance of charged and uncharged forms of a molecule. The logarithmic scale therefore hides large chemical differences behind small numerical changes, which is why the decimal places of a pH value matter in careful work.

Ball (2008) noted that the pH of the cell's interior is held near a set point despite constant production of acids and bases. The narrow control is why even a change of a few tenths of a unit can alter enzyme activity and protein shape. The pH scale is therefore not just a laboratory tool but a way of describing one of the most tightly regulated conditions in living things.

To summarize this chapter, pH is the negative logarithm of the hydrogen ion concentration, so each unit represents a tenfold change. Neutral water is near pH 7 at the standard temperature, and biological fluids sit within a narrow band of values.
