A sulfhydryl group, also called a thiol group, consists of one sulfur atom bonded to one hydrogen atom, written -SH. Sulfur sits below oxygen in the same column of the periodic table, so it has similar bonding patterns but larger atoms and a weaker pull on electrons. The sulfhydryl group is found in the amino acid cysteine, in the coenzyme glutathione and in many enzymes that handle reactive chemistry.

The S-H bond is only weakly polar, so a sulfhydryl group is less hydrophilic than a hydroxyl group. It forms weaker hydrogen bonds with water and contributes less to solubility. Its pKa is near eight to nine, which means that it is mostly uncharged at neutral pH, although it can lose its proton in more basic conditions and become a negatively charged thiolate.

Lewis (1916) described the sharing of electrons between atoms of similar electronegativity, and the S-H bond is a case where sharing is more even than in the O-H bond. This balance explains why sulfhydryl groups behave differently from hydroxyl groups, even though the two look similar when drawn on paper.

Two sulfhydryl groups can join together through an oxidation reaction, forming a disulfide bond, written S-S. This link is covalent and fairly strong, and it is one of the few covalent cross-links found in folded proteins. The disulfide bond locks a part of the protein in place, helping it keep its shape in the crowded and oxidizing environment outside cells.

Disulfide bonds are formed and broken by specific enzymes, and their presence can be controlled by the redox state of the cell. Inside cells the environment is usually reducing, so most disulfides are broken, while outside cells they often remain intact. This difference is a useful reminder that functional groups respond to the conditions in which they are placed.

Good et al. (1966) selected buffers that did not react with sulfhydryl groups, because such reactions could alter the composition of the solution. Some compounds that contain sulfhydryl groups were also tested as possible buffers, but their tendency to oxidize limited their use in many experiments.

Lipinski et al. (1997) observed that sulfur-containing groups can increase a molecule's lipophilicity, which affects its ability to cross membranes. A sulfhydryl group is less polar than an alcohol, so a compound that contains it may pass more easily into fatty environments. Drug designers take this into account when they add or remove sulfur-containing groups.

For students, the key lesson is that the sulfhydryl group combines two roles: it is reactive enough to form disulfide bonds and weak enough to remain uncharged at neutral pH. Cysteine, the amino acid that carries this group, is therefore unusual among the building blocks of proteins, and its behavior is studied closely in protein chemistry.

Sulfhydryl groups also defend cells against oxidative damage. Glutathione contains a sulfhydryl group that can donate a hydrogen atom to neutralize reactive oxygen species, and it is then recycled back to its active form. Cells keep a high ratio of reduced sulfhydryl groups to oxidized disulfides, and a shift in this balance is a sign of stress. The same functional group can therefore be both a structural link and a protective agent.

Students should therefore think of thiol chemistry as a balance between building and protecting, with the cell adjusting the balance as its needs change.

To summarize this chapter, a sulfhydryl group is an S-H group with a pKa near eight to nine, and it forms covalent disulfide bonds when two groups are oxidized together. These bonds help stabilize folded proteins outside cells and are controlled by the redox state of the cell. The next chapter shows how functional groups are used in drug design.
