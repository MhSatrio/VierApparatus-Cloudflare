A functional group is a small, specific arrangement of atoms within a larger molecule that behaves in a predictable way wherever it appears. An oxygen bonded to a hydrogen, a carbon double-bonded to an oxygen, and a nitrogen bonded to two hydrogens are all functional groups. Each one has its own characteristic polarity, charge behavior and reactivity, and these properties often matter more to the behavior of a molecule than the length or shape of its carbon skeleton.

The idea is easiest to see by comparing two molecules that share a carbon backbone. Ethane is a gas with almost no attraction to water, because it has only carbon and hydrogen. Ethanol has the same two carbons but carries an oxygen bonded to a hydrogen, a hydroxyl group. Ethanol mixes with water readily, while ethane does not. The change in one small group changes the whole behavior of the molecule.

Lewis (1916) described the electron sharing that makes some bonds polar and others nearly neutral. Because a functional group contains bonds to oxygen, nitrogen or other electronegative atoms, it is usually the polar part of a molecule. The carbon framework tends to be nonpolar, so the functional groups are where the charges and hydrogen bonds sit.

A useful rule for beginners is that functional groups do their chemistry at the same place in every molecule. A hydroxyl group in a sugar reacts in much the same way as a hydroxyl group in a fatty alcohol, even though the rest of the molecules are very different. This consistency allows chemists to predict reactions without memorizing each molecule separately.

Lipinski et al. (1997) showed that counting polar groups is a practical way to estimate how a molecule will behave in water and across membranes. Their work on solubility and permeability relied on the number of hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, which are mainly functional groups. The same logic applies throughout biochemistry: the groups tell you most of what you need to know.

Good et al. (1966) selected buffer molecules that contain functional groups able to gain or lose hydrogen ions. Their chemistry depends on the groups, not on the backbone, and the same is true of many natural compounds. A reader who learns to recognize groups will understand why a buffer works and why a particular molecule dissolves or binds where it does.

Functional groups can be present more than once in a molecule. Glucose contains several hydroxyl groups, which is why it dissolves so easily in water. Amino acids contain both an amino group and a carboxyl group, which is why they can behave as acids and bases at the same time. Counting the groups and noting their positions is therefore the first step in describing any biological molecule.

It helps to learn the groups in pairs of related forms, because many groups are closely linked. An alcohol, a ketone and an aldehyde all contain a carbon bonded to an oxygen, but their arrangements differ, and so do their properties. The following chapters take the groups in turn and describe the differences in detail.

Chemists also use the word functional in a precise sense. A group is functional when it determines the reactions a molecule can undergo, not simply because it is present. The same carbon skeleton with a different group attached may be inert in one case and highly reactive in another, and this is why the group, rather than the skeleton, is named first when a compound is described.

To summarize this chapter, a functional group is a specific arrangement of atoms that behaves in a predictable way wherever it appears, and it usually controls a molecule's polarity, charge and reactivity. Ethanol and ethane show how one group can transform a molecule's behavior. The next chapter describes hydroxyl groups, which are among the most common in biology.
