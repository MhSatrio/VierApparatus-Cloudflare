A hydroxyl group consists of one oxygen atom bonded to one hydrogen atom, written -OH. It is attached to a carbon atom in the molecule, and it is one of the most common functional groups in biology. Sugars, alcohols, many amino acids and most of the steroids that act as hormones carry hydroxyl groups. The group is small, but its polarity makes it a strong influence on how a molecule behaves in water.

The oxygen in a hydroxyl group pulls electrons toward itself, so the O-H bond is polar. The oxygen carries a partial negative charge, and the hydrogen carries a partial positive charge. The hydrogen can form a hydrogen bond with a water molecule, and the oxygen can accept a hydrogen bond from water. As a result, each hydroxyl group can bind several water molecules, which pulls the molecule toward the aqueous phase.

Lewis (1916) explained the electron sharing that makes the O-H bond polar. Because the sharing is unequal, the hydroxyl group is a site where charge is separated, and this separation is what makes it so useful in biological chemistry. A molecule containing several hydroxyl groups can form many hydrogen bonds, which is why sugars dissolve so easily.

Glucose is the classic example. Its six carbons are arranged in a ring, and most of them carry a hydroxyl group. Each hydroxyl can form hydrogen bonds with water, so glucose is highly soluble and is carried in blood without difficulty. If glucose had no hydroxyl groups, it would be a nonpolar hydrocarbon and would not dissolve in the blood plasma.

Lipinski et al. (1997) listed hydroxyl groups among the features that affect solubility and permeability in drug development. A molecule with many hydroxyl groups is generally more soluble in water but less able to pass through a fatty membrane. This trade-off appears throughout biology and is one reason cells use different molecules for transport and for storage.

Hydroxyl groups also react. In an alcohol, the hydroxyl group can be replaced by other groups or can be oxidized to produce an aldehyde or a ketone. The product of each reaction has different properties, so the hydroxyl group is a point at which the chemistry of a molecule can change. Students will see this idea again in the chapter on carbonyl groups.

The hydroxyl group is also weakly acidic. In most alcohols and sugars the proton is held very tightly, so the group is a much weaker acid than a carboxyl group. Its pKa is high, which means that it is almost always uncharged at neutral pH. Good et al. (1966) would have recognized this contrast when they designed buffers, because a buffer needs groups whose pKa lies near the target pH, and hydroxyl groups are usually too weak to serve that role.

Ring structures in sugars change how hydroxyl groups are arranged. In glucose, one hydroxyl group becomes part of the ring itself, and the others stick out from it. The position of each hydroxyl affects how the molecule fits into an enzyme, and this is why enzymes that handle sugars are so specific.

Hydroxyl groups also change solubility in a graded way. A single hydroxyl on a long hydrocarbon chain barely changes its solubility, while several hydroxyls on a small molecule can make it freely soluble. The number and spacing of these groups decide where a molecule sits between water and fat.

To summarize this chapter, a hydroxyl group is an oxygen bonded to hydrogen, and its polar O-H bond allows it to form hydrogen bonds with water. Glucose shows how several hydroxyl groups make a sugar soluble, and hydroxyl groups are only weakly acidic. The next chapter describes carbonyl groups, which are often formed from hydroxyl groups by oxidation.
