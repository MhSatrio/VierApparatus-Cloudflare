A drug is a molecule that must do several things at once. It must dissolve in the blood, cross membranes to reach its target, bind tightly to that target and then be removed from the body without causing harm. Each of these requirements depends heavily on the functional groups the molecule carries. Medicinal chemists therefore choose and place functional groups with great care, and the groups discussed in this module are the tools they use most often.

Lipinski et al. (1997) described a set of simple guidelines that relate the numbers of hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, together with molecular size and lipophilicity, to how well a compound is absorbed. Their work showed that many compounds that are soluble and orally available have a moderate number of polar groups. The guidelines are not strict rules, but they give chemists a practical way to judge a candidate molecule early in development.

Hydroxyl and amino groups are common in drugs because they form hydrogen bonds with the target protein and with water. A drug with a well-placed hydroxyl group can bind more tightly to an enzyme and dissolve better in blood. However, too many polar groups can prevent the drug from crossing the membrane of a cell, so the balance must be chosen carefully.

Charged groups change the picture. A carboxyl group that is charged at blood pH helps a drug dissolve but may limit its ability to enter cells, which is why many carboxylic acid drugs are delivered as esters that are converted back to the acid inside the body. Similarly, a protonated amino group can bind strongly to a negatively charged target while reducing membrane permeability.

Lewis (1916) gave the electron-sharing picture that allows chemists to predict which groups will be polar, charged or neutral. A medicinal chemist uses this picture when deciding where to place an oxygen or nitrogen, and the predictions guide the design of the molecule before it is ever synthesized.

Phosphate groups are rarely used directly in drugs because their high charge prevents them from entering cells. Drug designers who want a phosphate-like effect often choose a group that mimics the shape of the phosphate while carrying less charge, or they deliver a phosphate prodrug that is converted inside the cell. This strategy shows how the chemistry of a group can be adapted to the needs of a medicine.

Good et al. (1966) would have recognized the same principle in buffers. The groups that control acidity must be chosen to match the pH of the environment, and drugs must be designed so that their charged groups are present in the right proportions at the site of action. The pKa of each group is therefore part of the design, not an afterthought.

Sulfur-containing groups and other less common functional groups are used when a drug needs a particular reactivity or lipophilicity. Designers must consider how the group will be metabolized, because some groups are oxidized or cleaved rapidly in the liver, which shortens the drug's action. The chemistry of each group therefore has consequences for how long the drug works.

For students, the lesson of this final chapter is that functional groups are not just descriptions of molecules but design choices that determine how a medicine behaves in the body. The next module in the curriculum, on carbohydrates, returns to the functional groups of sugars and shows how their hydroxyl and carbonyl groups create the diversity of carbohydrate structures.

To summarize this chapter, drug designers choose functional groups to balance solubility, membrane crossing, binding and metabolism. Polar groups help a drug dissolve and bind, while charged groups can limit its entry into cells, and prodrugs are one way to manage that limit. The chemistry of each group therefore shapes how a medicine acts in the body.
