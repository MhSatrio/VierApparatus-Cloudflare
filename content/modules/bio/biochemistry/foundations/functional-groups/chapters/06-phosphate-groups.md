A phosphate group consists of one phosphorus atom bonded to four oxygen atoms, arranged around the phosphorus in a tetrahedral shape. In biological molecules it is usually attached to another molecule through a single oxygen, and it may carry one, two or three negative charges depending on pH. Phosphate groups are found in nucleotides, in the sugar-phosphate backbone of DNA and RNA, in many metabolic intermediates and in the energy currency of the cell, adenosine triphosphate.

The phosphate group is strongly acidic. Its first proton has a pKa near two, and its second has a pKa near seven. At the pH of the cell, the phosphate group is therefore usually carrying at least one negative charge, and often two. This charge makes phosphate-containing molecules very soluble in water and gives them a strong attraction to positively charged proteins and metal ions.

Lewis (1916) described the sharing of electrons in bonds to oxygen, and the phosphate group is a clear example of how electronegative oxygens pull charge toward themselves. The negative charge of the phosphate is spread across several oxygen atoms, which stabilizes the group and makes it a weak acid.

Phosphate is also a key part of the buffer systems of cells. Good et al. (1966) included phosphate-based buffers among the systems they studied, because phosphate has useful pKa values near the pH of many biological fluids. A phosphate buffer can absorb hydrogen ions and release them as needed, which helps keep the cell's acidity stable.

Adenosine triphosphate, or ATP, is the best-known phosphate-containing molecule. It carries three phosphate groups in a chain, and the bonds between them are high in energy. When one of these bonds is broken by water, the products are more stable, and the energy released can drive other reactions. The energy stored in ATP therefore depends on the chemistry of its phosphate groups.

The high charge of phosphate groups also affects how molecules interact. A protein that binds ATP often has positively charged amino acids in its binding site, and these attract the negatively charged phosphates. The fit between charge and shape allows the enzyme to recognize its partner, and the chapters on enzymes describe this recognition in more detail.

Lipinski et al. (1997) noted that highly charged groups can limit the ability of a molecule to cross membranes. Phosphate-containing compounds therefore often need special transport systems to enter cells. Many drugs that mimic nucleotides are designed with this difficulty in mind, and they may be delivered in a form that releases the phosphate group after entering the cell.

Students should remember that phosphate chemistry is an example of how a single functional group can perform several roles. It carries charge for solubility and binding, it stores and releases energy, and it helps stabilize the pH of fluids. Recognizing these roles makes it easier to understand the nucleotide and energy modules that follow.

Phosphate groups also serve as attachment points inside cells. Many enzymes add phosphate groups to proteins and sugars, and other enzymes remove them, so the phosphorylated state of a molecule acts as a switch. The added charge can change the shape of a protein or create a binding site for another molecule. The ability of a phosphate group to switch activity on and off makes it one of the most widely used signals in the cell. Later modules on enzymes and signaling return to this idea in more detail.

To summarize this chapter, a phosphate group is strongly acidic, usually carrying one or two negative charges at cell pH. It is central to nucleotides, DNA backbones and ATP, where its high-energy bonds drive cellular reactions. The next chapter describes sulfhydryl groups, which form important links within proteins.
