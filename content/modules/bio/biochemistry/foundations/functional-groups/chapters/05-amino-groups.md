An amino group is a nitrogen atom bonded to hydrogen atoms and to the carbon framework of a molecule. In its simplest form, it is written -NH2. Amino groups are the basic partner of the carboxyl group, and together they define the amino acids that build proteins. Many neurotransmitters and drugs also carry amino groups, so this group is important far beyond protein chemistry.

The nitrogen in an amino group has a lone pair of electrons that is not used in bonding. This lone pair can accept a proton, which is why the amino group acts as a base. When it accepts a proton it becomes positively charged, written -NH3+. The pKa of the conjugate acid is usually between about nine and eleven for simple amines, which means that the amino group is mostly uncharged at neutral pH but can be protonated in more acidic conditions.

Lewis (1916) described the electron pairs that nitrogen can share and hold. The lone pair on the amino nitrogen is one of the key features of its chemistry, and it explains why amines can accept protons and form hydrogen bonds with water. The same lone pair also allows the amino group to take part in new bonds in metabolism.

Simple amines such as methylamine are basic and dissolve well in water. Their solutions are alkaline because the amino group takes protons from water and releases hydroxide ions. This behavior is the basis for the everyday use of ammonia as a cleaning solution, and it is the same chemistry that makes amino groups useful as basic centers in biological molecules.

Lipinski et al. (1997) noted that nitrogen-containing groups affect how molecules behave in drug design. A protonated amino group carries a positive charge, which helps a drug bind to negatively charged targets but can reduce its ability to cross membranes. The balance between charged and uncharged forms is therefore a central concern in pharmacology.

In an amino acid, the amino group sits on the carbon next to the carboxyl group. This arrangement allows the amino acid to form a zwitterion, with the carboxyl group negative and the amino group positive at neutral pH. The net charge is zero, but the molecule has both a positive and a negative region, and this affects how amino acids fold into proteins.

Good et al. (1966) included amine-based buffers among the compounds they examined, because amino groups can accept protons over a useful pH range. The buffer capacity of such compounds depends on the pKa of the amino group, which in turn depends on the groups nearby. Changes in the surrounding structure can shift the pKa by a useful amount.

Amino groups are also the sites where biological molecules are joined together. When an amino group reacts with a carboxyl group, water is removed and a peptide bond forms. This reaction links amino acids into chains, and it is the chemical basis of every protein in the body. The next modules on amino acids and peptide bonds develop this idea in detail.

Amino groups can also be modified inside cells. Adding a methyl group or an acetyl group changes the charge and size of the amino group, and cells use such changes to regulate proteins and other molecules. These modifications show that a functional group can be a platform for further chemistry, not only a fixed feature of a molecule.

To summarize this chapter, an amino group is a nitrogen with a lone pair that accepts protons, so it acts as a weak base with a pKa near nine to eleven for simple amines. In amino acids it pairs with a carboxyl group to form a zwitterion, and its reaction with a carboxyl group forms peptide bonds. The next chapter describes phosphate groups, which carry energy and charge in nucleotides.
