A carbonyl group is a carbon atom joined to an oxygen atom by a double bond, written C=O. It is one of the most reactive functional groups in biochemistry. The double bond is strongly polar, with the oxygen pulling electrons away from the carbon, so the carbon carries a partial positive charge and is open to attack by molecules that are rich in electrons. This pattern of reactivity appears in sugars, fats, amino acids and many metabolic intermediates.

Carbonyl groups appear in two main forms. In an aldehyde, the carbon of the carbonyl is at the end of a carbon chain and is bonded to a hydrogen as well as to the carbon chain. In a ketone, the carbonyl carbon sits in the middle of the chain and is bonded to two other carbon atoms. Acetone is a common ketone, while formaldehyde and acetaldehyde are simple aldehydes. Both forms share the polar C=O bond, but their positions differ, and that difference affects their reactions.

Lewis (1916) described the double bond as two shared pairs of electrons, which makes the carbonyl carbon electron-poor and the oxygen electron-rich. That description explains why the carbonyl group is so reactive. The polarity of the double bond is not distributed evenly, and this unevenness drives the reactions that carbonyl groups undergo.

Glucose can exist in a form that contains an aldehyde, and fructose contains a ketone. These sugars are classified by the carbonyl group they carry, which is why one is called an aldose and the other a ketose. In solution, a sugar can switch between its open-chain form and its ring form, and the carbonyl group takes part in that switch. The change affects how the sugar is recognized by enzymes.

Lipinski et al. (1997) listed carbonyl oxygens among the groups that can accept hydrogen bonds from water and other molecules. A carbonyl oxygen is therefore moderately polar, and it helps a molecule dissolve, although less strongly than a hydroxyl group. This balance makes carbonyl groups important in molecules that must both dissolve and cross membranes.

Carbonyl groups are often converted into other groups in cells. An aldehyde can be oxidized to a carboxyl group, and a ketone can be reduced to an alcohol. These conversions are part of the energy-releasing and energy-storing pathways of metabolism. Each step changes the polarity and charge of the molecule, so the carbonyl group acts as a switch in a chain of reactions.

Carbonyl groups are not acidic in the way that carboxyl groups are, but the hydrogens next to them can be weakly acidic. A proton on the carbon beside a carbonyl can be removed under suitable conditions, which allows carbonyl compounds to take part in chain-building reactions. Good et al. (1966) did not design buffers around these groups, but the same weak acidity is important in metabolic reactions.

When students learn carbonyl groups, they should remember that the position of the group in the carbon chain determines much of its reactivity. An aldehyde at the end of a chain is more accessible to reagents than a ketone in the middle. This difference is one reason that aldehydes are oxidized more easily in metabolism.

The carbonyl group is also important in cooking. Sugars that carry aldehyde groups can react with amino groups when heated, forming brown compounds that give baked bread its color and flavor. This reaction is an everyday example of how functional groups combine.

To summarize this chapter, a carbonyl group is a carbon double-bonded to oxygen, and its polar bond makes the carbon reactive. Aldehydes have the group at the end of a chain and ketones in the middle, and sugars are classified by this difference. The next chapter describes carboxyl groups, which are weak acids that carry charge at neutral pH.
