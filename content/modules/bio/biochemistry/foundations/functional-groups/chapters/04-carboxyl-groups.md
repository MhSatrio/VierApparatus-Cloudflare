A carboxyl group combines a carbonyl group with a hydroxyl group on the same carbon, written -COOH. It is the functional group that makes a molecule an organic acid. Fatty acids, amino acids, citric acid from the citric acid cycle and many other metabolites carry carboxyl groups. Because the hydrogen of the -OH part can be released as a proton, the carboxyl group is a weak acid, and its behavior is one of the most important topics in biological chemistry.

When a carboxyl group loses its proton, it becomes a carboxylate group, written -COO-. The negative charge is shared between the two oxygen atoms, which makes the carboxylate stable. This sharing of charge is why the carboxyl group is more acidic than a simple alcohol. The pKa of a typical carboxyl group lies between about three and five, which means that at neutral pH almost all carboxyl groups in a cell are in their charged form.

Lewis (1916) described the electron arrangements that allow charge to spread over several atoms in a single group. The idea of shared electrons across the two oxygens of a carboxylate is a direct extension of his picture, and it explains why the group loses its proton so readily.

Acetic acid, the acid of vinegar, is the simplest carboxylic acid. At the low pH of vinegar, most of its molecules are uncharged, and they taste sour. At the neutral pH of cell fluid, most of them are charged as acetate, and they behave as anions. This shift in charge is a clear example of the Henderson-Hasselbalch principle from the earlier module on acids and pH.

Lipinski et al. (1997) noted that charged groups such as carboxylates strongly influence solubility and membrane permeability. A charged molecule dissolves well in water but crosses lipid membranes poorly, which is why many cells use transporters to move carboxylate compounds across their membranes. The charge of the group, not only its polarity, is the decisive factor.

Fatty acids carry a carboxyl group at one end of a long hydrocarbon chain. The chain is nonpolar, and the carboxyl group is the polar head. This combination makes fatty acids amphipathic, able to sit at the boundary between water and fat. Long-chain fatty acids are therefore the building blocks of membranes and the storage form of energy in fat.

Amino acids also contain a carboxyl group. Together with the amino group, it allows each amino acid to act as both an acid and a base, forming a zwitterion whose net charge depends on pH. Good et al. (1966) studied groups of this kind when they developed buffers, because a group that is charged over a particular pH range can resist changes in acidity.

Carboxyl groups in metabolism are often joined to other molecules through ester or amide bonds. When two carboxyl-containing molecules are linked by losing water, the resulting bond stores energy that the cell can use later. This simple reaction connects carboxyl chemistry to energy storage, which is a recurring theme in biochemistry.

Carboxyl groups also contribute to the sour taste of many foods. The taste comes from the proton released by the acid, which stimulates receptors on the tongue. Like the pH of a solution, the sourness depends on how many protons are released, not on the total amount of acid present.

To summarize this chapter, a carboxyl group is a weak acid with a pKa of about three to five, so it is almost always charged in cells. Its negative charge shapes solubility and membrane transport, and it is found in fatty acids and amino acids. The next chapter describes amino groups, which act as weak bases and are the other half of an amino acid.
