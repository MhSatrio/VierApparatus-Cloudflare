## Overview

Biological molecules are enormously varied, yet a small number of chemical patterns appear again and again. A sugar, an alcohol and a steroid may look very different on paper, but each can contain an oxygen atom bonded to a hydrogen, an arrangement called a hydroxyl group. Recognizing these recurring patterns is one of the most useful skills in biochemistry, because a functional group tells a reader how a molecule will dissolve, what charge it will carry, and which reactions it can take part in.

Each important term appears in quotation marks the first time it is used, with a plain explanation. The module builds on the earlier modules on bonds, water and acids. A reader who understands polarity and pKa will find the chapters on charged groups especially clear, but no advanced chemistry is assumed.

The module has eight chapters. The first defines a functional group and explains why it matters more than the rest of the molecule. The second describes hydroxyl groups, which are common in sugars and alcohols. The third covers carbonyl groups, found in aldehydes and ketones. The fourth describes carboxyl groups, which are weak acids that carry charge at neutral pH. The fifth covers amino groups, which are weak bases and the characteristic part of amino acids. The sixth describes phosphate groups, which carry energy and charge in nucleotides. The seventh covers sulfhydryl groups, which form important links in proteins. The eighth shows how functional groups are used when chemists design drugs.

The examples in the module return to a few familiar molecules, including glucose, acetic acid, glycine and adenosine triphosphate. Following the same molecules across several chapters shows how one molecule can carry several functional groups at once, and how their combined effects determine its behavior. Each chapter ends with a summary and a link to the next one, so the module can be read in order or used as a reference.

The references were chosen to support the material. Lewis (1916) introduced the electron-sharing picture that explains why functional groups behave as they do. Lipinski et al. (1997) described how polar groups affect solubility and permeability, which is directly relevant to drug design in the final chapter. Good et al. (1966) showed how groups that gain and lose hydrogen ions are used to build buffers, a practical application of the groups discussed here.

By the end of the module, a reader should be able to look at an unfamiliar biological molecule, name its functional groups, and make a reasonable prediction about its solubility, its charge at a given pH and the kinds of reactions it might undergo. These predictions are needed for every later module on carbohydrates, lipids, amino acids, nucleotides and enzymes.

Functional groups are also a language. Biochemists describe a molecule by its groups in the same way that a reader describes a sentence by its words. Learning the vocabulary early makes the later modules easier to follow, and it helps the reader recognize a group quickly even when it is written in an unusual way.

Students can build confidence by naming the functional groups of a familiar product before reading each chapter. Aspirin, the sugar in a fruit drink and the protein in a meal each contain several of these groups, and identifying them turns an ordinary label into a chemistry lesson.

A practical note is useful too. Some groups change their state with pH, and the same molecule may carry a different charge in the stomach, in blood and inside a cell. The chapters point out these changes wherever they matter, so that the reader learns to connect the chemistry of a group with the conditions in which it works.
