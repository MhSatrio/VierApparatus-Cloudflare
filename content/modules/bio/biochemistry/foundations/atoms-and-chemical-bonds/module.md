## Overview

Every living thing is built from atoms, and the way those atoms join together decides what a molecule can do. Biochemistry begins here. Before a student can understand how an enzyme speeds up a reaction, how a fat stores energy, or how a protein folds, it helps to know what holds atoms together and why some molecules dissolve in water while others do not. This module introduces the ideas that every later biochemistry module depends on.

Each important term appears in quotation marks the first time it is used, with a plain explanation. Chemistry words can seem unfamiliar at first, so the module moves slowly and repeats key ideas in different forms. No prior chemistry course is assumed, although a student who has seen the periodic table will find the material easier.

A useful way to approach this module is to keep a short list of molecules in mind, such as water, glucose, a fatty acid and a simple amino acid. As each chapter introduces a new bond type or property, return to the list and ask how that idea changes what you know about each molecule. This habit turns abstract rules into working predictions and makes the later modules easier to follow.

The module also uses the same examples on purpose. Water appears in almost every chapter because it is the medium in which biology happens, and it shows polarity, hydrogen bonding and ionic attraction in a single molecule. Simple ions such as sodium and chloride show how charge controls behavior, while carbon compounds show how covalent bonds build large structures. Each return to an example adds a layer of understanding.

The module is written for readers who are new to the subject, so every chemical formula is explained in words before it is used. A student who finishes with a clear picture of atoms, electrons, bonds and polarity will have the vocabulary needed for the enzyme, metabolism and macromolecule modules that follow. Working through the examples slowly, and pausing to sketch each molecule, is the most reliable way to make these ideas stick.

The module is organized in eight chapters. The first chapter describes what an atom is. The second names the three particles inside it and explains their charges. The third shows how electrons are arranged in shells and why the outer shell matters most. The fourth covers covalent bonds, in which atoms share electrons. The fifth covers ionic bonds, in which electrons move from one atom to another. The sixth describes hydrogen bonds and other weak attractions that are common in living systems. The seventh explains polar and nonpolar molecules and how they respond to water. The eighth ties these ideas to the molecules of a cell.

Readers can use the module as a reference when later modules mention bond strength, charge or solubility. Each chapter ends with a short summary that points to the next chapter, so the module can be read straight through or in pieces. The references at the front of the module are the starting points for anyone who wants to read further, and they include the original source for the idea of shared electron pairs, a review of the role of water in cells, and a study of the buffers used in biological laboratories.

The aim is that by the end of the module a student can look at a simple molecule and predict roughly where its charges sit, how strongly its atoms are held together, and whether it will mix with water. Those three predictions are the foundation for everything that follows in biochemistry. Each prediction can be checked against how the molecule behaves in the laboratory, which keeps the module tied to real experiments. A reader who can make all three predictions for water, glucose and sodium chloride has learned the core of the module.
