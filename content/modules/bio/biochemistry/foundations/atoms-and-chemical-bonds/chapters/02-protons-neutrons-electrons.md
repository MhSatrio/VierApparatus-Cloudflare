Every atom is made of three kinds of particle. Protons carry a positive electrical charge, neutrons carry no charge, and electrons carry a negative charge. Protons and neutrons sit together in the nucleus at the center of the atom, while electrons move in the much larger space around it. Because the charges of protons and electrons are equal in size and opposite in sign, a neutral atom has the same number of each.

The number of protons is the most important property of an atom. Carbon atoms have six protons, oxygen atoms have eight and hydrogen atoms have one. This number is called the atomic number, and it is what makes an element what it is. If an atom gained or lost a proton, it would become a different element, which is why nuclear changes are so different from ordinary chemical reactions.

Neutrons add mass but do not change the charge. Most carbon atoms have six neutrons, giving a total mass number of twelve, while some carbon atoms have seven or eight neutrons. These heavier forms are isotopes, and they behave almost the same way in chemical reactions because the number of electrons, which controls most reactions, is unchanged.

Electrons are very light compared with protons and neutrons, but they control almost all of chemistry. Because they are negatively charged, they are attracted to the positive protons in the nucleus. They also repel one another. The balance between attraction and repulsion sets how electrons are arranged, and that arrangement decides how an atom bonds with others.

Lewis (1916) built his idea of chemical bonding on the role of electrons. He suggested that atoms with incomplete outer sets of electrons tend to share or transfer them to reach a more stable arrangement. This view explained many patterns in the chemistry of the elements and remains the starting point for teaching bonds to students of biology.

A useful way to picture an atom is to imagine the nucleus as a tiny ball and the electrons as a faint cloud around it. The cloud does not have sharp edges, but its density is highest close to the nucleus and falls off with distance. The shape of this cloud changes with the number of electrons and with the energy level that each electron occupies.

Charge matters in biology because many of the forces that hold molecules together are electrical. Positive and negative charges attract, and like charges repel. When a molecule has charged regions, those regions can attract or repel charged regions on other molecules, which affects how molecules meet, bind and react. This is why an understanding of charge is so central to the rest of this module.

Some molecules carry charges that are not fixed to a single atom. In water, for example, the electrons are shared unevenly between the oxygen and hydrogen atoms, creating small partial charges across the molecule. Ball (2008) explained that these partial charges allow water to surround and interact with many other molecules in a cell, which is a central reason that water is so useful in biology.

Buffers rely on charged particles as well. Good et al. (1966) described compounds that can take up or release hydrogen ions, which are simply protons that have lost their electron. Because the number of hydrogen ions in a solution depends on how the electrons are arranged in the molecules that release them, the charge structure of a molecule directly controls how it behaves in acid and base.

It is important to keep track of charges in a diagram or a formula. A positively charged particle is written with a plus sign and a negatively charged particle with a minus sign. A neutral atom has no sign, and a molecule with equal positive and negative charges is also neutral overall, even though its parts may be charged.

To summarize this chapter, protons are positive, neutrons are neutral and electrons are negative, and the number of protons defines the element. The electrons are the particles that take part in bonding, which the next chapter describes by looking at the shells in which those electrons are arranged.
