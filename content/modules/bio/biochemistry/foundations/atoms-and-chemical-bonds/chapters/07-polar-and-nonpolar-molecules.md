A molecule is polar when its electrical charge is unevenly distributed, so that one part is slightly positive and another part is slightly negative. Polarity comes from two sources: polar bonds, in which electrons are shared unequally, and the overall shape of the molecule. A molecule can contain polar bonds and still be nonpolar if the bonds point in directions that cancel out. Water is polar because its two oxygen-hydrogen bonds are polar and bent into a V shape, so the charges do not cancel.

A nonpolar molecule has its electrons spread evenly across its atoms, or has polar bonds arranged so their effects cancel. Many hydrocarbons, which are chains of carbon and hydrogen, are nonpolar. Their carbon-hydrogen bonds are only slightly polar, and the molecules have no region that carries a clear net charge.

Lewis (1916) described electron sharing in a way that allows polarity to be understood. When two atoms share electrons equally, the bond is nonpolar, and when one atom pulls the shared electrons more strongly, the bond becomes polar. This simple idea explains a great deal of the behavior of molecules in water and in cell membranes.

The most important rule in this chapter is that like dissolves like. Polar molecules tend to dissolve in polar solvents such as water, and nonpolar molecules tend to dissolve in nonpolar solvents such as oil. A polar molecule can form hydrogen bonds or ionic attractions with water, which helps it dissolve, while a nonpolar molecule cannot, and so it is pushed away from water.

Ball (2008) explained that water's polarity makes it an excellent solvent for many biological molecules. Water surrounds polar and charged molecules, pulling them apart and holding them in solution. Nonpolar molecules, by contrast, tend to cluster together when placed in water, because water molecules prefer to form hydrogen bonds with each other rather than with them.

This clustering is the basis of the hydrophobic effect, which is one of the most important forces in biology. Fats and oils gather into droplets in water, and the tails of the phospholipids that make up cell membranes line up away from water. The same effect helps proteins fold, placing nonpolar side chains in the interior of the folded shape.

The behavior of buffers also depends on polarity. Good et al. (1966) chose buffer molecules that were soluble in water and carried ionizable groups, so that they could dissolve completely and release or take up hydrogen ions without leaving the solution. A nonpolar buffer would separate from water, and a buffer that could not dissolve would be unsuitable for biological work.

Polarity can be estimated from a molecule's structure. Look for bonds to oxygen and nitrogen, which are strongly electronegative, and for charged groups. Count whether those polar parts are spread around the molecule in a way that cancels out or leaves a clear positive and negative end. Long carbon chains with few polar groups are usually nonpolar.

Many biological molecules are amphipathic, meaning they have both polar and nonpolar regions. A phospholipid, for example, has a polar head that likes water and two nonpolar tails that avoid it. Such molecules arrange themselves at interfaces, forming membranes and micelles, and this dual behavior is one of the most useful ideas in biochemistry.

A practical habit helps here. Before using any molecule in an experiment, a biochemist checks whether it is polar, charged or nonpolar, because that decides which solvent to use, what concentration is achievable and how the compound should be stored. Choosing the wrong solvent can cause a compound to come out of solution, and that is one of the most common reasons a biochemical experiment fails.

To summarize this chapter, polarity arises from uneven electron sharing and from molecular shape, and polar molecules dissolve in water while nonpolar molecules avoid it. The hydrophobic effect drives nonpolar parts together in water, which shapes membranes and proteins. The final chapter of this module connects these ideas to the molecules of a living cell.
