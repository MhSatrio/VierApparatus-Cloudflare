Electrons do not move around the nucleus at random. They occupy layers, usually called shells, that sit at different distances from the nucleus and hold different amounts of energy. The innermost shell fills first, then the next, and so on. This orderly filling explains why atoms of the same family behave in similar ways and why the periodic table has the pattern that it does.

The first shell can hold up to two electrons. The second shell can hold up to eight, and the third can hold up to eighteen in the general pattern, although the atoms that matter most in biology fill only the first two shells. Hydrogen has one electron, which sits in the first shell. Carbon has six electrons: two in the first shell and four in the second. Oxygen has eight, with two in the first shell and six in the second.

The electrons in the outermost shell are the most important for chemistry. They are called valence electrons, and they are the ones that take part in bonding. An atom with a full outer shell is stable and tends not to react, which is why the noble gases such as neon and argon rarely form bonds. Most atoms of biological importance have partly filled outer shells, so they tend to share or transfer electrons to complete them.

Lewis (1916) described this tendency by arguing that atoms reach a stable arrangement when their outer shells are full. In the case of the elements of life, this usually means eight electrons in the outer shell, a rule often called the octet rule. Hydrogen is an exception, because its first shell is full with two electrons rather than eight.

The number of valence electrons tells a student how many bonds an atom is likely to form. Hydrogen has one valence electron and forms one bond. Oxygen has six valence electrons and needs two more, so it usually forms two bonds. Carbon has four valence electrons and needs four more, so it forms four bonds. Nitrogen has five and typically forms three bonds, while phosphorus and sulfur, in their common biological forms, often form several bonds in a similar way.

This simple counting rule explains why carbon is the backbone of life. Carbon's four bonds allow it to link to other carbon atoms in long chains and rings, as well as to hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen. The variety of shapes that carbon can form is the reason that sugars, fats, amino acids and nucleotides can all be built from the same small set of elements.

Ball (2008) pointed out that the simple arrangement of electrons in water also determines how the molecule behaves. The oxygen atom in water has two lone pairs of electrons in its outer shell in addition to the two bonds it forms with hydrogen. These lone pairs make the oxygen region of the molecule slightly negative and encourage water to form links with other molecules.

Good et al. (1966) selected buffer compounds in part because of the arrangement of their valence electrons. A buffer must be able to accept or release a hydrogen ion without changing its own structure in harmful ways, and the electron arrangement in its outer shells decides whether it can do this in the range of pH found in living things.

A helpful way to remember the shells is to picture them as rings of seats around a stage. The inner ring fills first, and the outer ring holds the seats that people in the audience can reach. Only the outer seats are involved when the audience meets the performers, just as only valence electrons take part in most chemical bonds.

To summarize this chapter, electrons fill shells around the nucleus, and the electrons in the outermost shell, called valence electrons, decide how an atom bonds. Most atoms of life try to reach eight outer electrons, which explains why carbon forms four bonds and oxygen forms two. The next chapter describes covalent bonds, in which atoms share those outer electrons.
