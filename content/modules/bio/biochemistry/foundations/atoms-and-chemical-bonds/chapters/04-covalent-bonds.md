A covalent bond forms when two atoms share one or more pairs of electrons. Each shared pair is held in the space between the two nuclei, and both atoms are attracted to it. Because the electrons are shared rather than given away, covalent bonds link atoms into stable molecules that can be large and complex. Most of the bonds that make up sugars, fats, proteins and nucleic acids are covalent.

The simplest example is the hydrogen molecule, H2. Each hydrogen atom has one electron, and when two hydrogen atoms come close, they share their two electrons as one pair. Each atom then has the two electrons of a full first shell, and the pair holds the atoms together. This shared pair is the single covalent bond that is written as a short line between the two symbols.

Lewis (1916) introduced the idea that such shared electron pairs can be drawn as dots or lines. His picture showed how atoms with partly filled outer shells could share electrons to reach a stable arrangement. The same idea explains molecules that contain more than one bond, such as carbon dioxide, in which each oxygen shares two pairs with the carbon atom.

Single, double and triple bonds differ in how many pairs are shared. A single bond shares one pair, a double bond shares two and a triple bond shares three. More shared pairs usually make the bond shorter and stronger, but the bond also becomes less flexible. Carbon-carbon double bonds in biological molecules, for example, do not rotate freely, which affects the shape of fats and sugars.

Covalent bonds are strong. Breaking one normally requires a large amount of energy, which is why the backbone of a protein or a strand of DNA is stable enough to survive in a living cell. This stability is important, because a molecule that fell apart easily could not carry information or store energy. Strong covalent bonds give biology a framework that can last while weaker bonds come and go around it.

Covalent bonds can be shared equally or unequally. When two atoms have a similar pull on electrons, the pair is shared almost equally, and the bond is called nonpolar. When one atom pulls more strongly, the shared electrons spend more time near it, and the bond becomes polar. Oxygen and hydrogen form polar covalent bonds, and this polarity is one of the reasons water behaves as it does.

Ball (2008) described how the covalent bonds in water make the molecule both a solvent and a participant in reactions. The oxygen-hydrogen bonds are polar, so the oxygen end of the molecule carries a small negative charge and the hydrogen ends carry small positive charges. These charge differences let water join with many other molecules in the cell, even though the covalent bonds inside the water molecule remain intact.

Good et al. (1966) designed buffers that had to resist changes in acidity without breaking their own covalent structure. A buffer that fell apart in the presence of acids or bases would be useless, so the strong bonds in its backbone were an important part of its design. Stability of this kind is a theme that runs through biochemistry, from the smallest buffer to the largest protein.

Covalent bonds also explain why molecules have shape. Each bond points in a particular direction, so atoms bonded to a carbon atom are arranged in a three-dimensional pattern. This arrangement determines how a molecule fits into an enzyme or how a sugar is recognized by a receptor. The rest of biochemistry relies on those shapes.

A simple way to think about a covalent bond is as a handshake that both atoms agree to hold. The handshake is firm, and it is maintained as long as both partners stay in place. Breaking the handshake takes effort, which is why covalent bonds are not easily undone in an ordinary cell.

To summarize this chapter, a covalent bond forms when two atoms share electron pairs, and covalent bonds are strong and give molecules their stable framework. Bonds can be single, double or triple, and they can be polar or nonpolar. The next chapter describes ionic bonds, in which electrons are transferred rather than shared.
