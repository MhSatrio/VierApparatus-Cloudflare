Some attractions between molecules are much weaker than covalent or ionic bonds, yet they are essential to life. The most important of these is the hydrogen bond. A hydrogen bond forms when a hydrogen atom that is already bonded to an electronegative atom, such as oxygen or nitrogen, is attracted to another electronegative atom nearby. The hydrogen carries a small positive charge, and the nearby oxygen or nitrogen carries a small negative charge, so the two are drawn together.

The word "electronegative" describes an atom that pulls electrons strongly toward itself. Oxygen and nitrogen are both strongly electronegative, which is why they create the partial charges needed for hydrogen bonds. A hydrogen bond is therefore not a bond in the sense of sharing electrons, but an attraction between a partly positive hydrogen and a partly negative neighbor.

Hydrogen bonds are weak compared with covalent bonds. Each one is roughly a few percent as strong as a typical covalent bond, which means that it can be broken and reformed easily under the conditions found in cells. This weakness is useful. A structure held together by many hydrogen bonds can be stable when they act together, yet flexible enough to change shape when needed.

Lewis (1916) described the electron arrangements that make such attractions possible, although the detailed picture of hydrogen bonding was developed later. The underlying idea, that electrons are unevenly distributed in molecules, explains why some bonds are polar and why partial charges can form between neighboring molecules.

Water is the most familiar example of hydrogen bonding. Each water molecule can form hydrogen bonds with several neighbors, creating a network that is constantly breaking and reforming. Ball (2008) described how this network gives water its high boiling point, its capacity to dissolve many substances and its ability to stabilize the shapes of large molecules inside cells.

Hydrogen bonds also hold together the two strands of DNA and help form the folded shapes of proteins. Each base pair in DNA is held by hydrogen bonds, and these bonds are strong enough to keep the strands together while still allowing them to be separated during copying. The same principle lets a protein form stable helices and sheets from a chain of amino acids.

Other weak interactions work alongside hydrogen bonds. Van der Waals forces are short-range attractions that arise when electron clouds briefly become uneven, and they are important when atoms pack closely together. Ionic attractions between charged groups on a molecule also act at a distance. Together these weak forces give biological molecules their shape and allow them to recognize one another.

Good et al. (1966) selected buffer compounds in part because their hydrogen bonding allowed them to hold or release hydrogen ions in a controlled way. The ability of a molecule to form and break hydrogen bonds with water affects how readily it dissolves and how it responds to changes in acidity. These properties are important in choosing buffers for living systems.

It is useful to think of weak interactions as a large crowd of small magnets. No single magnet is strong, but many together can hold a shape firmly, and the shape can still be rearranged by a stronger push. This combination of individually weak attractions and collective strength is one of the central lessons of molecular biology.

A student should remember that weakness is not the same as unimportance. A protein folded by many hydrogen bonds and other weak forces is held in a precise shape, and that shape determines what the protein can do. Losing these bonds through heat or chemicals can unfold the protein and destroy its function, which is why temperature and acidity matter so much in the laboratory.

To summarize this chapter, hydrogen bonds form between a partly positive hydrogen and a nearby electronegative oxygen or nitrogen, and they are much weaker than covalent bonds. Many weak interactions together shape water, DNA and proteins. The next chapter describes polar and nonpolar molecules and how they behave in water.
