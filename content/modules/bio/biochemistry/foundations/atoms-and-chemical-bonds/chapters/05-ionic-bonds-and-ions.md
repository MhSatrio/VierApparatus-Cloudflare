Not every bond involves sharing. In an ionic bond, one atom gives up one or more electrons to another atom. The atom that loses electrons becomes positively charged, and the atom that gains them becomes negatively charged. Because opposite charges attract, the two resulting ions stay together. Common table salt, sodium chloride, is the classic example of an ionic compound.

An atom that gains or loses electrons is called an ion. A sodium atom has one valence electron, which it readily gives up to reach the stable arrangement of the shell below it. After losing that electron, it has eleven protons and ten electrons, so it carries a net positive charge and is written Na+. A chlorine atom has seven valence electrons and readily accepts one more, becoming Cl-, with a net negative charge.

Lewis (1916) described how atoms can reach a stable outer shell either by sharing electrons or by taking them completely. His account covered both kinds of bond and explained why some elements tend to lose electrons while others tend to gain them. The idea that the outer shell drives these choices is the same one used in the previous chapter.

Ionic bonds are not as tightly directional as covalent bonds. An ion is surrounded by oppositely charged ions in all directions, and the attraction is spread through the whole crystal rather than limited to a single pair of atoms. This is why salt forms solid crystals with regular patterns, and why ionic compounds tend to dissolve well in water.

Ions are important in living systems for reasons that go beyond bonding. Sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium and chloride ions carry electrical signals across nerve cells and help control the movement of water and nutrients. Their charges are the basis of many biological processes, and the cell maintains different concentrations of them on either side of its membrane.

Water is the main medium in which ions are dissolved and moved about. Ball (2008) described how water molecules surround each ion, with their negative oxygen ends facing positive ions and their positive hydrogen ends facing negative ions. This shell of water weakens the attraction between the ions, which allows many of them to separate and move freely in solution.

Ionic charges also affect acid and base chemistry. A hydrogen ion is simply a proton, and when a molecule releases or takes up hydrogen ions, its charge changes. Good et al. (1966) designed buffers with this in mind, so that their charged forms stay in balance over the range of acidity found in living tissues. Their performance depends on how readily their groups gain or lose charged hydrogen ions.

It is useful to remember that ionic and covalent bonds are two ends of a range rather than two completely separate categories. In many bonds, electrons are shared unequally, so the atoms carry partial charges. These bonds are called polar covalent bonds, and they sit between the fully shared covalent bond and the fully transferred ionic bond.

A simple picture for an ionic bond is a pair of magnets with opposite poles. The attraction is strong when they are close, and it extends in all directions. Like a magnet, an ion attracts any oppositely charged partner it can reach, which is why ions gather in solutions and crystals rather than forming single pairs.

To summarize this chapter, an ionic bond forms when one atom transfers electrons to another, creating charged ions that attract each other. Sodium and chloride are a classic example, and ions carry the electrical signals and mineral balances that cells depend on. The next chapter describes hydrogen bonds and other weak attractions that are also common in living systems.
