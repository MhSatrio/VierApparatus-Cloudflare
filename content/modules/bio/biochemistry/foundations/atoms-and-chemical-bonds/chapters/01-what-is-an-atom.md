An atom is the smallest unit of an element that still has the properties of that element. Carbon atoms, oxygen atoms and hydrogen atoms are the basic pieces from which every living molecule is built. A protein, a sugar and a strand of DNA are all arrangements of atoms, and the chemistry of life is largely the chemistry of how those atoms are arranged and linked.

Atoms are extremely small. A single carbon atom is far too small to see with a light microscope, and the atoms in a drop of water are so numerous that they cannot be counted by hand. Even so, the properties of a substance can be predicted from the kinds of atoms it contains and how they are connected. This predictive power is one reason that chemistry is central to biology.

Lewis (1916) proposed one of the first useful pictures of how atoms join. He argued that a chemical bond is formed when atoms share electrons, and that the number of shared electrons follows a regular pattern. That idea still underlies most of the chemistry taught to biology students. It explains why carbon forms four bonds, why oxygen forms two and why hydrogen forms one.

Atoms of the same element all have the same number of protons, which is the defining feature of the element. Atoms of one element can differ in the number of neutrons they contain, and these versions are called isotopes. Isotopes of the same element behave almost identically in chemical reactions, which is why researchers can use them as labels to follow molecules through a cell.

It is helpful to think of an atom as a small central core surrounded by a cloud of moving electrons. The core is dense and holds most of the mass, while the electrons occupy a much larger region of space. Most of the volume of an atom is therefore empty, although this empty-looking space is where all the chemical action happens.

Biochemists use a few simple symbols for atoms. Carbon is written C, hydrogen H, oxygen O, nitrogen N, phosphorus P and sulfur S. These letters are a shorthand, not a picture, and they stand for a particular kind of atom with its own number of protons. A chemical formula combines these symbols to describe which atoms a molecule contains and in what numbers.

Water, which is the most abundant molecule in living cells, is made of two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom, written H2O. Ball (2008) reviewed how the properties of this simple molecule shape the behavior of cells, from the way proteins fold to the way nutrients move. The properties of water come from its atoms and the bonds between them, which is why the rest of this module focuses on those bonds.

Atoms also differ in size and in how strongly they attract electrons. Oxygen attracts electrons more strongly than hydrogen does, and this difference will matter in later chapters when we explain why water molecules are polar. For now, the key point is that every atom has a characteristic size and a characteristic pull on electrons.

Buffers, which resist changes in acidity, depend on the atoms and charges of their molecules. Good et al. (1966) described a set of buffer compounds designed for work with living systems, and their usefulness comes from how their atoms hold and release hydrogen ions. Understanding the atom is therefore useful even in practical laboratory work.

To summarize this chapter, an atom is the smallest unit that keeps the properties of an element, and the atoms of life are mainly carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus and sulfur. Molecules are formed when atoms are linked by bonds, which the next chapter describes by first looking inside the atom.
