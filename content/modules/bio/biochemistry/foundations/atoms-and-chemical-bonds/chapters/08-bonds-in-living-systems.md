A living cell uses every kind of bond described in this module, and each one has a job. Covalent bonds hold the backbones of large molecules together, so that sugars, fats, amino acids and nucleotides keep their shape under ordinary conditions. Ionic attractions help control the charge balance of the cell and guide some molecules toward one another. Weak bonds, including hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, allow large molecules to fold, pair and separate as the cell needs.

Carbohydrates are a good example. A sugar such as glucose is a ring of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen atoms linked by covalent bonds. When sugars join to form starch or glycogen, new covalent bonds are made between the rings, creating long chains. These chains can be broken down by enzymes that add water and release the sugar units, which is how the cell releases stored energy.

Lipids follow the same pattern. A fat molecule is built from a glycerol backbone and fatty acid chains, all joined by covalent bonds. Because the chains are long and largely nonpolar, the fat does not dissolve in water. Phospholipids add a polar head to this arrangement, and their amphipathic character lets them form the membranes that enclose every cell.

Proteins depend on all of the bond types. The peptide bond, which is a covalent link between amino acids, forms the chain. Hydrogen bonds stabilize helices and sheets, ionic attractions pair charged side chains, and hydrophobic interactions pack nonpolar side chains into the core of a folded protein. Each protein is therefore a balance of strong and weak bonds.

Nucleic acids use covalent bonds for their backbones and hydrogen bonds for their base pairs. The two strands of DNA are held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases, and each strand is a chain of sugars and phosphates joined covalently. This arrangement is strong enough to store information and weak enough to be copied and read.

Ball (2008) described water as the medium in which all these bonds operate. Water forms hydrogen bonds with polar and charged molecules, surrounds ions and pushes nonpolar molecules together. Its own covalent bonds remain intact, but its polar structure makes the cell's chemistry possible.

Buffers keep the cell's acidity within a narrow range, and Good et al. (1966) showed how chemical groups that gain or lose hydrogen ions can do this job. Living cells use similar buffering systems, so that enzymes and other molecules remain in the correct charge state. Changes in acidity can break ionic and hydrogen bonds and disrupt the shape of proteins, which is why cells defend their pH so carefully.

Understanding bonds also helps explain why many drugs and nutrients work. A drug that fits into a protein may do so through hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts, and a nutrient may be absorbed because it is polar enough to dissolve in blood but nonpolar enough to cross a membrane. The same principles therefore guide medicine and food science.

Lewis (1916) would recognize the same logic in these molecules: every bond is a shared or transferred pair of electrons, and the pattern of those pairs decides the shape and behavior of the whole structure.

Students who have followed this module should be able to look at a biological molecule and ask three questions. Which bonds are strong and covalent? Which charges can attract or repel? Which parts are polar and which are nonpolar? Answering these questions gives a working picture of how the molecule behaves in a cell, and it prepares the ground for the later modules on sugars, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids.

To summarize this chapter, covalent bonds build the backbones of life's molecules, ionic and weak bonds shape how they interact, and water provides the setting in which these bonds act. The module on amino acids and peptide bonds, which follows this one in the curriculum, applies these ideas to the building blocks of proteins.
