Living things use only some of the possible stereoisomers of their molecules. Most natural sugars belong to the D series, and most natural amino acids belong to the L series. These conventions were introduced before the R and S system, and they remain common in biochemistry, so students need to understand both. The D and L labels refer to the arrangement around one particular chiral carbon, not to the direction in which a molecule rotates light.

The D and L convention is based on glyceraldehyde, the simplest sugar with a chiral center. The arrangement of its hydroxyl group on the chiral carbon, drawn in a standard projection, was assigned as D or L. Other sugars and amino acids are then compared with this reference. A sugar is D if the chiral carbon farthest from the aldehyde or ketone group has the same arrangement as in D-glyceraldehyde.

Glucose is the most important example. Its naturally occurring form is D-glucose, and it is the sugar carried in blood and burned by most cells. Its mirror image, L-glucose, has the same formula but is not used by the enzymes of the body in the same way. The difference in configuration at one chiral center is enough to prevent most enzymes from recognizing the mirror form.

Amino acids use the L convention. Almost every amino acid in a protein has the L configuration at its alpha carbon, and the chapters on amino acids build on this fact. Alanine, the simplest amino acid with a chiral center, is L-alanine in proteins, and the R and S label for this form is S. The two systems give different names to the same arrangement, which is a common source of confusion for students.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the uniform choice of L-amino acids is essential for protein folding. A chain built from a mixture of L and D residues would fold into different shapes, and many of the enzymes of the cell would not recognize the chain. The uniformity of configuration in biology is therefore a condition for predictable protein structure.

Lewis (1916) gave the bonding picture that explains why the alpha carbon of an amino acid is tetrahedral, bonded to an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen and a side chain. The four different partners make the carbon chiral, and the choice of L or D determines which mirror form is used. Cells maintain this choice through enzymes that make only one form.

Cahn, Ingold and Prelog (1966) explained that the R and S system is the modern standard, and that the D and L labels are kept for historical reasons in sugars and amino acids. A student should be able to convert between the two when reading a textbook or a research paper. For many common amino acids, L corresponds to S, but the rule does not hold for every compound, and the conversion must be checked in each case.

Stereochemistry in sugars also affects their ring forms. In solution, a sugar can close into a ring, and the new chiral center that forms at the carbonyl carbon can take two arrangements. These arrangements, called alpha and beta, change the properties of the sugar and the way it is joined into polymers such as starch and cellulose. The next modules on carbohydrates describe these differences in detail.

To summarize this chapter, most natural sugars are D and most natural amino acids are L, with the labels referring to a reference arrangement rather than to rotation. Glucose and alanine show how the convention is applied, and the stereochemistry of these molecules determines how enzymes and proteins recognize them. The final chapter of this module describes how stereochemistry guides biological recognition.
