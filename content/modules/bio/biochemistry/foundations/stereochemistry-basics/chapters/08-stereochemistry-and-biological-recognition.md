Every biological recognition event is a meeting between two three-dimensional shapes. An enzyme binds its substrate, a receptor binds a hormone, and an antibody binds an antigen, and in each case the fit depends on the arrangement of the groups on both partners. Because enzymes and receptors are themselves built from chiral amino acids, they are chiral environments, and they respond differently to the two mirror-image forms of a molecule.

The simplest model of recognition is the lock and key. A substrate fits into an enzyme's active site when its groups match the groups lining the site, and the match is three-dimensional. An enantiomer that places its groups in the wrong positions may fit poorly or not at all. This explains why an enzyme that acts on one form of a sugar or amino acid usually ignores its mirror image.

Anfinsen (1973) described how the folding of a protein creates these binding sites. The chirality of each amino acid determines the direction in which the chain turns, and the resulting pockets are shaped by that chirality. A binding site built from L-amino acids is therefore chiral by construction, and it is well suited to recognizing molecules of a particular configuration.

Lewis (1916) gave the tetrahedral bonding picture that explains why the two forms of a chiral molecule are distinct. The groups of an enantiomer occupy particular points in space, and the enzyme or receptor provides points of contact that match only some arrangements. The chemistry of recognition is therefore a geometric problem as well as a chemical one.

Cahn, Ingold and Prelog (1966) provided the naming system that allows a biochemist to describe which enantiomer is recognized. A paper that reports an enzyme's specificity will state the configuration of the substrate using the R and S or the D and L convention, so that other scientists can compare results. Precise naming is part of the science of recognition.

Stereochemistry also matters for drugs that bind to receptors. A receptor that is activated by one enantiomer of a drug may be blocked or ignored by the other. Some drugs are sold as pure enantiomers for this reason, and the other form is removed during manufacture. This practice has improved the safety and effectiveness of many medicines.

Odour and taste offer everyday examples. Some pairs of enantiomers smell or taste different because the receptors in the nose and tongue recognize them differently. The caraway flavor, for instance, comes from one form of a compound, while its mirror image has a different smell. These differences show that the nervous system can detect the shape of a molecule as well as its composition.

Students should see recognition as a dialogue between the molecule and the surface that receives it. The shape of the surface is itself chiral, so the match depends on both partners. Learning to picture this match in three dimensions prepares the reader for the later chapters on enzymes, where binding sites and catalysis are described in detail.

Recognition also operates in the immune system. An antibody binds an antigen through a surface whose shape is chiral, and an antibody raised against one form of a molecule may bind the other form weakly or not at all. Immunologists take this into account when they design vaccines and tests, because a reagent that cannot tell the mirror forms apart may give misleading results.

To summarize this chapter, enzymes and receptors are built from chiral amino acids and therefore distinguish between enantiomers. The fit between a molecule and its binding site depends on three-dimensional shape, and this is why one mirror form is often active while the other is not.
