A chiral center is an atom in a molecule that carries four different groups attached to it. Chirality comes from the Greek word for hand, and the name fits: a chiral molecule cannot be placed exactly over its mirror image, just as a hand cannot be placed exactly over its reflection. Most chiral centers in biology are carbon atoms, although other atoms can also be chiral in some compounds.

The condition of four different groups is the important one. If two of the four groups are the same, the carbon is not a chiral center, because the molecule and its mirror image can then be turned into each other. A carbon bonded to two hydrogens, a methyl group and a hydroxyl group is an example, since the two hydrogens are identical and the molecule has a plane of symmetry. Students should check each carbon in turn and ask whether all four partners are truly different.

Lewis (1916) described the bonding of carbon, which forms four bonds directed toward the corners of a tetrahedron. When four different groups surround a carbon in this arrangement, the tetrahedron can be built in two ways that are mirror images of each other. These two arrangements are the source of the chirality, and they cannot be made identical by rotation alone.

Lactic acid provides a clear example. Its central carbon carries a hydrogen, a hydroxyl group, a methyl group and a carboxyl group. All four groups are different, so the carbon is a chiral center, and lactic acid exists as two mirror-image forms. In the body, the muscle cells that produce lactic acid during intense exercise usually make only one of these forms, showing that the cell's enzymes recognize the arrangement.

Anfinsen (1973) discussed how the chirality of amino acids affects protein folding. Every amino acid except glycine has a chiral alpha carbon bonded to an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen and a side chain. Living organisms use one arrangement almost exclusively, and this uniformity makes protein folding predictable. A protein built from mixed arrangements would fold very differently.

Cahn, Ingold and Prelog (1966) provided the rules that rank the four groups around a chiral center, which are needed to name its configuration. The next chapter describes these rules and how to apply them. For now, the essential skill is recognizing the chiral center itself, and it helps to circle each carbon in a drawing and count its four partners.

It is worth noting that a molecule can have more than one chiral center. Each one adds a possible arrangement, and the number of mirror-image pairs grows accordingly. Glucose, for example, has several chiral centers, and this is why it exists in many stereoisomeric forms. The chapters on diastereomers later in the module address this situation.

Chiral centers are also important in designing synthetic compounds. A chemist who wants to make a single form of a molecule must control the arrangement at each center, because a mixture of forms may behave differently in the body. This practical concern makes the recognition of chiral centers a basic skill for anyone working with biological molecules.

Chiral centers can also be recognized in larger molecules. In a long chain, each carbon that carries a hydroxyl group, an amino group or a branch is a candidate, and each candidate must be checked separately. Counting the chiral centers in a molecule tells a reader how many stereoisomers are possible, which is the starting point for every later discussion of shape.

To summarize this chapter, a chiral center is an atom bearing four different groups, and most chiral centers in biology are carbon atoms. Lactic acid and the alpha carbon of most amino acids are common examples. The next chapter explains enantiomers, the mirror-image pairs that chiral centers create.
