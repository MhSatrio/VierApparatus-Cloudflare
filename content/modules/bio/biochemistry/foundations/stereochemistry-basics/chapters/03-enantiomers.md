Enantiomers are a pair of molecules that are mirror images of each other and cannot be superimposed. Each member of the pair has the same atoms, the same bonds and the same chemical formula as its partner. The only difference is the spatial arrangement around the chiral center, which is reversed in the mirror image. Enantiomers are therefore the simplest example of the spatial differences discussed in the previous chapters.

Because enantiomers have the same formula, they share many physical properties. They have the same melting point, the same boiling point, the same density and the same solubility in ordinary solvents. A chemist who measures these properties cannot tell the two forms apart without a special test. This similarity is why enantiomers were difficult to separate and identify in the early history of chemistry.

The difference appears when enantiomers meet other chiral objects. A chiral enzyme, a chiral receptor or a chiral solvent can distinguish one enantiomer from the other, because the two forms fit into the chiral binding site in different ways. A right-handed glove fits the right hand and not the left, and the same logic applies to enantiomers. This selective fit is the source of their different biological effects.

Lewis (1916) gave the bonding picture that explains why such mirror images are possible. A carbon with four different partners can be arranged in two ways, and the two arrangements are reflections of each other. Because the atoms are connected in the same order, the difference is purely spatial, and the molecules cannot be converted into one another without breaking bonds.

Anfinsen (1973) pointed out that living systems use one form of each amino acid almost without exception. The amino acids that build proteins are of one configuration, and the enzymes that make and break them are built to handle that form. A protein assembled from the wrong enantiomer would fail to fold in the normal way, which shows how strongly biology depends on the choice.

Cahn, Ingold and Prelog (1966) provided the system that gives each enantiomer a distinct name, so that the two forms can be referred to without confusion. Their rules are applied in the next chapter, and they make it possible to write the configuration of an enantiomer in a notation that other chemists will understand.

Enantiomers are also important in medicine. The two forms of a drug can have different effects, and one form may be active while the other is inactive or harmful. A famous historical case involving a sedative drug showed that a single enantiomer could cause serious harm, and that case led to much stricter testing of chiral drugs. Today, regulators usually expect the activity of each enantiomer to be studied.

A useful way to remember enantiomers is to think of two hands that have the same parts but point in opposite directions. The pair looks alike when viewed side by side, yet each hand fits only its own glove. The reader who can picture this image will find it easier to recognize enantiomers in drawings and to predict their biological behavior.

Enantiomers can also be told apart by how they react. When a chiral molecule reacts with a symmetrical reagent, both enantiomers usually give the same products at the same rate. When the reagent is itself chiral, the rates can differ greatly. This is one reason chemists use chiral catalysts and enzymes to make one enantiomer selectively, and the difference in rates is a practical measure of how well a chiral partner can tell the mirror forms apart.

To summarize this chapter, enantiomers are mirror-image molecules that differ only in the arrangement around a chiral center. They share most physical properties but differ in how they fit chiral partners such as enzymes and receptors. The next chapter describes the Cahn-Ingold-Prelog rules, which give each enantiomer a precise name.
