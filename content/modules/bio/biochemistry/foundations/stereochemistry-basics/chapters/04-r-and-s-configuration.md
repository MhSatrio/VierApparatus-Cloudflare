The R and S system names the configuration of a chiral center in a way that does not depend on how a molecule is drawn. The letters come from the Latin words rectus, meaning right, and sinister, meaning left. The system was made rigorous by Cahn, Ingold and Prelog (1966), whose priority rules are still used today. Learning the rules takes some practice, but once a student has applied them a few times, they become routine.

The first step is to rank the four groups attached to the chiral center by priority. Each group is ranked by the atomic number of the atom directly bonded to the center. A higher atomic number gives a higher priority. Oxygen outranks nitrogen, which outranks carbon, which outranks hydrogen. If two groups begin with the same atom, the next atoms in each branch are compared until a difference is found.

Lewis (1916) explained the electron structure that gives each atom its characteristic place in this ranking. The atomic number reflects the number of electrons, and the priority rules follow naturally from how electrons are distributed around each center. The rules are therefore not arbitrary; they follow from the chemistry already described in earlier modules.

The second step is to orient the molecule so that the lowest-priority group points away from the viewer. Imagine looking at the chiral center with the hydrogen, or the other lowest-priority group, pointing directly backward. Then trace the path from the highest-priority group to the second and third. If the path runs clockwise, the configuration is R. If it runs counterclockwise, the configuration is S.

Consider lactic acid as an example. The carbon has a hydroxyl group, a carboxyl group, a methyl group and a hydrogen. The hydroxyl group has the highest priority, because oxygen has the highest atomic number, followed by the carboxyl group, then the methyl group, and finally the hydrogen. Once the hydrogen points away, the path from hydroxyl to carboxyl to methyl determines whether the molecule is R or S.

Anfinsen (1973) described how the common amino acids are named using the same system, and most of them are S at the alpha carbon, with glycine as the exception because it has no chiral center. Knowing this fact allows a student to check the configuration of an amino acid quickly, and the later modules on amino acids use this convention throughout.

Cahn, Ingold and Prelog (1966) also explained how to handle more complicated cases, such as groups that begin with the same atoms. The method works by exploring each branch outward until a difference appears, and it is especially useful for groups containing double bonds. Students who meet such a case should work through the branches one step at a time, recording each comparison.

A common mistake is to forget to orient the lowest-priority group away from the viewer. A correct ranking followed by a wrong orientation gives the wrong answer, so students should always check the orientation before reading the direction of the path. Practising with physical models or with careful drawings is the best way to avoid this error.

Students should practise the rules on several molecules before relying on them. A useful sequence is to start with a simple chiral compound, rank its groups, draw the lowest-priority group pointing away and read the direction. Repeating the process on amino acids and sugars builds the speed and accuracy needed in later modules.

To summarize this chapter, the R and S system ranks the four groups by atomic number and reads the direction of the path from highest to lowest priority with the lowest group pointing away. Lactic acid and most natural amino acids provide practical examples. The next chapter describes molecules with more than one chiral center, which can be diastereomers or meso compounds.
