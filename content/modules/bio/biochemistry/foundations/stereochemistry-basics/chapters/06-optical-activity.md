Light is a wave that vibrates in many directions at once. When ordinary light passes through a polarizing filter, only the waves vibrating in one plane emerge, and the light is called plane-polarized. Chiral molecules have a remarkable effect on this plane: a solution of a single enantiomer rotates it, turning the plane to the left or to the right. This property is called optical activity, and it was one of the first ways that chemists discovered the three-dimensional nature of molecules.

The rotation is measured with a polarimeter. Light passes through a tube containing the sample, and the instrument reads the angle through which the plane has been turned. The size of the angle depends on the concentration of the sample, the length of the tube, the wavelength of the light and the temperature. Chemists report a standard value called the specific rotation, which allows results from different laboratories to be compared.

The direction of rotation is marked with a plus sign for clockwise and a minus sign for counterclockwise, and this is written in parentheses before the compound's name. The sign does not directly tell whether a molecule is R or S, although the two properties are often correlated. The R and S system describes the geometry, while the optical rotation is a physical measurement of how the geometry interacts with light.

Lewis (1916) explained the electron distribution in molecules, and the interaction of light with that distribution is the basis of optical activity. A chiral molecule has an asymmetric electron arrangement that interacts differently with the two circular components of polarized light. The interaction results in a rotation of the plane, which is a measurable consequence of the molecule's shape.

Enantiomers rotate light by equal amounts in opposite directions. A solution of one enantiomer at a given concentration turns the plane to the right by a fixed angle, and the solution of its mirror image turns it to the left by the same angle. A mixture containing equal amounts of both forms, called a racemic mixture, shows no net rotation, because the two effects cancel. This cancellation is a simple way to test whether a sample is pure.

Cahn, Ingold and Prelog (1966) provided the naming system that is used alongside optical rotation in the literature. Because the rotation sign does not always match the R or S label, chemists specify both when they describe a compound, and the two systems together give a complete description of its stereochemistry.

Anfinsen (1973) noted that proteins made from natural amino acids rotate polarized light in characteristic ways, and that the rotation changes when the protein unfolds. Chemists use optical rotation to monitor the folding state of proteins in solution, which shows how a basic property of chiral molecules can be used to study large biological structures.

The practical lesson for students is that optical rotation is a measurement that can confirm the presence of a single enantiomer. A sample that rotates light strongly is usually enriched in one form, and a sample that does not rotate may be a racemic mixture or an achiral compound. Combined with the naming rules, this measurement gives a reliable way to check the stereochemistry of a compound in the laboratory.

Optical rotation also helps researchers follow a reaction. If a chiral starting material is converted into a product, the change in rotation during the reaction shows how the stereochemistry is changing over time.

To summarize this chapter, chiral molecules rotate plane-polarized light, and enantiomers rotate it by equal amounts in opposite directions. A racemic mixture shows no net rotation, and optical measurements can confirm the purity of a sample. The next chapter applies stereochemistry to the sugars and amino acids of living things.
