Many molecules contain more than one chiral center, and this creates a richer set of possible arrangements. With two chiral centers there can be up to four stereoisomers, meaning molecules with the same connections but different spatial arrangements. Some of these stereoisomers are mirror images of each other, which makes them enantiomers. Others are not mirror images at all, and these are called diastereomers.

Diastereomers differ in configuration at some, but not all, of their chiral centers. They have different physical properties, such as melting points, boiling points and solubility, so chemists can separate them by ordinary methods like crystallization or chromatography. This is a practical advantage over enantiomers, which share most of their properties and are much harder to separate.

Lewis (1916) described the bonding that gives each chiral center its arrangement. When a molecule has several such centers, each one can be independently arranged, and the combination of arrangements creates the variety of diastereomers. The principle is the same as for a single center, but the number of possible combinations grows quickly.

Sugars are a rich source of diastereomers. Glucose and galactose differ in configuration at only one of their chiral centers, and they are therefore diastereomers rather than enantiomers. Their different arrangements cause them to be handled differently by the enzymes of the body, and this is why the two sugars are metabolized through different routes despite their similar formulas.

Meso compounds are a special case. A meso compound has two or more chiral centers but also contains an internal plane of symmetry, so that the molecule is identical to its own mirror image. Because it is superimposable on its mirror image, a meso compound is achiral overall, even though it has chiral centers. Tartaric acid provides a classic example, with one meso form that does not rotate polarized light.

Cahn, Ingold and Prelog (1966) explained that the same R and S labels can be used for each chiral center in a meso compound, and that the presence of two labels that cancel each other is a sign of symmetry. A student who finds such a pattern in a molecule should suspect a meso compound and check whether the molecule has an internal mirror plane.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the combination of chiral centers in a protein is a major factor in its folding. Each amino acid residue has its own chiral center, and the sequence of configurations shapes the way the chain can fold. A change in the configuration of one residue can alter the entire folded structure, although in normal biology every residue has the same configuration.

Students should remember that the number of stereoisomers does not always equal the simple count of combinations, because meso compounds and symmetry reduce the total. Careful inspection of each molecule is needed to count its distinct forms, and drawing the molecule and its mirror image is a useful check.

A practical example helps. Consider a molecule with two chiral centers, each bearing four different groups. The four possible arrangements form two pairs of enantiomers. The members of one pair are not mirror images of the members of the other pair, so each pair is diastereomeric to the other. This pattern holds for many sugars and amino acid derivatives, and it explains why a list of stereoisomers often falls into more than one family.

To summarize this chapter, diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images and differ in some but not all chiral centers, which gives them different physical properties. Meso compounds have chiral centers yet are achiral because of an internal mirror plane. The next chapter describes optical activity, the property that distinguishes enantiomers in the laboratory.
