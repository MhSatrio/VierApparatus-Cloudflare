Stereochemistry is the study of how atoms are arranged in space. Two molecules that have the same formula and the same bonds between atoms can differ in the way those atoms point in three dimensions, and such differences can change the properties of the molecules completely. A chemist who writes a formula on paper has only shown part of the story. The rest lies in the spatial arrangement, which the formula alone does not show.

The simplest way to see this is to remember that a biological molecule is a shape before it is a list of atoms. An enzyme has a pocket that fits one particular arrangement of groups, and a receptor has a binding site that recognizes a particular outline. If the molecule arrives in the wrong arrangement, the fit is poor, and the reaction or signal does not happen. Shape is therefore a key part of the molecule's function.

Lewis (1916) explained that a carbon atom forms four bonds, and that these bonds point toward the corners of a tetrahedron when the carbon has four different partners. This tetrahedral geometry is the starting point for stereochemistry. It means that a carbon bonded to four different groups can exist in two arrangements that look alike at first sight but cannot be superimposed.

Stereochemistry also matters for the way that drugs act. A drug that binds to a receptor in one arrangement may have a strong effect, while its mirror image may have little effect or even a harmful one. Chemists who design drugs therefore pay close attention to the three-dimensional form of each candidate molecule, and the regulatory agencies that review medicines often require the arrangement to be specified.

Cahn, Ingold and Prelog (1966) introduced a systematic way of naming spatial arrangements, so that chemists in different countries can describe a molecule without confusion. Their system assigns each chiral center a label that is independent of the drawing, and it is still the basis of the names used in biochemistry. The later chapters of this module show how their rules are applied.

Anfinsen (1973) showed that the folding of a protein depends on the arrangement of its amino acids in space, and that the particular form adopted by a chain is determined by the sequence and by the chemistry of its groups. Although his work concerned large molecules, the principle is the same as for small ones: the arrangement in space is essential to what the molecule does.

Students should notice that stereochemistry does not change the composition of a molecule. Two mirror-image forms have identical masses, identical melting points in many cases, and identical reactions with simple reagents that ignore shape. Their differences appear mainly when they meet a partner that is itself three-dimensional, such as an enzyme, a receptor or another chiral molecule.

A helpful image is a pair of gloves. Both gloves have the same parts and the same size, but a left glove fits only the left hand. A right-handed person can use only the right glove comfortably, even though the gloves look similar. Biological recognition works in a similar way, and the enzyme or receptor is the hand that fits only one of the two gloves.

The three-dimensional view also changes how a reader should interpret a flat drawing. A zigzag line on paper represents a chain that extends into space, and a wedge or a dash shows which groups point toward or away from the viewer. Learning to read these conventions is part of learning stereochemistry, and it is needed for every later chapter in this module.

To summarize this chapter, stereochemistry is the study of spatial arrangement, and a molecule's shape often determines its function. Tetrahedral carbon atoms can give rise to mirror-image forms, which living systems distinguish. The next chapter introduces the chiral center, the atom at the heart of this difference.
