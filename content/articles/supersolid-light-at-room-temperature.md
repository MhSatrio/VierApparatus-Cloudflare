A supersolid sounds like a contradiction. A superfluid flows without any viscosity, while a crystal holds its atoms in a fixed, repeating arrangement. A supersolid does both at once: it has the frictionless flow of a superfluid and a periodic density pattern like that of a crystal (Léonard et al., 2017). The first clear demonstration came in 2017, in an ultracold quantum gas that had to be cooled to temperatures close to absolute zero (Léonard et al., 2017).

A 2026 paper in *Science Advances* reports a supersolid at room temperature, in a quantum fluid made of light rather than of cold atoms (Muszyński et al., 2026).

## How a Fluid of Light Is Made

The experiment uses exciton-polaritons. These are hybrid particles formed when light is trapped in an optical microcavity and coupled strongly to electronic excitations in a material, so that the particles are part light and part matter. Under the right conditions, many polaritons can condense into a single quantum state, which behaves as a fluid of light.

The team built a special microcavity. It is filled with a nematic liquid crystal and two thin layers of an organic polymer, methyl-substituted ladder-type poly(p-phenylene) (MeLPPP), which hosts the exciton resonances (Muszyński et al., 2026). The liquid crystal induces spin-orbit coupling, which creates two separate lowest-energy states, or degenerate minima, for the polaritons to condense into (Muszyński et al., 2026).

## What the Experiment Shows

The condensate forms in those two minima, and its density arranges itself into stripes, a periodic pattern in space. The position of the stripes changes from one run of the experiment to the next, even though the device contains a random disorder potential. The authors describe this as evidence that the stripes are robust against disorder and that the system spontaneously breaks translational symmetry, meaning it picks one of several equivalent arrangements on its own (Muszyński et al., 2026).

The same condensate also shows vortices that appear at random, another hallmark of superfluidity (Muszyński et al., 2026).

Taken together, these observations match the defining features of a supersolid: a fluid with a crystal-like pattern. The authors report that the state exists at room temperature, which is the main difference from the earlier atomic experiments (Muszyński et al., 2026; Léonard et al., 2017).

## Why Room Temperature Matters

The earlier demonstration in a quantum gas showed that a supersolid could be made from a fluid that breaks a continuous translational symmetry (Léonard et al., 2017). Making the same state in a solid-state optical device at room temperature opens the study of this physics to much simpler equipment. According to the University of Warsaw, one of the four institutions in the team, the approach could also support future technologies such as optical computing and highly sensitive sensors (University of Warsaw, as reported by ScienceDaily, 2026). Those applications are proposals, not demonstrated devices. The paper establishes the physics; practical use will need further work.

## What Is Still Unsettled

The result is an observation of a new state in one specific device. Its random stripes and vortices are a clear signature, but they do not yet show how the state would perform as part of a working optical component. Questions about stability over time, how precisely the pattern can be controlled, and how the state scales to larger devices remain for future experiments.

## Why It Matters

Supersolids connect fluid dynamics, crystallography and quantum statistics, and they are a test case for how quantum matter organizes itself. A supersolid that forms in a device, at room temperature and with an optical readout, gives physicists a new way to explore those questions. It also shows that exciton-polariton systems can host collective quantum states beyond simple condensation.
