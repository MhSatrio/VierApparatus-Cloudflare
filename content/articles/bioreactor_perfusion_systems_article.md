The biopharmaceutical industry is moving from batch and fed-batch production toward continuous biomanufacturing. At the centre of this change is the **bioreactor perfusion system**. It keeps mammalian cells growing at high density for long periods by continuously adding fresh medium and removing spent medium, while a cell-retention device keeps the cells inside the vessel. Compared with fed-batch culture, perfusion can give higher volumetric productivity, steadier product quality and a smaller facility footprint (Bielser et al., 2018; Pollock et al., 2012).

## Why Perfusion Changes the Culture Environment

In fed-batch culture, cells accumulate lactate and ammonium over a fixed run. These byproducts slow growth, eventually push cells toward death, and can reduce the quality of the therapeutic protein. A perfusion culture avoids this by holding the medium close to a steady state. Fresh medium replaces spent medium continuously, so growth is no longer limited by nutrient depletion (Pollock et al., 2012).

Perfusion has been used to reach very high densities of Chinese Hamster Ovary (CHO) cells, well above the densities usually reached in fed-batch culture (Clincke et al., 2013). A shorter residence time for the product in the vessel also limits its exposure to proteases, which can reduce degradation and product heterogeneity.

## Core Cell Retention Technologies

What distinguishes a perfusion bioreactor is its **cell retention device**. The device lets spent medium out while returning cells to the vessel. Several designs are in use, and each makes a different trade-off between retention, shear stress and fouling (Voisard et al., 2003; Clincke et al., 2013).

### Alternating Tangential Flow (ATF) filtration

ATF uses a diaphragm pump to make the flow across a hollow-fiber membrane alternate in direction. The reversing flow keeps the membrane surface cleaner than steady flow does, which reduces fouling. ATF is widely used for long continuous runs (Clincke et al., 2013).

### Tangential flow filtration (TFF) and unidirectional loops

TFF recirculates culture fluid in one direction across a membrane, usually driven by a peristaltic or centrifugal pump. It is efficient for shorter runs and lower cell densities. Constant one-way shear can cause membrane fouling or cell stress during runs of several weeks, which is why many long perfusion processes choose ATF (Voisard et al., 2003).

### Acoustic and centrifugal separators

Acoustic separators use standing ultrasonic waves to gather cells into clusters that settle out of the flow, with no membrane. Centrifugal devices separate cells by density. Neither type has a filter that can clog, but their retention efficiency can fall at very high cell concentrations.

## Process Control, Steady State and Cell Bleed

Running a perfusion culture for weeks requires process analytical technology (PAT). Two common control parameters are the **vessel volumes per day (VVD)** and the **cell-specific perfusion rate (CSPR)**, which is the medium volume supplied per cell per day.

To hold the viable cell density (VCD) steady, operators bleed off a controlled fraction of the cell suspension, balancing the cells added by growth. Cell-density sensors based on capacitance or optical density can drive the bleed pump automatically. Keeping the culture in steady state helps maintain high viability for the whole run.

## Economic and Operational Advantages

- **Higher volumetric productivity.** High cell densities give more product per litre per day. Economic comparisons of perfusion and fed-batch processes weigh this productivity gain against the cost of extra equipment and media (Pollock et al., 2012).
- **Possible gains in product quality.** A short residence time limits exposure to degradation for labile proteins.
- **Smaller facility footprint.** Smaller equipment can mean lower capital and utility costs, although media handling adds its own infrastructure.
- **Flexibility with single-use systems.** Perfusion can be run in single-use bags and disposable flow paths, which shorten turnaround and reduce cross-contamination risk.

## Challenges and Outlook

Perfusion brings its own engineering problems. Large volumes of medium must be stored cold, supplied under sterile conditions and prepared automatically. Longer runs also increase the chance of contamination, so the sterility of every connection and air filter needs to be validated.

Looking ahead, real-time multi-attribute monitoring (for example by mass spectrometry) and automated control loops could make perfusion easier to run. Linking continuous upstream culture with continuous downstream purification is the next step toward fully continuous biomanufacturing.
