Cancer does more than grow in one place. It can change the blood and immune system, sometimes suppressing the body's ability to attack the tumor. Much of that work has focused on the bone marrow, where blood cells are made. A November 2026 *Science* paper asks whether a tumor can set up blood-cell production in another organ, and reports that the liver can be used this way (Pan et al., 2026).

## A local blood-forming niche

The authors identify a form of blood formation in the liver driven by stem cells that the tumor recruits from the bone marrow. Liver metastases create what the paper calls ectopic periportal niches, local structures that recruit stem cells from the bone marrow. The recruitment depends on signalling through a chemokine receptor, CCR1, acting on Kupffer cells, the immune cells of the liver (Pan et al., 2026).

## From stem cells to suppressive neutrophils

The recruited stem cells keep their ability to renew and to form several cell types, but a signalling switch, CREB activation, pushes them towards the myeloid lineage. The result is a population of immature neutrophils that express annexin A1. These neutrophils make glucocorticoids, which act on nearby cells in a paracrine way (Pan et al., 2026).

Those glucocorticoids raise the expression of immune checkpoint receptors on CD8 T cells and natural killer cells and leave them functionally unresponsive, which the authors call anergy. In short, the tumor's own metastases in the liver generate the signals that quieten the immune cells that could attack them (Pan et al., 2026).

## Why the liver is a special site

The liver is a filter for blood coming from the gut, and it contains resident immune cells, including the Kupffer cells that the paper shows are needed for the recruitment signal. That makes it a place where immune cells are already present and constantly exposed to material from the rest of the body. A tumor that changes the liver's immune environment therefore has access to a large and well-connected set of cells.

## Why the chain matters for treatment

Immunotherapy works by releasing the brakes on T cells and natural killer cells, and it works best when those cells can still respond. The paper suggests that metastases in the liver may hold those cells in an unresponsive state through a mechanism that is distinct from the tumor itself, which, if it holds in patients, could help explain why liver spread sometimes responds poorly to treatment. The paper does not test that link, so it is a hypothesis to examine in patients, but it points to a concrete question: whether a patient's liver metastases carry the same neutrophil and glucocorticoid signature.

## What is established and what is not

The paper ties together a chain of mechanisms, from recruitment to differentiation to immune suppression. Each step has specific molecular players, which makes the chain testable. The conclusion the authors draw is that liver metastases reprogram hepatic hematopoiesis to promote immunosuppression (Pan et al., 2026).

Mechanistic chains are easier to trace in experiments than to confirm in patients. The next steps would be to show that the same niches exist in human liver metastases, and that blocking any link in the chain restores anti-tumor immunity. Those tests decide whether the mechanism is a practical target.

## Why it matters

For cancer treatment, the paper widens the set of places to look for immune suppression. If liver metastases can create their own neutrophil supply, then drugs that target the recruitment signal, the CREB switch or the glucocorticoid pathway could be tested as ways to restore immune attack. For readers, the broader point is that tumors can change distant organs, not only the tissue around them.
