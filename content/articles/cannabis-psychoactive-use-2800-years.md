Cannabis is one of the most widely used psychoactive plants today, yet direct evidence of its use in the distant past is rare. Most of what archaeologists have found is plant material, which shows that the plant was present but not how it was used. A November 2026 *Science* paper reports something more direct: chemical compounds from the plant preserved in human hair (Caspari et al., 2026).

## What was found

The study examined hair from people buried in a monumental tomb in Siberia. The authors report offerings of plant parts and chemical compounds from the cannabis plant in that hair, which they date to at least 2,800 years ago (Caspari et al., 2026). Hair keeps chemical traces of what a person consumed or was exposed to, which is why it is a useful archive when other evidence is scarce.

The authors interpret the findings as the earliest direct evidence of cannabis use. They also suggest that the plant was used for its psychoactive qualities across Eurasia, and that wild populations might have had naturally higher levels of the active compounds (Caspari et al., 2026).

## What the evidence can and cannot show

Hair records exposure in a way that can be read by chemistry, but it does not record how often a person used a substance, in what amount, or why. The paper describes offerings in a burial context, which suggests a ritual role. It does not show that the same practice was common across the region.

The authors' broader claim, that cannabis was used for its psychoactive qualities across Eurasia, rests on this tomb and on the wider context they describe. A single site, however well dated, is a starting point for that conclusion rather than the whole argument.

## How hair keeps a record

Hair grows at a fairly steady rate, roughly a centimeter a month on the scalp, so a strand can preserve a timeline of exposure. Compounds from a substance reach the hair through the blood and stay in the hair shaft after they have cleared from the body. Reading that timeline takes chemical methods sensitive enough to detect trace compounds, and the authors' claim rests on those measurements. Hair from a burial also raises questions of contamination and preservation, which is why independent replication matters for a finding this early.

## Reading the claim carefully

The central finding is that cannabis compounds were present in ancient hair from a monumental tomb, and that the plant appears there as an offering. The broader claim about use across Eurasia is a larger inference. Readers should keep the two levels apart: what was measured in one tomb, and what the authors suggest about the wider region and about the plant's properties. Plants can vary in their active compounds with growing conditions and genetics, so a finding about wild populations depends on sampling many plants from many places, which a single tomb cannot provide.

## Why it matters

The paper links ancient evidence to modern debates. The authors argue that their findings inform discussions about cannabis in medicine, public health, law and industry (Caspari et al., 2026). Knowing when and how people first used a plant helps separate what is new about modern use from what has a long history.

For readers, the value of the study is in its method as much as its date. Combining archaeology with chemical analysis of preserved tissue can answer questions that plant remains alone cannot, and the same approach could be applied to other plants and other burial sites.
