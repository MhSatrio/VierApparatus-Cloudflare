Every atomic clock today counts oscillations of electrons in atoms or ions. A nuclear clock would count oscillations inside the nucleus, which is better shielded from the environment and so could in principle keep time more steadily. Thorium-229 has a uniquely low-lying nuclear transition that laser light can reach, which is why it has been pursued for decades (Huang et al., 2026).

Two papers published in *Nature* on 7 October 2026 report that this idea now works in the laboratory. One comes from a group led by Thorsten Schumm, and the other from a group led by Shiqian Ding (Toscani De Col et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026).

## How the clock works

In the Vienna work, the thorium-229 nuclei are embedded in a small, room-temperature calcium fluoride crystal. A continuous laser at 148 nm is stabilized to the nuclear transition using rapid feedback from absorption spectroscopy. The laser's frequency is then compared with an ytterbium single-ion clock, which provides the reference. The authors report a shot-noise-limited fractional frequency instability of 3 × 10⁻¹² divided by the square root of the averaging time, reaching about 10⁻¹⁵ after a day of operation (Toscani De Col et al., 2026).

The Beijing group describes a similar goal with a different set-up. It stabilizes a narrow-linewidth 148.4 nm vacuum-ultraviolet laser to the nuclear transition in thorium-229 doped calcium fluoride crystals, and it generates that light by four-wave mixing in cadmium vapour (Huang et al., 2026).

## What is new

Earlier experiments located the nuclear transition and measured its properties, but a clock needs a stable reference that can be compared with another clock over time. Both papers close that gap. The two groups approach it from different directions. The Vienna paper centers on a feedback loop that locks the laser to the nuclear transition, while the Beijing paper centers on a vacuum-ultraviolet light source built by four-wave mixing in cadmium vapour.

## What is still open

A working clock is not yet a better clock. The reported stability is still short of the best optical atomic clocks, and the Vienna authors project that improvements of several orders of magnitude are still possible. The methods are also sensitive to the crystal, the laser and the readout, so reproducing them in other labs will be the real test.

The promise is large. A clock whose ticks come from a nucleus would be less sensitive to many environmental effects that limit electron-based clocks, which makes it a candidate for tests of fundamental physics, including searches for changes in fundamental constants and for dark matter. Those experiments require long, stable data, and this is where the next work will matter.

## Why it matters

The result turns a long-standing proposal into a working device. Timekeeping has always advanced by moving the reference to a finer process, from the rotation of the Earth to the swing of a pendulum, then to the electron, and now potentially to the nucleus. For readers, the useful point is that two groups reported the same milestone in the same issue of the journal, which makes the result more credible than a single report would be.
